Ashish lived as though the universe had been constructed specifically for his amusement. He walked through the massive, red brick corridors of the Indian Institute of Technology at Kharagpur with a careless grace, wearing tailored shirts that cost more than most students paid for a semester of lodging. Pankaj Sen, on the other hand, treated the world as a strict landlord he could barely afford to pay. He kept his head down, carefully calculating the price of his textbooks, and wished only to pass through his education entirely unnoticed. They were two boys occupying opposite ends of the human experience. There was no mathematical formula that should have brought them together.
Yet the sprawling, sunbaked campus possessed a strange gravity of its own. It was a suffocating ecosystem of ancient banyan trees and brutalist concrete blocks, filled with thousands of young men trying to secure their futures in the heavy heat of West Bengal. It was a place where loneliness could feel absolute. Ashish navigated the chaos with a loud, golden confidence, drawing people toward him effortlessly. Pankaj sat in the back rows of the lecture halls, watching the noise with dark, calculating eyes, preferring the safety of his own silence.
Their collision occurred over something entirely ordinary. It happened in the sweltering mess hall of their hostel, a cavernous room thick with the smell of boiling lentils, damp plaster, and the sweat of a hundred exhausted students.
Ashish sat staring at his metal tray with an expression of profound, personal betrayal.
"This cannot legally be classified as chicken," Ashish declared.
Pankaj, seated directly across the sticky wooden table, methodically chewed a piece of dry bread. He looked down at the grey, unidentifiable lump floating in a pool of oily gravy on Ashish's plate.
"You are making a rather large assumption that it was ever an animal."
Ashish stopped completely. He stared at the quiet boy opposite him; his brow furrowed in genuine surprise. Pankaj did not look back up. He simply continued to eat with the stoic resignation of a person who expected very little from his surroundings. For several long seconds, the heavy humidity of the dining hall pressed down on them in absolute silence. Then Ashish threw his head back and let out a laugh so sudden and booming that students at three neighbouring tables turned around in their chairs to look.
That single, mundane interaction marked the quiet end of Pankaj's isolated existence. Ashish simply decided that they were friends, and Pankaj found himself lacking the energy to argue against such a relentless force of nature. They fell into a rhythm that required no formal acknowledgement. If Ashish was wandering toward the campus gates for midnight tea, Pankaj was walking beside him, offering dry commentary on Ashish's terrible ideas. If Pankaj retreated to the library to salvage his grades, Ashish would eventually appear at his desk with two steaming cups of tea, looking deeply offended by Pankaj's dedication to hard work. They wasted entire afternoons sitting under the heavy canopies of the campus trees, debating the merits of films they had barely understood and arguing over the tactical formations of football clubs they would never play for.
The vast disparities in their backgrounds should have naturally driven them apart. Ashish was the son of Arindam Roy, a colossus in the political landscape of the state. The Roy family's wealth was generational, their influence stretching across bureaucrats, industrialists, and the highest echelons of power in Kolkata. Pankaj was the only son of a widowed mother who fought a quiet, daily war against poverty. His childhood was defined by the careful mathematics of survival, measuring the cost of electricity and quietly ignoring the things they simply could not afford.
Ashish had never once checked the price of a meal or a shirt, yet he never treated Pankaj with the patronising grace of a wealthy benefactor. They navigated the financial gap with brutal honesty. Pankaj mercilessly mocked Ashish for his extravagant wastefulness, while Ashish endured the insults with an amused, unbothered grin.
The only real friction occurred when Ashish attempted to pull Pankaj into the orbit of his father's political world. It usually began with a casual invitation to a gathering at the Roy estate in the city. Pankaj despised those events, finding the sprawling, marble-floored rooms suffocatingly tense. He hated watching Ashish navigate the crowds of men in crisp white linen, men who smiled with calculating eyes and spoke in coded pleasantries while secretly plotting the ruin of their rivals.
Late one night, returning from a particularly lavish dinner, they sat in the cavernous back seat of a chauffeur-driven car. The streetlights of Kolkata flashed rhythmically across the dark leather interior.
"Do you actually enjoy any of that?" Pankaj asked, his voice thick with exhaustion.
Ashish looked over, pulling his attention away from the passing city.
"Enjoy what?"
"All of it," Pankaj said, gesturing vaguely toward the fading lights of the estate. "The endless smiling. The pretending. Everyone in that room talking as though they are performing some noble public service while secretly calculating exactly how much blood they can drain from one another."
Ashish leaned his head back against the seat and closed his eyes.
"You think entirely too much, Pankaj."
"That is your answer to what we just witnessed?"
"My answer is that you look at the world and think too much about how it should work, rather than how it actually operates."
Pankaj turned away, staring out at the dark pavement, letting a heavy silence fill the space between them. Eventually, Ashish spoke again, his voice stripped of its usual golden arrogance, reduced to a quiet, vulnerable whisper.
"It is complicated."
Pankaj slowly turned his head back.
"Everything in this world is labelled complicated the moment rich people are asked to explain their crimes."
Ashish let out a sudden, sharp laugh. The tension dropped from his shoulders as he looked at his friend with a rare, unguarded affection.
"You see? This is exactly why I force you to come along to these things."
"Why?"
"Because every single person in that house lies to my face," Ashish said softly. "You are the only one who does not."
The years at Kharagpur moved with a cruel and unyielding velocity, transforming uncertain teenagers into young men facing the terrifying expanse of adulthood. Placements approached, carrying the weight of familial expectations and the sudden reality of the future. Ashish knew his path was already paved in the political machinery of West Bengal, an inheritance he accepted with a fatalistic shrug. Pankaj merely wanted a steady job, hoping only to earn enough to ease the quiet suffering of his mother and to perhaps buy a refrigerator that did not rattle like a dying engine.
The evening before their final departure, they walked across the sprawling campus long after the rest of their cohort had surrendered to sleep. The air was warm and still, carrying the scent of damp earth and the faint, distant hum of a radio. The massive concrete structures that had once terrified them now felt intimately familiar. They walked for a long time without speaking, the rhythmic crunch of their shoes on the gravel serving as the only sound, neither willing to vocalise the permanent nature of the approaching dawn.
Eventually, Pankaj stopped near the edge of a quiet pathway and stared out into the dark treeline.
"This is it, then," Pankaj murmured.
Ashish stopped a few paces ahead, turning back with a look of feigned ignorance.
"What is it?"
"This place. It is over."
Ashish let out a dismissive scoff, kicking a loose stone into the darkness.
"Do not be so terribly dramatic, Pankaj. We will come back. We will visit."
"You say that tonight, under these trees."
"We will."
"No," Pankaj said softly. "You will become some great, untouchable political leader, surrounded by men in white linen, and you will eventually forget the rest of us."
Ashish froze, the casual amusement draining completely from his face. He stood absolutely still for a moment before closing the distance between them, reaching deep into the pocket of his trousers.
"Give me your hand."
Pankaj frowned, his natural suspicion instantly flaring.
"Why?"
"Just give me your hand, Pankaj."
"This sounds incredibly suspicious."
"Everything in the universe sounds suspicious to you."
With a heavy sigh of reluctance, Pankaj extended his arm. Ashish grasped his friend's wrist, his grip surprisingly tight, and placed something heavy and cold against Pankaj's skin. The metal snapped into place with a sharp, mechanical click.
For several seconds, Pankaj simply stared down at his own arm. It was a Rolex, a vintage 1908 model, its smooth face catching the faint moonlight. It radiated a quiet, devastating elegance that looked entirely absurd against the frayed cuffs of Pankaj's faded shirt.
Pankaj slowly raised his head.
"Ashish."
"Do not start."
"What is this?"
"It is a watch, Pankaj. I know engineering students struggle with basic observation, but try to keep up."
"I can see that it is a watch. Why is it on my wrist?"
Ashish held his gaze, his jaw tightening slightly.
"It is a farewell gift."
"For graduating?"
"For surviving my company without killing me."
Pankaj looked down at the immaculate dial again. The craftsmanship was unmistakable, the smooth sweep of the second hand representing a sum of money that his mother would have taken years to earn.
"I cannot take this, Ashish. You have lost your mind."
"Yes, you can."
"No. I will not."
Ashish stepped forward, forcibly folding Pankaj's fingers over the cold face of the watch, trapping the gift against his friend's palm. The golden arrogance was entirely gone from his face, replaced by a raw, desperate sincerity that Pankaj had never seen before.
"Listen to me," Ashish commanded, his voice dropping to a fierce, trembling whisper. "No matter where we end up. No matter what happens. Do not become one of those people who disappear."
Pankaj let out a faint, hollow laugh, the tragedy of their divergent paths suddenly crystallising in the humid air.
"You are saying that to me?"
"Yes."
"You are the son of the kingmaker, Ashish. You are quite literally preparing to disappear into an endless convoy of black cars and locked rooms."
Ashish smiled, but the expression did not reach his eyes, which remained wide and terribly earnest.
"Then make certain that you find me."
Pankaj looked at him for a long, silent eternity, the ticking of the mechanism against his pulse suddenly the loudest sound in the world.
"I will."
It was a simple, quiet promise, spoken into the heavy darkness of the West Bengal night. That evening, Pankaj returned to his cramped hostel room and placed the heavy silver timepiece carefully beside his bed, watching the hands sweep silently toward morning. When the sun rose and they walked out of the gates of IIT Kharagpur, stepping onto the separate paths that would define the rest of their lives, the Rolex remained tightly fastened to Pankaj's wrist.
The first few years after they left IIT Kharagpur did not feel like a separation. The physical distance between them increased, but the tether held firmly.
Ashish stepped into the political machinery of West Bengal exactly as everyone had always anticipated. The transition was seamless. His father’s name opened doors long before Ashish ever needed to knock, and the vast, intricate network surrounding the Roy family had been preparing for his arrival since he was a child. He began attending closed-door meetings, touring rural constituencies, and standing silently beside his father on massive wooden stages. Slowly, he learned the specific, calculated language of men who measured the world exclusively in vote banks, alliances, and influence. There was a sudden gravity to Ashish in those early days that Pankaj had never seen at college—a tightening of his jaw and a carefulness in his eyes—but beneath that new armour, enough of the old, arrogant Ashish remained to make their friendship feel entirely untouched.
They spoke constantly. Ashish would call from the back seat of an SUV moving rapidly between two political rallies, from the sterile quiet of an airport lounge, or late at night when the sprawling Roy estate was finally asleep. Pankaj would complain bitterly about his entry-level software job, detailing the absurdity of corporate managers and the sheer exhaustion of staring at broken code for ten hours a day. Ashish would listen, laughing loudly through the phone, pretending that his own life of navigating cutthroat political betrayals was not considerably more exhausting.
“Come to Kolkata this weekend,” Ashish demanded suddenly during one of those late-night calls.
Pankaj held the phone between his shoulder and his ear, trying to open a stale packet of biscuits in his small, rented kitchen.
“I thought you were in Delhi for the committee meetings.”
“I am coming back.”
“When?”
“Friday night.”
“What time Friday night, Ashish?”
There was a brief pause on the line.
“Why are you asking so many logistical questions? Just say yes.”
“Because I need to know whether you are actually going to be in the city, or if you are going to disappear into another rally.”
Ashish laughed, the sound carrying a genuine warmth.
“Saturday evening. I will personally send a car to pick you up.”
“You promised that same thing last month.”
“I was incredibly busy last month.”
“You were at a luxury hotel party. You literally sent me a photograph of yourself holding a glass of champagne next to an ice sculpture.”
“That was a vital political networking event.”
“With three fashion models?”
Ashish laughed so loudly that Pankaj had to pull the receiver away from his ear.
“Fine,” Ashish conceded, still chuckling. “Saturday. I promise you.”
He did return on Saturday. He arrived nearly three hours late, stepping out of a black SUV looking exhausted, but Pankaj was still waiting on the pavement. They spent the entire evening eating at a chaotic roadside stall, arguing fiercely about European football exactly as they had done in the mess hall at Kharagpur. Ashish paid the bill, waving away Pankaj's protests with familiar arrogance, and for a few hours, the creeping weight of adulthood seemed to entirely vanish.
Those evenings became fiercely precious to Pankaj, long before he realised how rare they were about to become.
Then, Arindam Roy died.
The death occurred suddenly, sending a massive shockwave through the political ecosystem of the state. For three chaotic weeks, every newspaper in West Bengal was plastered with photographs of the enormous state funeral. Columnists speculated wildly about the succession of power, and television anchors dissected the legacy of a man whose reach had extended far beyond the official offices he held. Thousands of people flooded the streets to pay their respects. Loyal party workers maintained a vigil outside the Roy residence through the night. Convoys of state ministers arrived with blaring sirens, and ancient political rivals who had not spoken to the family in a decade appeared at the heavy iron gates wearing solemn, calculating expressions.
Pankaj took a train to Kolkata, walked to the estate, and remained there through the suffocating heat of the day. He finally found Ashish sitting alone in an upstairs study, surrounded by the heavy mahogany furniture his father had favoured.
For the first time since they had met, Ashish looked utterly lost.
Pankaj walked in and sat heavily in the leather chair beside him. Neither of them spoke for a long time. The muffled, endless noise of the thousands of people gathered outside seeped through the thick glass of the windows.
Eventually, Ashish leaned forward, resting his face in his hands.
“I do not know what I am supposed to do, Pankaj.”
“You do not have to do anything today.”
“Every single person in this house expects me to.”
“Then let them expect it. They can wait.”
Ashish looked up, his eyes red and exhausted. Pankaj reached out and placed a firm hand on his shoulder. For that one brief, suspended moment, the heir to the political empire completely vanished, and Pankaj saw only the terrified eighteen-year-old boy he had met in Kharagpur.
Ashish leaned into the touch.
“My father is gone.”
“I know.”
“I do not think I ever truly understood how much he was holding together until this morning.”
“You do not have to understand it right now.”
Ashish closed his eyes, his breathing slowing. Pankaj stayed in the room for the rest of the day, ignoring the politicians who periodically knocked on the door. He stayed because there was absolutely nowhere else in the world he wanted to be.
In the chaotic months that followed the funeral, Ashish inherited far more than a famous surname. He inherited a sprawling, ruthless organisation. He inherited a vast network of fiercely loyal fixers, complicated business interests, heavy financial obligations, and a legion of inherited enemies. The men who had once whispered in his father's ear now whispered in his. The political machinery of West Bengal did not pause simply because its architect had died; it relentlessly found the next available centre of gravity and began to orbit around Ashish.
He was young, deeply ambitious, and intelligent enough to adapt rapidly. He threw himself into the brutal campaign to retain his father's parliamentary seat.
The election entirely consumed him. Pankaj watched the transformation from a quiet distance. He saw photographs of Ashish in the morning papers, standing before enormous, roaring crowds, his white sleeves rolled up to the elbows, his face radiating the terrifying confidence of a man who had finally understood the sheer physical weight of power. There were massive hoardings bearing his face at major intersections. His name became a constant presence on evening television debates. People in Pankaj's office, people who had never spoken a word to Pankaj, suddenly spoke about Ashish Roy with familiar reverence.
Ashish won the election by a crushing margin.
The night the results were officially declared, Ashish called.
Pankaj was sitting alone in his small, rented room, eating a lukewarm dinner directly from a steel container.
“I told you.” Ashish's voice crackled through the phone, backed by the deafening roar of a celebration.
“You won.”
“Of course I won.”
“Congratulations, Ashish.”
“You sound incredibly disappointed.”
“I sound incredibly hungry.”
Ashish laughed, the sound carrying the old, familiar arrogance.
“Come here.”
“Where? To Kolkata?”
“Yes. Right now.”
“Ashish, it is almost midnight.”
“Exactly. The worst of the crowd is gone. Nobody important will be here.”
Pankaj hesitated, looking at his empty room, before a small smile broke across his face.
“Alright. I will come.”
Ashish sent a car for him, and it was the moment Pankaj slid into the leather seats that he truly realised his friend’s life had mutated into something entirely irreversible. The car was a massive, heavily armoured SUV. The driver spoke into an earpiece, coordinating Ashish's exact movements. The security parameters around the Roy estate were suffocating. When Pankaj finally reached the house, the corridors were still packed with people. A minor politician enthusiastically shook Pankaj's hand, congratulating him without having any idea who he was. Another man in a sharp suit aggressively asked if Pankaj was a journalist. Pankaj just laughed and shook his head.
Ashish finally found him standing near the grand mahogany staircase, looking utterly out of place.
“There you are.”
“You have become remarkably difficult to reach, Ashish.”
“I am a Member of Parliament now.”
“Congratulations. Your entire personality has already deteriorated.”
“Shut up.”
Ashish pulled him into a crushing hug. He still hugged Pankaj the same way he had at IIT—a sudden, fierce grip that ignored the expensive tailoring of his suit.
That was the strangest, most painful part of the transition. Nothing about Ashish's affection for Pankaj ever felt false. Whenever they managed to occupy the same physical space, the old, effortless warmth returned instantly. Ashish still remembered the ridiculous stories Pankaj had told him years ago. He still laughed at the same dry jokes. He still asked about the deteriorating health of Pankaj's mother with genuine concern, remembering minor details about her medication that Pankaj himself occasionally forgot.
But there were simply too many people crowded around Ashish now.
There were cabinet ministers, wealthy industrialists, political strategists, famous actors, and ruthless fixers whose opinions dictated the future of the state. Ashish's phone never stopped ringing. His mornings began with strategy briefings before breakfast, and his nights ended in smoke-filled rooms negotiating alliances. His calendar was fiercely protected, booked solid weeks in advance.
Gradually, silently, Pankaj was downgraded from a fixture in Ashish's life to an appointment.
At first, neither of them seemed to notice the shift. Pankaj settled into the monotonous, grinding rhythm of corporate software development. His office was a sprawling, sterile floor in a glass tower, where time was measured in project deadlines, pointless meetings, and the harsh blue glare of dual monitors. He wrote backend code, fixed other people's mistakes, ate lunch in the cafeteria when a colleague occasionally remembered to invite him, and returned to his empty apartment too exhausted to speak.
His salary was respectable, yet it vanished with terrifying speed. Rent consumed a massive portion. His mother's escalating medical bills took another. Transport, electricity, and the quiet, endless expenses of adulthood drained whatever remained. Pankaj learned the quiet art of postponement. A new shirt could wait. A dinner at a restaurant could wait. Everything could wait.
He rarely complained. There was no grand, cinematic tragedy in his daily existence, which made his growing misery almost impossible to explain to anyone else.
He was simply, deeply, profoundly lonely.
The loneliness did not arrive as a sudden blow; it accumulated in microscopic pieces. A group of colleagues would organise a Friday dinner and simply forget to include him on the email chain. Someone would casually mention a weekend trip they had all taken, and Pankaj would realise they had planned it while he was sitting three desks away. Conversations would flow easily in the breakroom, but the moment Pankaj approached the coffee machine, the topic would shift, or the group would suddenly disperse. None of it was actively malicious. None of it justified a confrontation or an outburst of anger. There was always a perfectly reasonable explanation.
They assumed he was too busy.
They thought he would not enjoy the bar.
They had simply forgotten.
He began to experience the same invisible friction with women. He would meet someone through a mutual acquaintance, exchange a few pleasant messages for a week, and then watch as the replies grew shorter and eventually stopped entirely. He asked a woman from a different department for coffee and received a painfully polite, immediate refusal. Another woman laughed when he suggested dinner, assuming he was making a joke, and then apologised with such frantic embarrassment that Pankaj felt sick to his stomach.
None of it broke him. That is not how chronic loneliness functions. It is not a single, bleeding wound. It is the slow, suffocating accumulation of tiny absences: the empty chair beside him at a cafe, the notification light on his phone that never blinked, the sudden silence when he entered a room. Years later, sitting in the dark, he would look back at those individual moments and find them almost ridiculous. None of them, in isolation, were powerful enough to deform a man's soul.
But together, over thousands of days, they entirely rewrote the architecture of his reality.
Ashish remained his only exception.
Pankaj would still call him when he finally fixed a massive bug at work. He called when his mother’s tremors worsened. He sent Ashish grainy photographs of their old hostel, links to obscure articles he thought Ashish would find amusing, and inside jokes that had once made them laugh until they couldn't breathe.
Ashish usually answered. Sometimes he called back hours later. Sometimes, he called back days later.
“I am so sorry, yaar. I was on the floor of the Parliament.”
“It is fine, Ashish.”
“I will call you properly tonight.”
“Okay.”
The call sometimes came. More often, it did not. Pankaj slowly learned to stop asking why.
Once, Pankaj carefully planned an entire weekend trip to Kolkata around a narrow window of time Ashish had sworn he would be free. Ashish cancelled the meeting via text message late on Friday night.
Something came up.
What came up? Pankaj typed back, sitting on the edge of his bed with his packed bag beside him.
Party leadership meeting. Emergency.
Pankaj stared at the screen, the blue light reflecting in his eyes, and did not reply.
A minute later, his phone rang.
“Do not be angry, Pankaj.”
“I am not angry.”
“I swear I will make this up to you.”
“When?”
“Next week. Without fail.”
Pankaj smiled into the dark room, a bitter, exhausted expression.
“Okay.”
The next week seamlessly became the week after. The week after vanished into the following month. And the following month bled into another year.
Ashish did not intend to hurt him. That was the most devastating truth of all. There was no grand betrayal for Pankaj to hate, no deliberate cruelty he could point to, no defining argument where Ashish had consciously chosen to sever the bond. Ashish was simply moving at a terrifying velocity through a world that had become impossibly large, while Pankaj remained entirely static in a world that was shrinking every day.
Ashish’s reality expanded violently. He acquired a sprawling new property in the hills. He travelled constantly to Europe and Dubai. He was photographed at exclusive business conclaves, highly publicised charity galas, and private dinners where the press was forced to wait outside the gates. His clothing became immaculate. His cars grew larger and darker. He was seen standing shoulder-to-shoulder with men Pankaj only recognised from the covers of magazines.
Pankaj watched the ascent from the silent distance of a forgotten friend.
Sometimes, he was genuinely, fiercely proud. He would catch Ashish delivering a fiery speech on a news channel, his voice echoing over a massive crowd, and Pankaj would smile at the screen in his dark apartment.
Look at him.
That is my friend.
That is the boy who gave me this watch because he was terrified we would lose touch.
Pankaj still wore the heavy 1908 Rolex every single day. The cold silver had become a permanent part of his physical body.
Yet, there were heavy, suffocating evenings when Pankaj returned from the glass tower, loosened his cheap tie, and sat entirely alone in his apartment without turning on a single light. The city outside his window continued its relentless, ignorant movement. Headlights swept across the ceiling. In the building opposite his, windows glowed yellow—a family eating dinner, a couple arguing violently, a group of friends laughing over drinks.
Pankaj would pick up his phone. He would pull up Ashish's contact name. He would stare at the glowing letters for a long time.
And then he would gently place the phone face down on the table.
He was beginning to understand that a friendship could survive for a long time on the memory of affection alone, much like a starving man surviving on the memory of a feast. The terrifying question was simply how long the body could last before it began to consume itself.
There were still rare, beautiful moments when Ashish proved he hadn't entirely forgotten. On Pankaj's birthday, an enormous, expensive arrangement of flowers arrived at the software company with a handwritten card.
Happy birthday, idiot. I still owe you that dinner.
Pankaj kept the small white card in his wallet for years.
Another time, Ashish called him unexpectedly at two in the morning, standing on a balcony somewhere in Europe.
“I saw something today that instantly reminded me of you,” Ashish said, his voice slightly slurred with expensive wine.
“What was it?”
“An absolutely terrible, overpriced restaurant.”
Pankaj let out a sudden, genuine laugh, the sound breaking the silence of his apartment.
“You have become a truly terrible human being, Ashish.”
“I was always terrible, Pankaj. You just refused to accept it.”
“That is remarkably accurate.”
They stayed on the phone for nearly an hour that night, the old rhythm returning as though it had never left. When the call finally disconnected, Pankaj sat quietly in the dark for a long time, the heavy silver watch ticking steadily against his wrist, a soft smile resting on his face.
There had been a time when Pankaj believed that loneliness was simply another name for solitude. It was a temporary state one endured for a few hours after a difficult day at the office. It was a quietness that arrived on rainy Sundays when the city seemed to have momentarily forgotten his existence. It was a feeling that could be easily dismissed with a book, a cup of overboiled tea, or the simple act of standing by the window watching the traffic. In those earlier days, even when his life felt disappointingly small and rigidly ordinary, there had always been one specific door to return to at the end of the evening. Behind that door, moving quietly through the small rooms of their apartment, there had always been his mother.
The apartment in which they lived was neither particularly large nor desperately poor. It sat within one of those decaying neighbourhoods of Kolkata where the buildings seemed to grow exhausted alongside the people who inhabited them. The paint had begun to peel from the exterior walls a decade ago, revealing the grey concrete beneath. The iron railings of the narrow balconies carried thick coats of rust, and the poorly ventilated stairwell smelled permanently of damp earth and stale cooking oil. Yet, Pankaj had grown up within those walls, and his mother possessed the quiet, profound ability to make a space feel deeply inhabited. There was always a comforting rhythm to the house. It was the clatter of a steel spoon against a cup, the sound of running water in the kitchen, the rustle of a newspaper, or the low murmur of the evening television. Even when she was too tired to speak, her sheer physical presence anchored the rooms, filling the empty air with the undeniable weight of a home.
Whenever Pankaj returned from the glass tower of his office, exhausted and deeply irritated by another day spent staring at glowing monitors, there had always been someone waiting to ask if he had eaten. It was such a minuscule, repetitive question that he rarely answered it properly. Sometimes he would say yes when he was starving. Sometimes he would complain that she asked him the same exact thing every single night. He did not understand, during those thoughtless years, exactly how much of his humanity was held together by that one ordinary question.
The illness invaded their lives with a terrifying quietness. At first, there was only a faint, almost imperceptible trembling in her hands. It was a slight tremor that she herself laughed away and blamed on the changing weather. Then the stiffness arrived, settling deep into her joints, followed by a gradual, agonising slowing of her movements. She began to exhibit a peculiar, frightening hesitation before performing actions that had once required no thought at all. A cup of water would remain untouched on the table because the physical mathematics of lifting it had suddenly become impossible. A button on her blouse would take several humiliating minutes to fasten. Walking from the bedroom to the small kitchen became a monumental effort, as though the short hallway had somehow expanded into an impassable distance.
Eventually, the tired doctors at the clinic gave the creeping paralysis a name, diagnosing her with Parkinson's disease.
Pankaj learned more about the brutal reality of government hospitals during those ensuing months than he had ever wished to know. He learned the specific, suffocating smell of crowded corridors where cheap disinfectant never quite masked the metallic tang of old blood and unwashed bodies. He learned the sound of rubber slippers dragging endlessly across chipped marble floors. He memorised the exhausted, hollow faces of relatives waiting desperately beside locked doors, and he recognised the blank expressions of overworked doctors who were already thinking about their next surgical rotation before they had finished speaking to him. Pankaj learned the long, humiliating waits beneath flickering fluorescent tube lights, slowly realising that a human being could become entirely invisible when surrounded by too much suffering and too few hands to alleviate it.
There were chaotic days when his mother received proper attention, and there were miserable days when she was left completely unattended on a rusted trolley. Some nurses spoke softly to her, treating her frailty with gentle hands, and others treated her failing body as a profound inconvenience to their shift. Once, after his mother had been left waiting in a draughty hallway for five hours despite her inability to stand, Pankaj completely lost his temper with a passing ward attendant. The man stopped, looked at Pankaj's furious face, and told him without a trace of cruelty or sympathy that there were three hundred patients on the floor, and Pankaj was not the only man in the building who had a dying mother.
That sentence lodged itself deep into Pankaj's mind. It haunted him precisely because it was the absolute, undeniable truth.
He was forced to continue working through the entirety of her decline because poverty offered no alternative. Every morning, he joined the crushing crowds on the local trains, travelled to the sterile software office, and spent ten hours pretending that his reality had not fractured. He wrote backend code, corrected minor logical errors in corporate systems, and sat in an open-plan room surrounded by colleagues who loudly discussed weekend holidays, new restaurants, and office promotions. His phone remained permanently face up beside his keyboard. He developed a sickening habit of staring at the dark screen, his heart rate spiking violently every time the display illuminated, terrified before he could even read whether the caller was his mother or the hospital ward.
His colleagues were not intentionally cruel. Cruelty would have been easier to process, giving him a tangible target for his rising anger. They simply possessed their own vibrant lives, with their own demanding families, complex friendships, and weekend plans. A manager might occasionally stop by his desk to ask if everything was manageable, or a coworker might send a polite evening message inquiring about his mother's health. But the very next morning, the harsh office lights would snap on, the dual monitors would awaken, and the relentless machinery of corporate life would resume without missing a single beat. The world possessed a horrifying, indifferent ability to continue moving smoothly around a man's private catastrophe.
Ashish called whenever he remembered, and in the beginning, he remembered quite often. Pankaj would sometimes see his old friend's name flash across the screen while sitting in the suffocating heat of the hospital ward. For a few precious minutes, hearing Ashish's loud, confident voice, the old familiarity would return so completely that Pankaj could almost trick himself into believing they were still carefree students at Kharagpur. Ashish would ask about his mother, his voice laced with genuine concern, and fiercely demand that Pankaj ask for anything he needed. Pankaj believed the sincerity of the offer because there was absolutely no reason to doubt it. Whatever vast social distance had formed between them, Ashish's foundational love for Pankaj had not become false.
That was the exact detail that made the widening distance so agonising to endure. Ashish had not deliberately abandoned him. He had not woken up in his luxurious estate one morning and consciously decided that Pankaj no longer mattered. Ashish had simply acquired an empire that demanded his complete, undivided attention, and the political underworld of West Bengal possessed a ravenous appetite. There were high-stakes strategy meetings that could not be postponed, thousands of party workers awaiting instruction, aggressive journalists demanding quotes, and wealthy industrialists who required Ashish's presence at their dinner tables. Pankaj knew there were always crowds of important people surrounding Ashish. That was the most devastating difference between them. Ashish could never be entirely alone even when he desperately wanted to be, whereas Pankaj could not find a single moment of human connection even when he was starving for it.
His mother's condition worsened gradually, and then, with terrifying suddenness, it accelerated. Each passing week seemed to violently strip away another small certainty from her life. Her movements became rigidly restricted, her voice thinned into a weak whisper, and the fundamental acts of eating and dressing began to demand a monumental, exhausting effort.
One evening, after returning from a particularly brutal day at the office, Pankaj found her sitting quietly beside the bedroom window. A heavy monsoon rain had fallen earlier in the afternoon, and the glass was streaked with thin trails of water that blurred the yellow streetlamps below. His mother sat perfectly still, her trembling hands resting heavily in her lap, watching the wet, slick road as though she were waiting for something specific to appear from the darkness.
Pankaj placed his cheap leather bag on the floor and stood silently behind her chair.
"You are very late today," she said, her voice weak but clear, without turning her head.
"The traffic was terrible on the bypass."
"You always blame the traffic."
"I blame it because there is always traffic."
She smiled, a faint, ghostly expression that briefly smoothed the deep lines of pain around her mouth. For a few fleeting seconds, she looked exactly like the healthy, capable woman who had raised him. Pankaj found himself staring at her in absolute silence, terrified that if he moved or spoke, the illusion would shatter and the sickness would return to her face.
Then, her eyes slowly drifted down to his wrist resting on the back of her chair.
"You still wear that heavy watch."
Pankaj glanced down at the silver Rolex. Ashish had clamped it onto his arm the night before they left the institute, and over the intervening years, it had become completely indistinguishable from his own skin. The metal casing had acquired a network of microscopic scratches from constant use, but the dark face of the dial remained immaculate, catching the weak yellow light of the bedroom.
"Ashish gave it to me," Pankaj said softly.
"I know he did."
"You remember that?"
She finally turned her head, looking up at him with dark, tired eyes.
"I remember everything important to you."
Something about the weight of her answer made Pankaj deeply uncomfortable, a sudden tightening in his chest that he could not name. He looked away, staring out through the wet glass of the window. Down on the street, a lone motorcycle cut through the standing water, its tyres hissing loudly against the pavement. For a long time, the only sound inside the bedroom was the rhythmic dripping of rainwater falling from the concrete balcony above them.
His mother continued to watch his profile.
"Do you still speak to him?"
"Sometimes. When he has the time."
"You should speak to him more often."
"He is an incredibly busy man now."
"Everyone in the world is busy."
Pankaj smiled, a bitter, hollow expression that did not reach his eyes.
"That does not mean everyone has time."
She did not say anything else after that. Perhaps she understood the depth of his isolation far better than he realised.
The final end arrived without any of the quiet dignity Pankaj had desperately imagined death might possess. There was no profound final conversation, no carefully spoken farewell that provided closure, and no sudden revelation that made the years of suffering feel meaningful. There was only another chaotic hospital admission, another freezing corridor, and another sterile room where medical machines performed their indifferent, mechanical work while Pankaj stood helplessly nearby.
He would forever remember the biting cold of the air conditioning in the intensive care unit and the hard plastic of the chair he occupied for fourteen hours. He would remember the attending doctor speaking to him in a voice so heavily practised that it seemed entirely devoid of human empathy, delivering the final news without allowing a single ounce of the tragedy to touch his own conscience. But most vividly, Pankaj would remember standing beside the narrow hospital bed, picking up his mother's hand, and being violently struck by how terribly light it had become. The hand that had carried him, fed him, and anchored him to the world now felt entirely hollow, resembling dry paper resting against his palm.
At some unrecorded hour in the early morning, the machines flatlined, and she was gone.
The world outside the hospital did not stop. That was the very first thing Pankaj truly, actively hated about the nature of death. People had always told him that when someone vital died, the entire world fundamentally changed. The truth was infinitely more cruel. Absolutely nothing changed. The sun rose, yellow taxis continued honking aggressively through the intersections, street vendors shouted their prices from the crowded pavements, and the local trains arrived and departed perfectly on schedule. Somewhere in the city, people were laughing loudly over morning coffee, completely ignorant of the fact that Pankaj's entire universe had just collapsed into a singular, suffocating point of grief.
He performed the exhausting rituals expected of a grieving son. Distant relatives materialised from towns he had not visited in decades, filling the apartment with meaningless noise. Neighbours crowded into the living room offering generic condolences. People who had not spoken to his mother in fifteen years sat on his furniture, drank his tea, and told him with absolute certainty that she had moved on to a far better place. Pankaj sat rigidly in the corner, nodding in silence, because there was nothing else left for him to do.
For three days, the apartment was packed with bodies. Then, the crowd thinned. Then, there were only two aunts left. Finally, they packed their bags and returned to their own lives, leaving the apartment to its natural, terrifying silence.
The following Monday evening, Pankaj returned from his first day back at the software office. He slid the metal key into the lock, pushed the heavy wooden door open, and stepped inside.
He stood in the narrow entryway for a very long time without removing his shoes. The silence in the apartment was structurally wrong. It was not merely the absence of noise, for a house could be perfectly quiet and still feel alive. This specific silence was dense, heavy, and aggressive, occupying the physical space in the rooms. The small kitchen was completely dark. The television screen was black. There was no clatter of steel plates. There was no voice calling out from the bedroom to ask him why he was late. There was absolutely no one to ask if he had eaten.
His mother was gone, and there was no one else on earth waiting for him.
Pankaj dropped his cheap leather bag onto the dusty floor and remained frozen in the centre of the living room. He had a stable corporate job, a stack of paid bills, a monthly salary, and a refrigerator containing fresh vegetables. He possessed a bed, a rack of clothes, a smartphone, and all the mundane, accumulated objects a man gathers when he believes he is building a functioning life.
But he possessed absolutely no one.
The crushing realisation did not strike him like a sudden physical blow. It settled over him slowly, thickly, like falling ash. There would never be another phone call asking when he was coming home. No one would ever notice if he decided not to return at all. The microscopic, ordinary interactions he had once considered entirely insignificant suddenly revealed themselves as the foundational architecture of human existence. Now, the apartment had lost the only person who gave that architecture any meaning, leaving Pankaj completely untethered, floating in a terrible, silent vacuum.
In the immediate aftermath, Pankaj attempted to force his life back into its previous shape. He forced himself onto the trains, sat at his desk in the glass tower, returned to the empty apartment, and cooked small meals when he could summon the physical energy. He spent his weekends lying on his bed in the dark, scrolling mindlessly through his phone, sleeping at highly irregular hours to avoid the silence of the house.
At the office, his colleagues spoke around him. They did not actively dislike him, as disliking someone required a certain amount of focused attention. Pankaj gradually came to understand that there was a secondary, far more insidious form of social rejection. It was the pain of being constantly tolerated without ever once being chosen. When large groups went out for drinks on a Friday evening, someone might discuss the location right in front of his desk without ever turning to explicitly invite him. He would sometimes attempt to insert himself into a lively conversation near the coffee machines, only to find that the group's attention had already shifted, leaving his sentence hanging awkwardly in the air.
The women he encountered rejected him with the same devastating, polite indifference. There were warm initial smiles, followed by brief, shallow conversations that inevitably faded into silence without any explanation. After gathering a massive amount of courage to ask a woman from the design department if she would like to get coffee after her shift, Pankaj sat at his desk in a state of physical nausea, waiting four hours for a reply. When her message finally arrived, it was perfectly courteous. She said she was incredibly busy with a project, but perhaps they could go another time.
There was never another time.
None of these small humiliations possessed the power to destroy him individually. That was precisely what made the accumulation so incredibly dangerous. A man could easily survive one rejection. He could survive two. He could endure a hundred minor disappointments if he possessed a safe harbour to retreat to at the end of the day. But Pankaj had nowhere to carry his wounds. No mother was sitting by the window, no best friend waiting at a roadside tea stall, no partner expecting his call.
The rejections began to layer over him, hardening like sediment. An unanswered text message. A forgotten lunch invitation. A weekend spent entirely without speaking a single word aloud to another human being. A conversation that died instantly because nobody in the room cared enough to ask him a follow-up question. Pankaj realised that a man's soul was not always destroyed by massive, cinematic catastrophes. Sometimes, it was slowly eroded by thousands of tiny, microscopic absences that nobody else in the world considered worthy of noticing.
And through this entire suffocating descent, Ashish continued to exist, operating in a dazzling universe entirely beyond Pankaj's reach.
At first, Pankaj only checked his old friend's public social media accounts occasionally. There was nothing inherently dangerous or strange about the impulse. People looked up the digital ghosts of their past all the time, especially the friends they had once loved fiercely and lost to the current of time.
One sweltering night, lying flat on his back in the dark, unair-conditioned bedroom, Pankaj unlocked his phone and typed his friend's name into a search bar. The harsh blue light of the screen illuminated Pankaj's exhausted face in the black room.
There were dozens of recent, high-resolution photographs. Ashish was standing at massive political rallies, flanked by heavily armed security personnel and senior cabinet ministers. Pankaj studied the images with a quiet, detached interest. He instantly recognised the sharp jawline and the confident posture, but there was something deeply unsettling about seeing the boy he used to argue with over hostel food presented to the world as a powerful, untouchable deity.
He is doing exceptionally well, Pankaj thought to himself in the quiet room.
And he was.
The next time Pankaj opened the application, the algorithm fed him photographs from a diplomatic trip abroad. There was a private, opulent dinner in a hotel ballroom overlooking a European skyline. Ashish was standing in the centre of the frame, holding a crystal glass, surrounded by a group of incredibly wealthy people Pankaj did not recognise. Ashish wore a bespoke suit that cost more than Pankaj earned in a year, radiating that same golden, effortless confidence he had possessed since their first year of college. He belonged there naturally, moving seamlessly among international businessmen, famous models, and people whose entire existences appeared to be insulated by unimaginable wealth.
Staring at the screen, Pankaj initially felt a surge of fierce, protective pride. His best friend had conquered the world. The boy who had dragged him through the red dust of Kharagpur was now standing beneath crystal chandeliers, shaping the future of the state.
But immediately following the pride, a secondary emotion arrived. It was smaller, colder, and infinitely darker.
Why had Ashish never once mentioned this trip to him?
Pankaj forcefully locked the screen and threw the phone onto the empty pillow beside him. Ashish was busy running a political empire. That was the only explanation required.
Yet, the very next evening, Pankaj picked up the phone and checked again.
What exactly is he doing tonight?
A few days later, sitting alone at his kitchen table eating cold rice, the questions began to mutate, growing sharper and more specific.
Who are the people standing next to him in this photograph?
And then, later that same week, scrolling endlessly through a gallery of a charity gala.
Where did they go after this event ended? Who was invited to the private room?
The questions began to multiply exponentially, spreading through Pankaj's mind like a quiet virus, entirely without him noticing the precise moment his harmless curiosity soured into obsession.
He was no longer simply checking on the well-being of his old friend. He began actively tracking the sprawling network of people who surrounded Ashish. A single photograph of a political dinner led Pankaj to click on a tagged profile, which revealed the name of a wealthy real estate developer. That developer's profile revealed a geolocation tag at an exclusive coastal resort. A face smiling in the blurred background of a selfie became a puzzle for Pankaj to solve. He cross-referenced names, matching a corrupt health bureaucrat to a hospital owner, linking a political fixer to an industrialist. He discovered that people who publicly claimed to be bitter rivals were frequently photographed attending the same private, unlisted parties on private estates.
The world Ashish inhabited was vast, intricate, and deeply corrupt. The deeper Pankaj looked into the glowing screen, the more the hidden architecture of that society revealed itself to him.
It rapidly became an unbreakable compulsion. After returning from the sterile software office to the absolute silence of his apartment, Pankaj would barely bother to turn on the overhead lights. He would sit at the small table, the heavy Rolex ticking against his wrist, and open the digital gates. Sometimes he promised himself he would only look for five minutes to see if Ashish had posted a speech. Three hours would pass in total silence before Pankaj finally noticed the numbness in his hands.
He refused to classify the feeling inside his chest as jealousy. The word was too small, too petty for the crushing weight of what he was experiencing.
He was still proud of Ashish. He wanted his friend to possess everything the world had to offer, and there were late nights when Pankaj stared at a photograph of Ashish laughing and felt an agonising, heartbreaking affection for the boy he had once known. But those moments of love were increasingly poisoned by the nights when Pankaj stared at the exact same photographs and wondered if Ashish even remembered that Pankaj was still alive.
The two emotions slammed into each other constantly, creating a contradiction that threatened to tear Pankaj's mind apart. He loved Ashish deeply, and he violently resented the corrupt, glittering world that had stolen Ashish away from him. He was thrilled that his friend possessed absolute power, and he was thoroughly, desperately miserable because he himself possessed absolutely nothing. He remembered the boy who had forced the expensive watch onto his wrist, begging him not to become a person who disappeared.
Now, Ashish was everywhere. His face was broadcast on the evening news, plastered across massive billboards, and documented in thousands of digital photographs. Pankaj could look at his friend whenever he desired.
And yet, staring at the screen in his dark, empty room, Pankaj had never felt further away from him.
Something fundamental had broken inside Pankaj during those long, silent evenings, though he lacked the vocabulary to identify the fracture. His own physical reality had shrunk to the size of a damp apartment and a cubicle, while the reality of Ashish stretched endlessly across the globe. The brighter and more luxurious Ashish's world appeared through the glass of the screen, the darker and more suffocating Pankaj's isolation became.
At first, he had only wanted to check if his old friend was happy.
Then he needed to know what Ashish was doing tonight.
Then he needed to know exactly who was with him.
Then he needed to know where they were hiding.
The questions grew, quiet and patient, expanding in the absolute silence of the apartment, until the sprawling, corrupt society surrounding Ashish began to occupy significantly more space in Pankaj's mind than his own existence.
At first, his interest in Ashish remained almost tender. There was a profound comfort in seeing the familiar face of an old friend at the end of an otherwise crushing, uneventful day. When the apartment became too quiet and the dark hours stretched without interruption, Pankaj would unlock his phone and immerse himself in the glowing photographs. It was not unlike turning the pages of an old album, except that this particular album was constantly expanding. There were new locations every few days, new tailored clothes, new extravagant occasions, and the curious, hollow sensation that his friend's life continued to unfold at a terrifying velocity while Pankaj remained entirely static. Ashish appeared to be everywhere at once. He was photographed stepping out of armoured black cars beneath the blinding white glare of camera flashes, sitting beneath heavy crystal chandeliers in hotel lobbies, leaning toward grave politicians, and laughing effortlessly with famous actors. The images were bright, polished fragments of a universe that possessed absolutely no room for silence.
Pankaj began to memorise the geography of places he had never visited. He learned the specific interior lighting of luxury hotels from repeated photographs, the mosaic tiles of exclusive restaurant terraces, and the carved wooden entrances to clubs where security guards stood rigidly beneath velvet ropes. He learned which foreign cities Ashish visited most frequently, which private parties attracted the exact same circles of influence, and which faces returned in the background of photographs taken months apart. It was a peculiar, parasitic kind of companionship. Ashish had absolutely no idea that Pankaj was watching, but Pankaj gradually began to feel that he was standing right there in the room. He watched short videos of elaborate dinners, political speeches, and loud celebrations, listening to Ashish laugh with wealthy strangers. Sometimes Pankaj would put the phone face down after several minutes and find himself smiling in the dark, as though he had actually been invited to the gathering. Then he would look around his cramped apartment, and the smile would instantly decay. The room would be exactly as static as he had left it. The unwashed steel cup sat beside the sink, the refrigerator hummed mechanically, and outside, the city continued its restless movement. Within the apartment, there was nothing except the ticking of the wall clock and the cold blue light of his screen.
The frequency of his observations began to escalate without any conscious decision. What had once been a quiet ritual reserved for the evening slowly infected his daylight hours. He checked the accounts during lunch, then during small breaks at the office, and eventually, before he had even poured his first cup of tea in the morning. The digital feed always offered something new. There was a sprawling dinner in Mumbai, a sudden flight to Dubai, a closed political committee in Kolkata, and a private birthday celebration for a billionaire whose name Pankaj had never heard spoken aloud. The crowds surrounding Ashish rotated constantly, but certain key faces appeared again and again. Pankaj began noticing them, studying their postures, and wondering exactly who they were. The question seemed entirely harmless in the beginning. He would search for a name attached to a blurred face, read a brief corporate biography, look at another tagged photograph, and move on. However, one answer invariably birthed another question. A real estate developer introduced him to a shell company. That company led him to another industrialist. A state minister appeared beside a man Pankaj had seen at a private, unlisted dinner. A famous model appeared in three different photographs taken months apart, always standing near the same political fixer. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the glittering world began to reveal itself not as a collection of random events, but as a meticulously constructed web.
Pankaj was exceptionally good at navigating webs. His profession had trained him to hunt for the hidden architecture beneath apparent simplicity. A piece of corporate software could appear perfectly straightforward to the end user while concealing massive, tangled layers of dependencies, restricted permissions, and hidden relationships that only became visible when a developer knew exactly where to pry. Human beings, he discovered, were built upon the same principles. Their public lives consisted of curated connections that remained entirely invisible until somebody began tracing the data points. A person appeared in one photograph because of a lucrative friendship, another because of a silent business arrangement, and another because of a political obligation forged in secret. The more Pankaj pulled at these invisible threads, the more he understood that the people surrounding Ashish were not merely a collection of wealthy acquaintances. They formed a closed, ruthless system, and systems could be mapped, studied, and eventually broken.
He possessed an abundance of time. His corporate work occupied the bright daylight hours, but the long, silent nights belonged entirely to him. He began tracking Ashish's immediate colleagues, then the rival politicians who appeared beside them, then the shadow financiers who funded their campaigns. He traced the models and actors who attended their private parties, and eventually, he became fixated on the ghosts. These were the people who appeared beside Ashish only once and then vanished. Those individuals became intensely fascinating precisely because of their brief proximity. Who was the quiet man standing directly behind Ashish at the charity dinner? Why had that specific woman been seated beside the health minister? Who was the sharply dressed person who arrived with the industrialist but never appeared in any press photographs afterwards? Pankaj's curiosity hardened into something cold and deeply methodical. He no longer looked only at what these powerful people chose to display to the public. He began aggressively hunting for the things they desperately wanted to hide.
His technical expertise rendered their digital boundaries utterly useless. He had spent his entire adult life manipulating networks and databases, and he understood the architecture of the internet well enough to know that the illusion of modern privacy was pathetically fragile. He did not bother to explain to himself exactly what he was doing or why he was doing it. At first, he simply told himself that he was satisfying a natural curiosity. Later, he justified his actions by reasoning that the information was already floating in the digital ether, and he was simply the one gathering it. Eventually, he crossed the threshold into highly encrypted, private spaces that had never been meant for his eyes. He bypassed rudimentary security protocols and accessed information from personal devices, secure email accounts, and private messaging servers. He read intimate conversations, downloaded hidden photographs, and absorbed the fragmented secrets of lives that their owners assumed were perfectly secure. He learned devastating things about people who had never heard his name and who would have looked right through him if they passed him on the pavement.
In his own mind, he did not behave like a criminal. That specific distinction mattered immensely to him. He was not stealing their wealth; he was not attempting to blackmail anyone, and he was not breaking into their physical homes. He was only looking. That phrase became his daily absolution, a psychological doorway through which he could freely pass without ever admitting to himself that he had become a monster. There was something deeply intoxicating in the utter simplicity of it. Looking required absolutely nothing from him in return. It did not demand the vulnerability of friendship, the courage of affection, or the risk of devastating rejection. Looking allowed him to remain perfectly hidden in the shadows, existing outside the lives of other people while possessing more intimate knowledge about them than their own families could ever imagine.
There was an endless, horrifying amount of filth to uncover. The more he watched, the more he discovered that the polished, glittering society surrounding Ashish was rotting from the inside out. The very men who spoke passionately about public development and poverty at massive rallies behaved like ravenous predators in private. Men who presented themselves as respectable pillars of the business community maintained deeply illegal arrangements they never discussed in the light of day. Politicians who preached conservative morality to the masses attended exclusive, drug-fuelled parties where all concepts of human decency were abandoned at the door. Celebrities whose public images were meticulously managed by agencies lived chaotic, abusive lives behind closed doors. Pankaj did not invent any of these sins. He merely brought them into the light, and that passive role became his ultimate justification. He convinced himself that he was not creating anything ugly; he was simply lifting the heavy velvet curtain and witnessing the truth that had always been there.
He began keeping extensive records. At first, his notes were scattered across his computer, consisting of brief observations typed hastily into hidden files or saved under meaningless numerical names. But the sheer volume of data quickly overwhelmed his digital folders, and eventually, even the encrypted files were not secure enough for his paranoia. He went to a local market and bought physical notebooks. The first was entirely ordinary, bound in a dark leather cover with thick ruled pages. He filled it rapidly with names, followed by tiny, meticulous pieces of information. He recorded dates, exact geographical coordinates, secret meetings, shell companies, and lists of people who publicly claimed to despise one another while repeatedly attending the same unlisted gatherings at private estates. Soon, there were five notebooks stacked on his desk. His handwriting grew progressively smaller, sharper, and more frantic as the pages filled, until the white spaces between the names completely disappeared beneath dense, black ink.
He began drawing the lines between them. Ashish remained near the absolute centre of the map, though Pankaj never consciously admitted to placing him there. Around Ashish, the circles of influence multiplied rapidly. There were politicians, real estate tycoons, media executives, high-ranking police officials, political fixers, and consultants who specialised in making enormous problems quietly disappear. Sometimes the names led Pankaj down much darker avenues, and he followed those paths without hesitation. He uncovered men whose public professions bore absolutely no resemblance to the violent work they actually performed in the dark. He found direct links to massive scandals that had inexplicably vanished from the evening news, criminal accusations that had been buried by the courts, and people who possessed the terrifying ability to survive legal consequences that would have instantly destroyed the lives of ordinary citizens.
The deeper he dug into the rot, the less the world resembled a simple division between respectable society and the criminal underworld. There were no good men left; there were only degrees of proximity to power. Everyone in that glittering circle was connected to someone terrible. Everyone owed a massive debt to someone dangerous. Everyone was hiding something capable of destroying them. Pankaj began to wonder whether society itself was simply a fragile arrangement in which people collectively agreed not to look too closely at the hands of their neighbours. Perhaps respectability depended entirely upon maintaining a safe distance. Perhaps the beautiful, wealthy world only existed because nobody had ever examined it from sufficiently close quarters. He looked anyway, and the more he stared into the abyss, the more the ordinary distinctions between right and wrong dissolved into a murky, suffocating grey.
At night, the damp apartment transformed into his personal observatory. He sat rigidly at the small wooden desk near the window, the curtains half drawn, while the sprawling city beyond the glass shifted with the late hours. In the early evening, heavy traffic flowed beneath the surrounding buildings in long ribbons of red and white light, the sound of blaring horns rising and fading into the general, chaotic noise of Kolkata. Later, the small street shops rolled down their iron shutters, and the pavements grew totally deserted. Around midnight, the sounds became sharp and distinct. He would hear the lonely wail of a distant train, a motorcycle accelerating too quickly down an empty avenue, a stray dog barking aggressively into the darkness, or the harsh metallic clatter of a security gate being locked. Sometimes the heavy monsoon rain slammed against the window pane, blurring the streetlights outside until the entire city appeared to be drowning beneath deep water. Pankaj remained wide awake through it all, the harsh glare of his computer monitor illuminating his hollow, exhausted face while the rest of the flat remained completely swallowed by the dark.
The vintage Rolex stayed firmly strapped to his wrist. Sometimes he would unclasp it while typing and place it gently beside his keyboard, and whenever he did, he found himself staring at the smooth sweep of its second hand. The heavy silver metal had begun to acquire a web of microscopic scratches over the years, tiny marks that could only be seen when the blue light of the monitor struck them at a specific angle. He could have easily replaced it with something newer and more practical. He never once considered doing so. It was the singular object from his past that he absolutely refused to surrender to the passage of time. Although he would never have confessed it to another living soul, there were terrifying nights when the watch felt less like a mechanical object he owned and more like the last surviving piece of the innocent boy he had once been.
He began to sleep in fractured, useless intervals. His work at the software company inevitably suffered, though he remained competent enough that nobody immediately noticed the decline. He would occasionally catch himself staring blankly at his office monitor in the middle of the afternoon, suddenly realising that he had spent the last hour thinking about a stolen photograph, a hidden financial connection, or a private conversation he had intercepted the previous night. Sometimes he would begin searching for a target's vulnerability during the day, feeling a sudden surge of regret at his own lack of control. He would swear to himself that he would stop, reasoning that there was absolutely no sane justification for spending another sleepless night hunting strangers whose lives had nothing to do with his own misery. Yet, the moment the sun set and he unlocked the door to his silent apartment, the same ravenous curiosity returned. He would sit at the wooden desk, power on the computer, and begin the hunt all over again.
The boundary separating Ashish's life from the corrupt lives surrounding him gradually ceased to exist. Pankaj was no longer simply following the trajectory of his estranged best friend; he was actively stalking an entire society. Ashish remained the gravitational centre, but the orbit around him had grown monstrously large. Pankaj moved through that digital ecosystem like a silent, invisible ghost, learning the darkest secrets of people who did not know his face, understanding complex relationships that had taken decades to solidify, and watching private depravities that would have instantly destroyed the public empires of those involved. There was something profoundly intoxicating about the omnipotence it granted him. For the first time since his mother had taken her final breath, Pankaj had found a purpose that demanded his absolute, undivided attention. The crushing loneliness remained inside his chest, but it had mutated into something weaponised. He was no longer a pathetic man sitting alone in an empty room with nothing to live for. He was the watcher in the dark, and the world, entirely ignorant of his presence, had begun to lay its throat bare before him.
There were still rare, agonising nights when he looked at Ashish's photographs and felt the old, pure affection return with such overwhelming force that he had to lean back in his chair and press the heels of his hands into his eyes. In those moments, he vividly remembered the scorching heat of Kharagpur, the red dust coating their shoes, the terrible meals they had shared, the long walks after midnight, and the evening when Ashish had clasped the heavy Rolex around his wrist and begged him not to disappear. He remembered the quiet promise he had made beneath the trees. I will find you. Perhaps, in some twisted, unrecognisable way, he had actually kept that promise. He had found Ashish. He knew exactly where the man went, whom he slept with, which politicians trusted him, which fixers feared him, and which industrialists wanted to buy him. He knew infinitely more about Ashish's life than Ashish could imagine.
And yet, the more Pankaj uncovered, the less satisfied the hunger became. The knowledge possessed a terrifying, insatiable appetite of its own. Every answer he found violently kicked open another locked door. Every door revealed another hidden room. Every room contained another corrupt person, and behind every single person, Pankaj began to suspect there was another terrible crime waiting to be dragged into the light. He had started this journey by simply asking himself how his old friend was doing. Now, he was meticulously disassembling the rotting foundation of the world in which his old friend lived.
The invitation arrived during a monsoon downpour that possessed no fury, only a cold and suffocating persistence. It was the kind of rain that turned the congested streets of Kolkata into black mirrors, drawing a thick, weeping veil across the windows of Pankaj's damp apartment. He had been sitting at his small wooden desk, bathed in the harsh blue light of his monitor, half working and half watching a silenced video of a political gathering. Ashish was on the screen, standing shoulder to shoulder with a cabinet minister, when a targeted advertisement suddenly interrupted the feed. Ordinarily, Pankaj would have dismissed the window without a second thought. Instead, something in the digital photograph arrested his hand. It displayed the towering, neoclassical facade of one of the city's most exclusive luxury hotels, its stonework washed in warm, golden light. Beneath the image rested a quiet announcement for a private celebration scheduled to take place there two nights later. The guest list was heavily redacted, yet it contained enough familiar surnames to make the true nature of the evening entirely unmistakable. Actors, models, industrialists, politicians, and people whose occupations were obscured by the protective language of wealth were expected to attend. It was not merely a social gathering. It was the exact kind of guarded ecosystem from which entire political careers emerged, where casual introductions became lucrative monopolies, and where men who appeared as bitter rivals in the morning newspapers discovered that they had been drinking from the same bottles of wine for years.
Pankaj read the announcement again, leaning closer to the glowing screen. He studied the high-resolution photographs of the hotel, the long entrance beneath the velvet canopy, the sprawling ballroom visible through heavy glass doors, and the crystal chandeliers suspended above tables dressed in immaculate white linen. He had seen places like this countless times before, but exclusively through the stolen, intercepted photographs of other people. He knew the names of the hotels and the heavily guarded clubs where men like Ashish spent their nights. He had memorised their interiors from pictures taken by people who naturally belonged within their walls, and he had watched videos of celebrations wherein waiters carried silver trays through rooms crowded with beautiful, unbothered strangers. Yet he had never crossed such a threshold himself. The thought came to him quietly in the dark, arriving almost as a morbid joke. He wondered what would happen if he simply walked through the front doors.
He sat back in his creaking chair and considered the sheer absurdity of the impulse. He possessed no invitation. He knew absolutely no one who would expect to see his face. He did not own the bespoke fabric such people wore, did not drive the armoured vehicles they arrived in, and certainly did not possess the golden, effortless confidence with which they seemed to occupy expensive spaces. More importantly, there was the terrifying variable of Ashish. The possibility of being recognised by his oldest friend should have been enough to extinguish the thought immediately. Instead, it introduced a new temptation that interested him far more than it frightened him. If he could manage to enter the hotel without being himself, he could finally stand inside the world he had spent so many suffocating years watching from the shadows. He would not have to imagine the scent of the air, the weight of a crystal glass in his hands, or the cadence of their voices when they were not filtered through a digital recording. For one single night, he could observe the corruption without a pane of glass standing between himself and the society he had meticulously mapped.
The decision became strangely simple once it took root in the silence of his apartment. Pankaj did not think of it as a criminal intrusion, but rather as a necessary, ultimate experiment. He spent the following day arranging his entry with the terrifying patience he had developed from years of digital stalking. His extensive technical knowledge allowed him to manipulate the event's encrypted registry systems, injecting a fabricated identity just deeply enough to create a digital invitation that would survive the preliminary security checks at the entrance. He did not linger over the ethical mechanics of the breach, nor did he feel the trembling nervousness that might once have accompanied such a massive risk. To him, the digital wall was merely another locked door in a world that had always been determined to keep him standing in the cold. He had spent years discovering exactly how these people lived, and now he wanted to know what it physically felt like to breathe their air.
The significantly more difficult task was deciding exactly who he would become to survive the room. He did not want merely to dress in borrowed clothes. He wanted to be entirely unrecognisable, requiring the face reflected in the hotel mirrors to belong to a man who had never drawn breath before that night. He spent hours studying candid photographs of wealthy businessmen, examining the microscopic physical distinctions that made a powerful middle-aged man appear convincing rather than theatrical. He studied the specific drape of a tailored lapel, the quietness of expensive leather shoes, the arrogant rigidity of a man's shoulders, and the absolute absence of any visible effort to impress the people around him. Eventually, he decided that his hair itself was a massive liability. He stood before the stained mirror in his bathroom, took a razor, and shaved his head completely. He spent a long time examining the result under the buzzing fluorescent bulb. Without hair, the architecture of his face appeared strangely brutal, significantly older and harder than he had imagined it would be.
Then came the skin. He darkened it heavily, avoiding the orange artificiality of cheap stage makeup, applying the tones with the patient, clinical work of a man determined to permanently bury his own physical reality. His eyebrows were altered as well, bleached lighter until they seemed almost white against the artificially darkened skin, producing a contrast that was deeply unsettling. He added subtle, shadowed lines around his eyes and mouth, altered the natural shape of his jaw with contouring, and forced the ordinary, defeated expression of his face into something entirely cold. The man looking back at him seemed to possess a completely different history. When he finally put on the rented, tailored suit he had chosen, the transformation became complete enough to satisfy his paranoia. The stranger standing in the mirror looked to be somewhere in his early forties. He was completely bald, broad-shouldered, dark-skinned, impeccably dressed, and carried himself with the subdued, heavy confidence of a man accustomed to being welcomed into exclusive rooms long before he had spoken his name. There was absolutely nothing remarkable about him except the heavy, suffocating impression of money. That, Pankaj realised, was perhaps the most useful disguise of all. Unimaginable wealth had a peculiar way of making strangers instantly familiar, and people rarely asked invasive questions of someone who appeared wealthy enough to destroy them.
He rented a heavy black BMW for the evening. When he saw it waiting beneath the flickering sodium lights outside his apartment building, something inside his chest stirred with a childish excitement that he had not felt since Kharagpur. He walked slowly around the vehicle once before getting in, almost amused by the absurdity of his own reflection in the tinted glass, then pulled the heavy door open and sat behind the wheel. The interior smelled intensely of treated leather and chemical polish. The controls were unfamiliar in the precise way expensive things often were, designed to subtly suggest that they had been manufactured for someone who already understood their worth. Pankaj drove carefully through the flooded streets, watching the chaotic sprawl of Kolkata slide past in distorted reflections of red brake lights and blinding white headlamps. For a long while he said absolutely nothing, simply gripping the leather steering wheel and letting the vibration of the German engine ground him in his new reality.
The hotel rose from the darkness of the city like a fortress built to keep the ordinary world at bay. Its massive entrance was covered by a vast, illuminated canopy beneath which luxury cars arrived one after another, their heavy doors opening to reveal women in flowing evening gowns and men in sharp dark suits. Valets moved with practised, silent efficiency while private security personnel stood near the entrance with earpieces, watching the arrivals with calculating eyes. Beyond the thick glass doors, the lobby shone with imported marble, polished brass, and warm yellow light. Pankaj slowed the BMW as he approached the drop-off zone, and for one brief, terrifying moment, his foot hovered over the accelerator, fighting the primal instinct to turn the steering wheel and drive back to his miserable apartment. Then a valet in a crisp uniform opened his door.
"Good evening, sir."
The words were entirely ordinary, a script repeated a hundred times a night, but they affected Pankaj more deeply than they should have.
"Good evening," Pankaj replied, keeping his voice low and remarkably steady.
The valet took the keys without ever looking him in the eye, and Pankaj stepped out of the vehicle and walked into the hotel. The air inside was aggressively cooled and carried the mingled, intoxicating scents of polished stone, expensive foreign perfume, fresh orchids, and the faint, sharp sweetness of alcohol. Music drifted from somewhere beyond the main reception area, not loud enough to dominate conversation but present everywhere as a soft, rhythmic pulse beneath the overlapping voices. The pristine marble floor perfectly reflected the massive chandeliers overhead, and people moved across it with the unhurried, graceful confidence of those who had never needed to wonder whether they belonged in a place like this. Pankaj walked slowly, resisting the overwhelming temptation to look around too openly. He had imagined this exact moment many times in the dark, but his imagination had never fully prepared him for the physical abundance of it. He absorbed the suffocating warmth of the lights, the thick softness of the carpets, the heavy scent of dying flowers, the gleam of crystal glass, and the low, confident murmur of hundreds of conversations overlapping one another in a symphony of influence.
At the entrance to the grand ballroom, his digital invitation was checked by a man holding an illuminated tablet. The attendant glanced at the screen, glanced briefly at Pankaj's altered face, and smiled warmly.
"Welcome, Mr Sen."
For a fraction of a second, Pankaj completely stopped breathing. He had fully expected suspicion. He had prepared his mind for a confrontation. Instead, there was instant, unquestioned recognition. It was not recognition of him, of course, but of the wealthy phantom he had manufactured. The name existed in the system, the invitation was mathematically valid, and the physical appearance perfectly matched the expectation of the room. The heavy wooden doors opened, and inside, the party seemed to have no physical end.
The ballroom was enormous, its high ceiling lost somewhere among dozens of crystal chandeliers and carefully arranged, flattering lighting. Tall, precarious arrangements of white flowers stood between large circular tables covered in heavy linen, while an army of waiters moved silently through the crowd carrying silver trays of champagne and red wine. Along one mirrored wall stood a long buffet beneath silver warming dishes, and beyond it was an elaborate dessert table where chocolate, fresh fruit, pastries, and sculpted sweets had been arranged with such artistic precision that they looked almost unreal. At the far end of the room, a raised stage had been prepared for speeches and musical performances, though for the moment the attention of the guests remained entirely fixed on one another. Soft music filled the spaces between the political conversations, and occasionally, loud laughter rose above the noise in sudden, arrogant bursts.
Pankaj stood just inside the doorway for several moments, simply taking it all in. He recognised the faces almost immediately. An actor whose films he had watched in his youth appeared near the glowing bar, speaking closely to a producer whose name had occurred repeatedly in Pankaj's hidden notebooks. A famous television personality laughed loudly with a cabinet minister's wife. Two rival industrialists stood together near a massive window, drinking from the exact same bottle of wine, while a young model he had seen in private photographs taken beside Ashish was speaking to another woman beneath a chandelier. There were journalists, banking executives, politicians, financiers, socialites, and people Pankaj could not immediately place but whose tailored clothes, relaxed manner, and company suggested that they were heavily accustomed to being known and feared. For years, he had watched these exact people through glowing screens in the dark, and now they were standing ten feet away, breathing the same manufactured air.
He accepted a tall glass of champagne from a passing waiter, feeling the freezing crystal against his fingers as tiny bubbles rose continuously through the pale golden liquid. He took a cautious sip and found himself smiling involuntarily. The champagne was not extraordinary, but the taste hardly mattered. What mattered was the absolute absurdity of the moment. He was standing directly in the centre of a corrupt world that had previously existed for him only as pixels on a screen, and absolutely nobody was looking at him as though he had wandered into the wrong room. A woman approached him while he was examining the ornate architecture of the ceiling.
"You are Mr Sen, are you not?" she asked, her voice carrying a relaxed, musical authority.
Pankaj turned slowly to face her. She was perhaps in her early thirties, dressed in a pale, flowing evening gown, wearing the calm and slightly amused expression of someone who had spent her entire adult life attending gatherings where everyone was eventually introduced to everyone else.
"Yes, I am," Pankaj answered.
"I do not think we have had the pleasure of meeting before."
"I do not think we have."
She smiled brightly, offering a perfectly manicured hand. "I am Rhea."
"Pankaj."
She looked at him for a moment, her eyes scanning the cut of his suit, perhaps trying to place him through some remembered corporate business connection or political alliance. "What exactly do you do, Pankaj?"
"Technology," he replied. "Mostly backend software and infrastructure systems."
"Ah," she said, flashing a bright smile. "You are one of those people who actually understands how everything works, while the rest of us simply pretend to understand."
Pankaj laughed, finding it a terrifyingly easy conversation. She introduced him to two other wealthy guests standing nearby, who asked what part of the country he operated in, whether his company worked in Kolkata, whether he travelled abroad often, and whether he personally knew a certain disgraced businessman. Pankaj answered naturally, his voice low and steady, inventing nothing more than was necessary to maintain the illusion. He quickly discovered that nobody actually cared very much about the details of his life. People at such gatherings did not investigate one another deeply. They exchanged useful fragments, measured each other's net worth, and moved on to the next target. Another man spoke to him at length about foreign investments, a model touched his arm and asked whether he was attending the party alone, and a television producer introduced himself and complained bitterly about the financing of a recent project.
Pankaj found himself moving smoothly from conversation to conversation with a relaxed ease he had never once experienced in his ordinary, miserable life. People smiled at him warmly, people actually listened when he spoke, and people made physical room for him at crowded tables. When he looked towards the bar, someone immediately offered to fetch him another drink. He began to understand, with a sickening clarity, that belonging was not always an emotional condition. Sometimes, belonging was simply the total absence of resistance. No one was asking him why he was there. No one was asking him who he truly was. No one was asking him to leave.
The evening grew warmer and louder as the hours passed. The champagne gave way to heavy red wine, and the conversations became significantly less formal and much more revealing. The live music changed tempo, people began moving between tables with greater frequency, and the vast ballroom acquired the pleasant, intoxicating disorder of a gathering that had entirely forgotten the rigid schedule with which it began. Pankaj ate much more than he had intended, sampling delicate fried starters, rich heavy curries, flatbreads served steaming hot, fragrant spiced rice, roasted vegetables, fish prepared with complex sauces whose exact ingredients he could not identify, and desserts that seemed almost too structurally beautiful to touch with a spoon. Then he noticed something deeply curious. At several tables across the room, waiters were delivering individual, silver-covered plates to specific guests, bypassing the massive buffet entirely. Pankaj watched one such plate appear before a woman sitting near him.
"What is that?" he asked as she lifted the heavy lid and smiled.
"My absolute favourite," she said, cutting into the warm food. "They asked everyone beforehand what they preferred to eat."
"Everyone gets their favourite dish?" Pankaj asked in genuine surprise.
"Apparently," she laughed, taking a long sip of wine. "It is completely ridiculous, is it not?"
Perhaps it was ridiculous, yet the minute detail affected him more profoundly than the expensive champagne, the leather of the BMW, or the crystal chandeliers had. Someone had asked what these people liked. Someone had recorded it. Someone had remembered. Someone had arranged for a particular, comforting dish to be waiting for each individual guest in a room packed with hundreds of powerful people. A few minutes later, a senior waiter approached Pankaj's table.
"Mr Sen?" the waiter inquired politely.
"Yes?"
"We have prepared your preference, sir."
The waiter placed a heavy, covered silver plate before him on the white linen cloth. Pankaj reached out and lifted the lid, and for a long moment, he simply stared down at the porcelain. It was one of the dishes he loved most in the world, the exact meal his mother used to prepare for him, cooked precisely in the manner he preferred. It was not an expensive dish in itself, and he had eaten it hundreds of times in ordinary roadside restaurants and in the cramped, humid kitchen of his own home. Yet seeing it there, served to him beneath the brilliant chandeliers of that immense ballroom, produced a physical emotion so sudden and violent that he had to look away from the table to compose himself. Someone had thought of him. The thought was entirely irrational, as the waiter did not know him and the digital system had simply recorded a random preference attached to the fake identity he had injected into the database. But human emotion does not always care about the cold mathematics of facts. For a few devastating seconds, Pankaj allowed himself to fully believe that the glittering room had deliberately made space for him. He ate slowly, savouring every single bite of the warm and heartbreakingly familiar food, and beneath the deafening noise of the political party, he experienced a small, almost painful surge of happiness. He had spent years feeling like a starving man standing outside a locked window in the freezing rain. That evening, for the very first time, the window had swung open.
He spoke to more people after the meal, laughing louder and listening closely to arrogant stories that would never have been told in an ordinary corporate office. He met an ageing actress whose romantic films his mother had once watched endlessly on their small television, and he spoke with a corrupt businessman whose name appeared several times in the darkest pages of his notebooks, discovering to his immense surprise that the man was genuinely funny and charming. He talked to a model about the terrible Kolkata traffic and to a local politician about cricket scores, finding himself discussing software architecture with a banking executive who knew almost nothing about it but seemed genuinely interested in learning. At one specific point near midnight, Pankaj actually forgot that the wealthy identity he was wearing was entirely false.
Then, he saw Ashish.
He was standing near the far side of the ballroom, illuminated by the golden glow of the bar, surrounded by three older men Pankaj recognised immediately from the evening news. Ashish wore a perfectly tailored dark suit and was holding a glass of dark red wine. His thick hair had begun to show the absolute slightest signs of grey at the temples, though from a distance he still looked almost exactly as Pankaj remembered him from Kharagpur, projecting an arrogant, confident, composed aura that made him effortlessly comfortable among people who possessed the power to destroy lives. He laughed loudly at something one of the men said, then placed a familiar, heavy hand briefly on another man's shoulder. Pankaj stopped moving completely. The immense noise of the ballroom seemed to recede, not because it actually became quieter, but because Pankaj's psychological attention narrowed until there was physical room for only one person in his entire vision.
He was so incredibly close that Pankaj could have easily walked across the polished marble floor, stopped in front of the group, and said his own real name out loud. He could have reached out, touched Ashish's shoulder, and watched the absolute astonishment and horror appear on his old friend's face, ending years of suffocating distance and bitter isolation in less than a minute. Instead, he remained frozen exactly where he was. Ashish looked in his direction once, his gaze sweeping lazily over the crowd, and their eyes almost met. Pankaj deliberately lowered his crystal glass and turned his body slightly, allowing another tall guest to pass between them and break the line of sight. Ashish looked away, taking another sip of wine, having completely failed to recognise him.
The knowledge should have hurt, feeling like the ultimate betrayal of their youth, but instead Pankaj felt something far stranger blooming in his chest. It was a profound, cowardly relief. He continued watching from the safety of the crowd as Ashish appeared near the edge of the stage later in the night. Pankaj stood among a group of wealthy guests several metres away and listened silently while Ashish spoke briefly with a notorious industrialist about an upcoming election. Once again, their physical paths crossed. Once again, Ashish came close enough that Pankaj could have easily spoken to him without shouting. Once again, he did not utter a single word. There was no logical reason to remain silent anymore, as the physical disguise was perfectly convincing, the digital invitation was legitimate enough to withstand scrutiny, and absolutely nobody would have questioned two men having a conversation in a room full of strangers. Pankaj could have simply stepped forward and said the words, but he did not.
He realised, with a cold clarity that surprised him, that approaching Ashish now would ruin everything. The exact moment he spoke, he would instantly stop being the omnipotent watcher and become the watched. Ashish would ask questions, wanting immediate explanations regarding where Pankaj had been living, why he had disappeared from their lives, and most dangerously, why he had come to this highly restricted party disguised as a wealthy stranger. The invisible, protective distance that Pankaj had spent years meticulously studying would suddenly collapse, exposing him completely, and Pankaj was absolutely not ready for that exposure. He preferred the cold, untouchable safety of being entirely unknown, standing at the very edge of Ashish's glittering world and seeing every filthy secret without ever being seen in return.
The party continued around them, oblivious to the ghost in the room. The music rose from the stage in a crescendo, someone laughed loudly near the bar, and a waiter passed carrying another tray of champagne. Beyond the towering glass walls, the city glittered beneath the wet darkness, Kolkata stretching away into the night with thousands of windows burning in the distance. Pankaj stood among people he had known intimately for years without ever formally meeting, surrounded by unimaginable wealth, beauty, power, and noise. For the first time in his entire miserable life, he did not feel entirely separate from them.
The party began to slowly empty sometime after midnight, though there was no precise moment when it could be said to have officially ended. The live music softened first, becoming less insistent as the tired musicians moved towards the final, slower selections of the evening. Conversations that had once filled the immense ballroom in a continuous, deafening tide broke into smaller, quieter islands. Guests stood beside tables gathering their phones and expensive handbags, men finished the last drops of their drinks near the bar, and the staff began collecting glasses from surfaces that had been crowded only an hour earlier. Beyond the great glass windows, the heavy rain had finally stopped, leaving the city dark, washed, and shining beneath the hotel lights. Kolkata seemed incredibly distant from that height, reduced to scattered headlights and illuminated windows, while inside the ballroom, the last remnants of the evening lingered in the warm, heavy smell of spilt wine, fading perfume, wilted flowers, and rich food.
Pankaj had absolutely no desire to leave. He had spent years looking at this corrupt world through a glowing screen in a freezing apartment, and now that he had finally breached its walls, he found himself deeply reluctant to return to the quiet, miserable reality waiting for him. The chemical excitement of the evening had not entirely faded from his blood. He had spoken to powerful people he had previously known only by their crimes, eaten food that had been prepared especially for him, laughed with dangerous strangers who had treated him as though his presence required no explanation, and moved smoothly through rooms that had once seemed impossibly distant. Even the heavy makeup and the bald head had begun to feel natural. When he passed a tall mirror in the corridor, he sometimes caught sight of the dark-skinned businessman looking back at him and experienced the peculiar, thrilling sensation of meeting a stranger who was somehow a better version of himself.
By then, perhaps twenty people remained in the ballroom, consisting of those who had nowhere urgent to be, or perhaps simply those who enjoyed the power of being among the absolute last to leave a room. A senior politician stood with two businessmen near the bar, speaking in hushed tones. An actress sat completely alone at a corner table, scrolling mindlessly through her phone while waiting for someone to return. Two young models were speaking quietly beside one of the large windows, while a film producer had removed his expensive jacket and was leaning far back in his chair, staring at the ceiling. A few others had gathered closely around the remaining bottles of wine and champagne, their voices slower, slurred, and warmer than they had been earlier in the evening. The room, which had seemed so enormously intimidating when it was full, now appeared strangely, claustrophobically intimate. Empty tables stretched vast distances between the remaining guests, covered with abandoned crystal glasses, crumpled linen napkins, and half-finished plates of cold food. The chandeliers still burned brightly above them, but the ballroom had lost the bright, almost theatrical energy of the first hours.
Pankaj stood near one of the floor-to-ceiling windows and looked down at the silent city. Somewhere far below, a single car moved through a wet intersection and disappeared behind a concrete building. He could see the blinking red warning lights of a distant radio tower, the pale, sickly glow of streetlamps reflected in the puddles on the road, and the occasional, brief flash of a late train moving through the absolute darkness. He reached into the pocket of his suit jacket and took out a cigarette, finding that the familiar, cheap object felt strangely ordinary and out of place in his hand after the staggering extravagance of the evening. He lit it, and the small orange flame illuminated his altered, severe face for an instant before disappearing with a click. He drew the thick smoke slowly deep into his lungs and held it there, allowing the familiar, comforting bitterness to settle over his tongue. He had smoked in countless ordinary, depressing places in his life, in damp stairwells, on rusted balconies, outside corporate offices, and beside broken roads where diesel buses shook the air as they passed. But there was something deeply ceremonial about doing it here, standing beneath the crystal chandeliers of a ballroom where absolutely nobody knew his real name.
He watched the grey smoke drift slowly upward toward the ceiling, and at first, nothing seemed unusual. Then, one of the lights flickered. It was so incredibly brief that nobody in the room reacted. The massive chandelier suspended above the nearest table dimmed for less than a fraction of a second and returned instantly to full brightness. Someone laughed at the bar and continued speaking, and a waiter glanced briefly towards the ceiling, shrugged, and looked away. Pankaj noticed it, but he did not attach any immediate significance to the event, knowing that massive buildings had their small, inevitable failures where electrical systems faltered, backup generators started, and machines corrected themselves. The world was full of such insignificant, momentary disturbances.
A second light flickered. Then another. The music stopped with a sharp, mechanical click.
This time, the remaining people noticed, and the ballroom fell into a brief, heavy silence in which the sound of a distant conversation from the outer corridors seemed suddenly, unnaturally loud. Someone near the windows asked loudly whether there had been a power failure, and a man standing near the bar lifted his smartphone and turned on its harsh white flashlight. Another person laughed nervously and assured the room that the luxury hotel's generators would kick in within a moment.
They did not. The chandeliers went completely dark, and every single light in the ballroom disappeared at the same instant.
The darkness was absolute, heavy, and suffocating. For several long seconds, absolutely nobody moved. There was no gradual adjustment of the eyes, no emergency red glow emanating from the walls, and no soft illumination beneath the exit doors. The enormous ballroom simply ceased to exist visually as the blackness swallowed the tables, the vast windows, the people, the dying flowers, and the polished marble floor. Pankaj could hear someone breathing very rapidly, very close to him. Somewhere across the pitch-black room, a heavy wooden chair scraped violently against the floor, and then the voices erupted in a chaotic wave.
"What is happening?" a voice shouted in the dark.
"Where is the hotel staff?" another demanded.
"Turn on your phone; I cannot see anything."
"Everybody just stay calm."
"Where is the main exit?"
Phone screens began appearing in the darkness, small, pale rectangular lifelines floating among the trapped people. Their harsh light illuminated terrified fragments of faces, grasping hands, and wide, startled eyes before disappearing again as people moved frantically. Someone shouted angrily for the security detail. Someone else began calling the hotel reception desk, while another voice, higher and tighter with panic, insisted that the heavy wooden doors were locked from the outside. Pankaj reached into his pocket and tried his own phone, but the screen remained dead black. He pressed the power button again, holding it down, but nothing happened. Around him, other glowing phones were beginning to behave strangely. Some would not turn on at all, while others showed a blank white screen before abruptly shutting down completely. A few guests began striking their expensive devices against their palms in the dark, as though sheer physical force might restore their connection to the outside world. The atmosphere in the room changed with a remarkable, terrifying speed. The golden confidence that had filled the ballroom earlier vanished almost completely, and people who had spent their entire evening speaking with the easy, untouchable authority of immense wealth and political influence were suddenly reduced to frightened, blind voices stumbling in the darkness.
Then, Pankaj smelled something. At first, it was incredibly faint, almost entirely hidden beneath the lingering scents of expensive perfume, stale wine, and his own tobacco smoke. It was difficult to identify. There was something deeply chemical about it, something dry and unfamiliar, carrying a faint, sickly sweetness that absolutely did not belong in the ballroom. He turned his head instinctively in the dark, trying to locate the source of the odour. Someone nearby coughed, and then another person coughed harder, producing a wet, tearing sound.
"What is that smell?" a woman asked, panic rising rapidly in her throat.
Nobody answered her. The very composition of the air seemed to change around them, growing thick and unnaturally heavy. Pankaj took another breath and immediately regretted it. His throat felt strange, as though it had been coated in dry dust. A terrible, crushing heaviness began spreading rapidly through his chest, not quite sharp pain, not yet pure fear, but a sickening sensation that his physical body had become subtly, dangerously disconnected from his commands. He tried to turn and move towards the nearest door, but his legs felt like lead, far heavier than they should have been. Someone stumbled hard against him in the dark, and a crystal glass fell from a table, shattering sharply on the marble. Then another fell.
The pitch-black room began to fill with the sound of desperate, hacking coughing. People shouted frantically for the doors to be broken open, and several bodies moved blindly towards the exits, but their movements quickly became sluggish and uncertain. One man reached the wall and slapped his hands against the wallpaper for support, sliding slowly downward. A woman dropped her phone, the glass cracking against the floor, and bent to pick it up, only to remain crouched there, breathing in ragged, heavy gasps. Pankaj's cigarette slipped completely from his numb fingers, the glowing orange ember striking the carpet, sparking briefly, and disappearing into the black. He tried to force his body to take another step, but the floor seemed to tilt violently upward. He reached blindly towards a table and caught its sharp edge, knocking over two wine glasses. The dark wine spilt across the white cloth and ran slowly towards the floor, sounding like water in the dark. His heart was beating far too quickly now, hammering aggressively against his ribs. He tried to breathe more slowly, more deliberately, but the air itself seemed entirely wrong, entirely devoid of oxygen.
Across the room, a heavy man fell, hitting the floor with a sickening thud. Then another body fell heavily nearby. The last thing Pankaj clearly remembered before his vision failed was the reflection of the dead ballroom lights in the glass windows, even though the room itself had gone completely dark. Outside those windows, the city remained brightly illuminated, utterly indifferent, and impossibly far away. Somewhere beyond that glass, thousands of people continued their ordinary, safe lives. Cars moved through the rain, trains travelled along the tracks, people slept safely in their beds, and somewhere, perhaps, someone was still laughing. Inside the locked ballroom, one by one, the powerful people who had been so vibrant and untouchable only minutes before were collapsing silently onto the floor. Pankaj's knees gave way entirely. The darkness rose rapidly around him like rising water, and then, there was nothing.

When consciousness finally returned to Pankaj, it did not bring the mercy of orientation. The heavy, suffocating darkness offered no familiar ceiling, no polished hotel ballroom, and no pale morning light bleeding through drawn curtains. Instead, there was a vast, terrifying emptiness broken only by the weak, jaundiced glare of industrial lamps suspended somewhere impossibly high above. Their anaemic light struggled to penetrate the immense vertical space, dissolving into thick, dusty shadows long before it ever reached the ground. Pankaj attempted to shift his weight, driven by the primal instinct of a waking animal, but cold, unyielding iron bit immediately into his flesh. He was strapped securely within a rigid metal structure that had been bolted directly into the concrete floor, his wrists and ankles locked inside heavy steel restraints. He pulled against them, a sudden spike of adrenaline flooding his system, and the metal answered with a dull, heavy clank that echoed horribly in the dark.
He was not alone. The vast, cavernous silence was rapidly filling with the chaotic, terrified sounds of human panic. Somewhere in the gloom nearby, a woman was weeping uncontrollably, her sobs bouncing off unseen walls. An older man was swearing violently, his voice cracking as he frantically demanded the immediate presence of his security detail. Another voice, tight with rising hysteria, repeatedly begged for a doctor. The shouts and cries emerged from different elevations and distant corners of the massive space, the acoustics twisting and distorting the voices as they travelled through a labyrinth of concrete and steel.
Pankaj forced his head back against the metal brace and looked upward. The sheer, impossible scale of the architecture instantly emptied his mind of all rational thought. They were suspended inside a colossal, vertical tower. It rose endlessly above them, ascending so high that the upper tiers were completely swallowed by the suffocating blackness. Rusted metal walkways crisscrossed the structure at dizzying elevations, while narrow, perilous platforms clung to the curved central walls, connected by steep industrial ladders and grated staircases that induced severe vertigo even from below. Thick, vertical steel girders formed an inescapable cage around the entire interior perimeter, enclosing the prisoners within the rotting skeleton of a brutalist silo. The concrete walls were unfinished, stained with dark, weeping streaks of ancient dampness and severe neglect. Thick, corroded pipes disappeared into the darkness overhead like the veins of a massive, dead beast. Somewhere far above, water dripped with a steady, metronomic rhythm, each heavy drop producing a sharp, metallic echo that measured the passing seconds.
It was not merely a room. It was an abyssal prison built vertically, designed to make its inhabitants feel profoundly insignificant.
Pankaj slowly turned his head as far as the restraints would allow, scanning the shadows. There were people everywhere. The same elite society that had been drinking imported champagne and negotiating political empires only hours earlier was now scattered throughout the brutal structure, each individual locked within their own isolated metal enclosure. Some were still slumped forward, unconscious in their restraints. Others were struggling violently, thrashing against the unyielding steel until their wrists bled. A woman in a torn evening gown was screaming a man's name over and over again, her voice tearing at her throat. One of the captured politicians had begun shouting aggressive threats into the void, promising severe legal and physical retribution to whoever had orchestrated this, clinging desperately to the delusion that the language of wealth and power still possessed any currency in this place.
But the most deeply frightening element of the tower was not the heavy iron restraints or the terrifying drop to the unseen floor below. It was the cameras.
They were absolutely everywhere. Small, black, mechanised lenses had been embedded into the weeping concrete walls at calculated intervals. Some were mounted on heavy brackets high above the walkways, monitoring the vast expanse of the silo, while others were positioned at eye level, pointing with predatory precision directly at individual prisoners. A few of the lenses whirred softly, making almost imperceptible mechanical adjustments as they tracked the frantic movements of the captives. Tiny, unblinking red indicators glowed beside the lenses, cutting through the heavy darkness like the eyes of silent, starving wolves.
Pankaj stared directly at the nearest lens. The camera stared blankly back at him.
Around him, the other prisoners were finally beginning to notice the red lights piercing the gloom, and the nature of their panic shifted from confusion to profound violation.
"What the hell is this?" a man screamed from a higher platform, his voice trembling. "Who brought us here?"
"Where is the hotel?" a woman cried out. "Can anybody hear me?"
"I want my lawyer!" another voice bellowed, heavy with desperate arrogance. "Somebody call the police right now!"
Their frantic demands echoed upward through the hollow shaft of the tower and returned to them strangely altered, mocking them, as though the decaying building itself were repeating their own terror back to them. Pankaj remained absolutely silent, conserving his energy, and carefully examined the terrified faces illuminated by the weak industrial lights. He knew almost all of them intimately. There was the corrupt cabinet minister from the bar. There was the ageing actress. There were the ruthless industrialists and the young fashion model. These were the exact people whose names he had spent years meticulously transcribing into his hidden notebooks, whose movements he had tracked through digital photographs, and whose filthy secrets he had traced through shell companies and political associations. The faces that had previously belonged exclusively to glowing screens and glossy newspaper covers were now stripped entirely of their protective wealth, their heavy makeup smeared, their bespoke clothing ruined by dirt and sweat. Their voices had completely lost the polished, untouchable confidence of the ballroom.
Pankaj looked higher, straining his neck. Far above them, somewhere entirely beyond the reach of the jaundiced light, the brutalist tower continued its vertical ascent. He could not see a roof. He could not see the outside world. He could not see a single avenue of escape.
For several agonising minutes, the tower contained nothing except raw, unfiltered panic. The prisoners shouted until their vocal cords tore, their voices breaking against the damp concrete and returning as distorted, ghostly echoes from the abyss above. They screamed for the police, for their private security guards, for hotel management, for senior ministers, for their lawyers, and for their powerful friends, invoking the names of the influential as though the mere mention of a political titan possessed the magical ability to shatter the iron walls holding them. Some thrashed violently against their restraints until the rough metal sliced deeply into their skin, the scent of fresh blood mingling with the foul air. Others tried desperately to communicate with the captives imprisoned nearest to them, calling across the vast, empty vertical space and receiving equally frightened replies from walkways they could barely see. A few simply ceased struggling and stared upward in catatonic shock, their faces drained of all human expression, their minds entirely unable to comprehend how the warm, golden lights and the soft music of the luxury hotel had evaporated, replaced by this immense, suffocating shaft of concrete and steel. The strangest, most oppressive element was the biting cold. It seemed to radiate directly from the walls themselves, carrying the stale, rotting odour of damp concrete, machine oil, rust, and something infinitely older and darker that none of them could easily identify. Every breath they took carried the heavy, dead air of a tomb that had been sealed for decades.
Then, somewhere high above their heads, a television monitor switched on.
The sound was so violently, domestically ordinary that it felt deeply obscene in the brutalist nightmare of the tower. A low, heavy electrical hum vibrated through the metal scaffolding, followed instantly by a sharp, cracking burst of audio static. A massive rectangle of blinding blue-white light suddenly illuminated a high section of the curved wall, casting long, skeletal shadows of the metal walkways across the concrete. One by one, the terrified faces ceased their screaming and turned upwards toward the screen. The monitor was enormous, suspended at a calculated angle ensuring that almost every single restrained prisoner could see the broadcast from their cage. Static crawled aggressively across the glowing image for several tense seconds before the picture slowly, deliberately sharpened into focus.
A man was sitting alone in a completely dark room.
His entire head and face were wrapped tightly in thick, medical bandages. The sterile white cloth covered his forehead, his cheeks, his heavy jaw, and his throat, layered so carefully and tightly that there was absolutely no suggestion of the human bone structure beneath it. Only his eyes were left visible through narrow slits in the fabric. They were dark, entirely steady, and extraordinarily still. There was absolutely no readable expression around them—no burning anger, no sadistic excitement, and no theatrical cruelty. The bandages themselves were not stained with enough blood to suggest a fresh, traumatic injury, yet they were not immaculate either. They looked yellowed and worn, as though the man had been living inside the suffocating wrap for a very long time.
He sat motionless behind a plain, heavy wooden table. There was no decoration of any kind in the room behind him. There was no window to indicate the time of day, no painting, and no secondary furniture except the chair supporting him. The darkness behind his shoulders seemed to continue indefinitely into a void.
For several agonising seconds, the bandaged man did absolutely nothing. He simply stared directly into the camera lens. The psychological effect of his absolute stillness was infinitely worse than if he had been screaming threats.
A wealthy man restrained near the lower tier finally broke the silence, screaming upwards at the screen. "Who are you?"
The bandaged man did not blink, nor did he acknowledge the shout. He continued staring into the lens, allowing the silence to stretch until it became physically unbearable.
Then, he spoke.
"Good evening."
His voice was terrifyingly calm and perfectly human. There was no electronic distortion masking his vocal cords, no artificial depth added for dramatic effect, and absolutely no attempt at projecting menace. It sounded exactly like the voice of an educated man conducting a polite, ordinary conversation in a quiet room. That stark, chilling normality made the chaotic prisoners instantly freeze and listen more carefully.
"My name is Karam."
The name meant absolutely nothing to any of them. A woman near the centre of the tower began weeping softly. Another man aggressively demanded to know exactly where they had been taken. Karam waited patiently, completely unbothered, until the echoes of their shouting had naturally diminished before he continued.
"You have been unconscious for quite some time. You are now secured inside a structure that was designed and prepared specifically for you. Every single level of this facility is monitored. Every corridor, every concrete chamber, every holding cell, and every vertical passage contains high-definition cameras. Some of these cameras are plainly visible to you. Many others are not. There is absolutely nowhere inside this building that I cannot see you."

The prisoners began looking around the vast space again, but their gazes had fundamentally changed. What had initially seemed like harmless industrial equipment suddenly became deeply sinister. They saw the small black lenses embedded in the weeping concrete walls. They noticed the cameras hanging like mechanical bats beneath the grated metal walkways. They saw the lenses fixed high above them, almost invisible against the total darkness of the upper ceiling, while others were positioned directly at eye level, staring intimately into their faces. The tiny red indicator lights glowed beside them, tracking their every twitch. A few of the heavier cameras moved with barely perceptible mechanical whirs, their servos adjusting focus whenever a captive shifted their weight against the iron restraints.
Karam allowed them to process their total lack of privacy before he spoke again.
"You are currently being recorded."
Nobody in the tower dared to speak. The silence was absolute.
"And the recording," Karam continued, his voice perfectly level, "is being streamed."
A nervous, breathy laugh escaped from a man restrained somewhere below Pankaj. It sounded entirely hysterical, the sound of a mind actively rejecting reality.
"No," the man gasped, shaking his head against the metal brace. "No fucking way. You're lying."
Karam looked directly into the camera lens, his dark eyes locking onto the feed.
"The entire world is watching you right now."
Something fundamental broke inside the tower at that exact moment. Until those words were spoken, the prisoners had been consumed entirely by the logistics of escape. Their terror had been deeply private and immediate: how to violently break the iron restraints, how to locate the exit doors, how to survive the physical torment of whatever madman had abducted them. But Karam’s quiet statement introduced a new, incomprehensible layer of horror. If the cameras were actively transmitting their feeds to the open internet, then there was absolutely no private terror left to them. Their ruined faces, their weeping panic, their physical weakness, and every desperate, cowardly thing they were about to do could already be broadcasting to millions of strangers outside. The realisation that they had been stripped of their ultimate weapon—their absolute control over their own public narrative—transformed the atmosphere from fear to utter devastation. Several prisoners instinctively tried to turn their faces away from the red lights, burying their chins into their chests. Others stared upwards at the unblinking lenses with expressions of hollow disbelief. One prominent socialite began begging the camera in front of her to turn away, weeping openly.
Karam did not offer them a second of reprieve.
"For years," he said, the calmness of his delivery slicing through the ambient sobbing, "you firmly believed that the world belonged to you because you alone could control exactly what the world was allowed to see. You controlled the flow of information. You controlled the legal investigations. You controlled the state's institutions. You controlled the stories printed in the morning papers. Whenever something threatened your empire, you simply found a convenient way to make it disappear. Sometimes, you paid someone off. Sometimes, you threatened a family. Sometimes, you made a single phone call to a judge. Sometimes, you simply waited comfortably in your estates until the easily distracted public became interested in a new scandal."
The live feed of Karam’s face abruptly vanished, replaced by a rapid succession of digital photographs.
Old, grainy photographs appeared first, followed quickly by newer, high-resolution images. Pankaj recognised the visual data immediately; it was the exact same corrupt web he had meticulously mapped in the dark of his apartment. There were imposing government buildings. Lavish corporate offices. Rotting, underfunded government hospitals. Unsafe construction sites. Closed-door political meetings. Private, drug-fueled dinners at luxury estates. Highly classified company documents. Buried newspaper articles. Empty court corridors. Local police stations. The screen flashed faces beside faces, linking politicians to criminals. Names appeared beside incriminating dates. Powerful men were shown shaking hands with known cartel fixers. Women were photographed leaving government buildings late at night. Unmarked vehicles were parked outside private gates. Secret meetings that had supposedly never taken place were displayed in undeniable, high-definition clarity.
The images changed with a relentless, punishing rapidity, but they did not move randomly. They were arranged according to a devastating, undeniable pattern of guilt that only Karam fully understood.
Several of the restrained prisoners instantly recognised themselves in the damning photographs.
One prominent state minister began shaking his head violently against his metal collar.
"No," he muttered, his eyes wide with panic.
His photograph appeared again, this time showing him accepting a heavy briefcase.
"No, no, that is a fake! That is completely doctored!"
Another captive, a wealthy media executive, screamed at the screen, "Turn it off! Turn that off right now!"
Karam’s calm, detached voice continued speaking smoothly over the relentless barrage of evidence.
"You learned very early on that ordinary people forget. You learned that public outrage has a remarkably short memory. You learned that a poor man can scream for justice for ten years and still die entirely unheard, while a powerful man can simply deny a crime once on television and be instantly believed. You learned that official records could be quietly altered, that crucial witnesses could simply disappear in the night, that federal investigations could be indefinitely stalled, and that your worst atrocities could always be buried beneath newer, more entertaining stories."
The massive screen flashed a horrifying photograph of a collapsed, substandard apartment building, dust rising from the crushed concrete. Then, it cut to a filthy, overcrowded corridor in a dying government hospital. Then, a skeletal child stood outside the locked gate of a ruined school. Finally, it showed a group of sharply dressed men entering a political campaign office, their faces blurred by motion but their identities unmistakable.
The tragic images vanished almost as soon as they had appeared, leaving a searing afterimage on the retinas of the captives.
"You learned how to execute all of this," Karam said, his voice dropping slightly in pitch, "and eventually, you simply stopped seeing the human beings crushed underneath the weight of your success."
The terrified prisoners had become deathly silent, the reality of their exposure paralysing their vocal cords.
"Every single one of you possesses a history."
The broadcast cut back to Karam’s bandaged face. His dark eyes remained fixed mercilessly on the camera lens.
"Some of you buried your histories far more carefully than others. Some of you actively convinced yourselves that the terrible things you did were an absolute necessity for the greater good. Some of you called it standard business practice. Some of you called it the reality of politics. Some of you called it legal procedure. Some of you called it an unfortunate, unavoidable mistake. Some of you simply called it survival."
He paused, letting the weight of their hypocrisy hang in the damp air.
"You found dozens of different, polite words to describe the same thing."
He leaned slightly closer to the lens.
"You hurt people."
"You forgot them."
A woman’s ragged, terrified breathing became loudly audible through the echoing shaft.
"And because you were able to forget them," Karam continued, his tone entirely devoid of anger, "you truly believed they no longer mattered."
The fact that the words were spoken without a single trace of rage was exactly what made them so profoundly terrifying. Karam did not sound like a wounded man desperately seeking personal revenge. He sounded like a cold, clinical scientist stating the inevitable result of a long, calculated experiment.
"You are restrained in this tower today because your corrupt lives crossed paths with one another. Some of you directly benefited from the same broken systems. Some of you actively protected one another from the law. Some of you paid one another for silence. Some of you remained completely silent because speaking the truth would have been financially inconvenient. Some of you destroyed vital evidence. Some of you altered medical records. Some of you used your political influence to protect monsters who should have faced severe consequences. Some of you made vast fortunes from the suffering of the weak."
His dark eyes shifted almost imperceptibly behind the white bandages.

"And some of you simply watched."
Pankaj felt something cold and incredibly heavy tighten violently inside his chest. The specific phrasing entered his mind with a sickening, terrifying familiarity.
Some simply watched.
Karam allowed the absolute silence to stretch, ensuring the weight of the accusation settled deeply into the bones of every person in the room.
"Now, you will all watch."
The television screen briefly snapped to black. When the feed returned a second later, Karam had moved significantly closer to the camera, his eyes filling the frame.
"You will face a series of physical and psychological tests. Each specific test concerns the exact suffering you chose to comfortably ignore while you were free. You will be given brutal opportunities to make different choices. You will be given opportunities to finally accept responsibility for your actions. You will be given opportunities to prove to the watching world that your regret actually means something when there is finally a physical price attached to it."
"For the very first time in your pampered lives, you are all truly equal."
That final, devastating sentence provoked an immediate explosion of fury from the cages.
"You have no idea who you're dealing with!"
"Do you know who my father is, you sick bastard?"
"You'll be a dead man the second they find us!"
"My lawyers will absolutely destroy you and your entire family!"
"Show your fucking face, you coward!"
The chaotic voices rose aggressively throughout the concrete tower. Their empty, desperate threats collided with one another, travelling upwards until the entire brutalist structure seemed to be filled with the deafening noise of trapped, frightened animals tearing at their cages.
The massive television screen snapped to black, plunging the upper levels back into darkness.
The heavy silence that followed felt infinitely more oppressive than the noise. For several long seconds, absolutely nobody moved. The only sound was the collective, uneven breathing of twenty terrified people, echoing faintly from the different levels of the tower. Then, a man strapped to one of the lower metal platforms began shouting in a voice thick with violent entitlement.
He was broad-shouldered, aggressively middle-aged, and still dressed in the bespoke, ruined suit he had worn to the gala. His name was Rajiv Agarwal, a ruthless industrialist whose massive manufacturing companies had comfortably survived severe environmental complaints, violent labour disputes, and federal investigations that had somehow always ended without a single legal consequence. The initial shock of the abduction had already begun burning into pure, unadulterated anger inside his chest. Perhaps anger was simply the only emotion his mind was capable of processing when his authority was challenged.
"This is absolute bullshit!" Rajiv screamed, violently twisting his neck to glare at the nearest camera lens. "You think you're some kind of god? You think you can judge me?"
The camera stared back, silent and indifferent.
Rajiv pulled ferociously against his heavy restraints, his muscles straining against the iron.
"Do you hear me, you coward?"
He struggled harder, his face turning dark red with exertion. The metal restraints groaned loudly against the concrete floor.
"You cannot keep us in here! My people will tear this city apart looking for me!"
Several of the terrified prisoners above him shouted for him to stop, begging him not to provoke the architect of this nightmare. Rajiv completely ignored them, his arrogance overriding his survival instinct. He looked directly into the glowing red light of the nearest camera.
"Come out!" he roared, his voice cracking against the damp concrete walls. "Come out here and say this to my face, you bandaged freak!"
He slammed his heavy fist against the iron railing beside the camera lens. The sharp, metallic sound rang out like a gunshot through the hollow tower. He struck it again. Then again, harder.
The camera remained entirely motionless.
Rajiv raised his bruised fist to strike the metal a fourth time.
There was a faint, remarkably precise mechanical click from somewhere deep inside the concrete wall behind the lens.
Rajiv froze; his fist suspended in the air. For the very first time since he had awakened in the tower, genuine, unadulterated terror bled through the anger on his face.
A narrow, perfectly concealed section of the concrete wall beside the camera smoothly slid open.
The matte-black barrel of a mechanised rifle emerged from the darkness.
The gunshot was so catastrophically loud inside the enclosed, echoing tower that several prisoners screamed in sheer terror before their brains could even process what had happened. A blinding flash of muzzle flare illuminated the lower tier. Rajiv's heavy right arm jerked violently backwards, striking the iron restraint with a sickening crunch of shattering bone. A massive spray of bright arterial blood exploded across the grey concrete and soaked his tailored sleeve. He collapsed heavily backwards against his metal chair, making a horrific, gurgling sound that was half-scream and half-disbelief, his left hand instinctively clutching the ruined, bleeding mass of his bicep against his chest.
For a long, agonising moment, absolutely nobody in the tower moved or breathed.
With a soft mechanical whir, the rifle smoothly withdrew into the shadows. The narrow opening in the concrete slid shut, clicking into place. The wall became solid and featureless once again.
It had all occurred with such surgical, automated speed that the human mind violently struggled to accept the reality of the violence. There was no visible guard pulling a trigger, no flashing warning light, and no physical mechanism that the wealthy prisoners could comprehend or negotiate with. A lethal weapon had simply materialised from solid rock, shattered a man's arm, and vanished back into the architecture, as though the concrete building itself had grown a limb to punish defiance.
Rajiv stared blankly at the solid wall where the barrel had been, his face entirely drained of colour, his chest heaving as shock rapidly overtook his nervous system.
The massive television monitor abruptly switched back on. Karam was sitting in the same position, perfectly still.
The screen snapped to black for the final time.
Rajiv finally began sobbing openly, a pathetic, broken sound that echoed through the vast space. He was not crying from the physical pain of the shattered bone alone, but from the sudden, devastating collapse of his lifelong belief that wealth and physical distance offered absolute protection. He looked around the dark tower with wide, terrified eyes, as though every single piece of stained concrete and rusted steel had become a living, breathing entity completely capable of killing him. The other prisoners watched him bleed with the same paralysing horror. They no longer looked at the small black lenses as mere recording machines. They looked at them as living eyes, attached to a mind that possessed absolute, unquestionable control over their lives.
Pankaj remained completely still within his iron restraints, his heart hammering against his ribs. He slowly looked at the camera positioned directly above his cage, then at another mounted across the expanse of the tower, and finally higher towards the constellation of tiny red lights scattered throughout the suffocating darkness. He had spent years of his life meticulously studying the art of surveillance. He understood, better than anyone else in that decaying tower, the peculiar, God-like power of seeing without ever being seen.
Far away from the suffocating dampness of the concrete tower, the initial broadcast was discovered entirely by accident. A fatigued night producer at a minor twenty-four-hour news channel in Kolkata initially assumed the raw, unedited footage was some kind of elaborate, tasteless publicity stunt. The encrypted signal had forcefully overwritten their active feed during the dead, early hours of the morning, displaying a vast industrial structure filled with terrified people, filmed simultaneously from dozens of high-definition angles. There was no channel identification watermark, no political advertisement, no manifesto, and absolutely no indication of where the transmission was originating from. The producer, irritated by the interruption, reached out to cut the signal and return to scheduled programming, but his hand hovered over the console when one of the terrified, ruined faces on the monitor suddenly became recognisable. He leaned closer to the glowing screen, his eyes widening in disbelief, and then he stood up slowly, the blood draining completely from his face.
Within minutes, the sleepy, hollow newsroom had mutated into absolute chaos. Senior editors were dragged from their beds and summoned from other floors of the building. Frantic reporters began aggressively searching digital databases and calling high-ranking police contacts who were just waking up. Producers froze the live frames on their monitors and ran the faces through facial recognition software. One powerful name was confirmed, then a second, and then a third. The people weeping and bleeding inside the concrete tower were not ordinary hostages. They were sitting cabinet ministers, billionaire industrialists, untouchable public figures, ruthless businessmen, and prominent socialites whose names had dominated the front pages of newspapers and the closed corridors of government offices for a decade. The horrifying broadcast was completely live.
Rival news channels rapidly intercepted the unencrypted signal and began carrying the footage across their own networks. Social media algorithms caught the terrifying images, filling timelines with screenshots, wild speculation, and genuine horror. Families woke their sleeping relatives to stare at their televisions. Police officers gathered silently around monitors in local station houses, their coffee growing cold. Senior bureaucrats were abruptly called into emergency meetings, and ministers who had been asleep in their guarded estates were suddenly answering secure phones with trembling hands. Within a few short hours, the footage had entirely escaped the control of the original broadcast, spreading across the fractured country like a virulent digital infection.
Millions of ordinary people watched from roadside tea stalls and dark bedrooms, from crowded railway platforms and sterile hospital waiting rooms, from office desks and flickering smartphone screens. They watched the untouchable elite become utterly helpless. They watched men who had spent decades speaking with golden, unbothered confidence before press cameras now beg for mercy from a masked man whose face they could not see. They watched the ruthless industrialist Rajiv Agarwal bleeding uncontrollably inside his iron cage, his shattered arm hanging uselessly. They watched desperately frightened people invoking powerful names and political titles that no longer seemed to possess a single ounce of currency in the dark.
The state government swiftly announced that a special, high-level investigation had been formed, and heavily armed search teams began moving aggressively through abandoned factories, unfinished apartment towers, forgotten warehouses, underground facilities, and remote industrial sectors along the edges of the city. Satellite imagery was scrutinised by intelligence agencies. Decades-old construction records were unearthed and searched for matching brutalist architecture. Transport movements and highway cameras were meticulously studied, and police detectives began frantically retracing the final known movements of every single prisoner from the luxury hotel. Yet, all attempts to trace the broadcast produced digital locations that led nowhere. False signals pinged and vanished across completely different geographical regions of the country. Whoever had engineered the system had perfectly anticipated the desperation of the search. Absolutely nobody knew where the tower was.
The first sound the prisoners heard after Karam’s broadcast ended was the terrible, rhythmic sound of breathing. At first, none of them could determine where the noise was originating from. It seemed to rise directly through the iron grates beneath their feet rather than travel through the freezing air—a slow, uneven inhalation followed by a long, heavy mechanical exhalation, as though something enormous and ancient were breathing deep beneath the foundations of the tower. The sound was so impossibly deep that they felt it long before their ears registered the noise, a faint, terrifying vibration travelling up through the soles of their expensive leather shoes and vibrating directly into their bones. Every few seconds, the rusted metal scaffolding beneath them gave a tiny, violent shudder. Somewhere in the pitch-black darkness far below, unseen industrial machinery groaned and settled, groaned again, and settled again, possessing the patient, agonising discomfort of a massive, living beast that had been awake for far too long.
For several suffocating minutes, absolutely nobody spoke. Karam's physical absence had left behind a heavy silence that felt infinitely more oppressive than his presence. While he had been speaking through the monitor, there had at least been a tangible voice to hate, a bandaged face to stare at, a human being somewhere beyond the television screen whom they could desperately imagine reaching, threatening, bargaining with, or perhaps even convincing with their wealth. Now, there was only the indifferent, crushing weight of the tower. The mechanised cameras remained rigidly fixed upon them from the dark corners of the walls, their tiny red indicators glowing without a trace of human expression, and every single prisoner had begun to feel the indecent, violating intimacy of being actively watched while they were terrified. A man pressed his bleeding arm tightly against his chest, trying to hide his agony. A woman wiped tears from her ruined makeup with violently trembling fingers. Someone repeatedly swallowed against a throat so dry it felt lined with cracked glass. Another captive stared blankly at the concrete floor as though utterly refusing to meet the unblinking lenses. None of their suffering was private anymore. Every pathetic weakness, every involuntary physical tremor, and every desperate, panicked movement had become part of a grand spectacle taking place somewhere beyond the cold walls.
Then, every television monitor suspended in the massive chamber switched on simultaneously with a sharp, synchronised crack. The massive screens were pitch black for a brief moment, then instantly filled with hissing grey static. The static slowly dissolved into an image so pale, distorted, and blurred that several of the restrained captives leaned forward against their iron collars before fully understanding what they were looking at. The picture sharpened with brutal clarity.
It was water. An entire rural town had been violently swallowed by it.
The archival footage appeared to have been recorded from a military helicopter or some other high vantage point during the absolute height of a devastating monsoon flood. Paved roads had become raging rivers of thick, churning brown water, carrying debris and dead livestock. The roofs of submerged houses emerged from the violent floodwaters like the tops of mossy gravestones. Ancient trees stood submerged almost to their highest branches, their leaves shaking violently beneath a relentless, punishing rain. A corrugated tin sheet spun slowly and dangerously through a narrow, flooded lane. A wooden bed frame floated upside down beside a crumbling brick wall. A rusted refrigerator drifted past the shattered upper window of a house with its door hanging wide open, carrying the strange, tragic domestic intimacy of a home that had been violently emptied of all life.
Farther away, somewhere beyond the frame, a temple loudspeaker miraculously continued broadcasting through the torrential rain. The devotional song was faint, fractured, and heavily distorted by the distance and the static of the recording. Its ancient melody rose and fell above the roaring water with an almost unbearable, indifferent calm, sounding exactly as though the gods were continuing their evening prayers while the desperate people beneath them drowned in the mud.
The camera moved lower, the lens cutting through the heavy rain. There were terrified people huddled on the collapsing roofs. A young woman held a small child tightly beneath a torn blue plastic sheet while the freezing rain struck both of them without mercy. An old man stood waist-deep in the churning brown water outside his flooded house, one frail hand raised desperately toward a motorised rescue boat that was already moving away toward the horizon. Three small children sat stranded on the slanted roof of a primary school building with their knees pulled tightly against their chests, shivering uncontrollably. A man clung to a rusted television antenna with both bleeding hands to keep from being swept into the current. On another fragile roof, several people were waving frantic arms and shouting silently toward the helicopter passing overhead.
The helicopter did not stop. The camera did not look away.
That was exactly what made the footage so deeply, psychologically difficult to watch. There was no manipulative background music designed to tell the audience when to feel sorrow, no polished news narrator explaining who had suffered and why the tragedy had occurred, and no sympathetic voice offering the cheap comfort of a political interpretation. The camera simply hovered there, cold and objective, watching desperate people wait for a salvation that simply did not come. One of the prisoners whispered nervously that the footage was years old. Another recognised the specific, devastated district. Someone muttered a village name, and then another name was spoken into the damp air. The hushed words moved through the metal chamber like a contagious sickness, a creeping realisation of exactly whose past they were being forced to witness.
Finally, the screens cut abruptly, showing a well-dressed man standing safely beneath a pristine white canopy, smiling widely for press cameras while massive sacks of government rice and heavy cartons of medical supplies were dramatically unloaded behind him.
It was Gourab Sarkar.
He was noticeably younger in the footage, slightly heavier in the face, his dark hair immaculately styled against the backdrop of a disaster zone. He stood among subservient local officials and loyal party workers with the effortless, golden confidence of a man who had never expected his virtue to be questioned. He shook hands vigorously with exhausted volunteers, placed his manicured palm upon the wet head of a frightened child for a photograph, and warmly embraced an elderly, weeping woman while journalists crowded around him, their flashes illuminating his benevolent smile. Behind his broad shoulder hung a massive, professionally printed banner proudly announcing the state distribution of emergency flood-relief materials.
Trapped in the tower, Gourab watched himself smiling on the screen. He did not seem to recognise the confident, powerful man in the footage, his eyes wide and hollow. Or perhaps he recognised him far too well, the memory of his own hypocrisy paralysing him.
The footage changed with a sharp cut. The exact same relief camp was shown again, recorded several days later when the press cameras had moved on. The pristine white canopy remained standing in the mud. The political banners remained tied to the poles. The desperate, starving people remained huddled in the rain. But the heavy sacks of rice were entirely gone. The cartons of vital medicine were gone. The transport trucks were gone. A skeletal woman was shown arguing frantically with an indifferent relief worker who simply turned his back on her. A young child sat barefoot in the freezing mud, scratching relentlessly at an infected insect bite on his severely swollen leg. An old man lay shivering beneath a thin piece of torn plastic beside a flooded road, dying of exposure. An entire family crowded together beneath a collapsing school veranda because their house had disappeared completely beneath the brown water, and the promised salvation had vanished as quickly as the politicians.
The massive screens changed again, abandoning the human tragedy for the cold violence of bureaucracy. This time, there were highly classified documents. Procurement ledgers. Time-stamped photographs of overflowing private warehouses. Forged invoices. Massive offshore bank transfers. Detailed lists of devastated villages. Forged delivery signatures. Staggering quantities of grain and antibiotics supposedly delivered to places where the previous footage clearly showed people waiting for supplies that had never physically arrived. Transport trucks appeared in covert photographs leaving government warehouses in the dead of night, followed immediately by official records falsely showing that they had reached rural destinations they had never even driven toward. There were dates. There were names. There were offshore account numbers.
And at the bottom of the most damning ledger, rendered in perfect, undeniable clarity, was Gourab's sprawling signature.
Gourab stared at the overwhelming evidence with the rigid, terrifying concentration of a man desperately searching the screen for one single missing detail—a forged date, a misspelt name—that might somehow make the entire prosecution false.
"No," he whispered into the damp air, his voice trembling violently.
Absolutely nobody looked at him.
The screens flashed to another isolated village. This time, the footage was recorded at night. A heavy rescue vehicle stood idling on a submerged, ruined road, its bright headlights illuminating a small, crumbling house whose roof had almost disappeared entirely beneath the rushing water. Several desperate people stood balanced on top of it, packed tightly together to survive the freezing rain. A woman was holding something wrapped in cloth tightly against her chest, her face buried in the fabric. The camera slowly, mercilessly zoomed in, cutting through the dark. It was a child, completely still, its small arm hanging limply toward the rising water. The tragic image froze on the screen, burning into the retinas of the captives.
Beneath the frozen image, a line of stark white text appeared.
RELIEF ALLOCATED.
A second line appeared directly beneath it, flashing like a heartbeat.
RELIEF DELIVERED: NO.
Gourab suddenly twisted violently toward the nearest camera lens, his eyes wide with a frantic, animalistic desperation.
"You don't know anything!"
His voice was no longer the deep, commanding voice of the confident politician from the archival footage. It had become thin, reedy, and thoroughly terrified, stripping him of all his remaining dignity.
The television screens snapped to pitch black. For a terrible, suspended moment, the prisoners saw their own ghastly reflections mirrored in the dark glass. Twenty frightened, ruined faces stared silently back at them.
Then, the lighting inside the tower violently changed. The harsh, white industrial lights suspended high above them flickered once and died completely. A fraction of a second later, amber emergency lamps came alive along the rusted walls, followed immediately by a deeper, blood-red illumination that seemed to seep slowly through the concrete chamber rather than emanate from any visible source. The entire vertical cage was entirely submerged in a suffocating crimson glow. Wet, terrified eyes appeared pitch black in the new light. Teeth flashed stark white whenever somebody opened their mouth to scream. The thick metal bars cast long, stretching vertical shadows across the faces of the prisoners, making each of them look exactly as though they were already buried behind the iron bars of a mass grave.
The deep, mechanical sound beneath the concrete floor suddenly shifted in pitch. It became significantly louder, transforming from a patient groan into an aggressive, rushing roar.
Then came the unmistakable, terrifying rush of pressurised water.
For one agonising second, absolutely nobody moved. A thin, dark line of moisture appeared between two massive metal plates directly beneath their suspended feet. The line widened with a sharp hiss. Black water began forcefully pushing through the widening seams in the floor. It appeared thick and obsidian under the harsh red light, carrying a foul, stomach-turning stench of river mud, oxidised rust, raw sewage, and something infinitely older and more decayed that none of them dared to identify. The freezing liquid spread rapidly around their expensive shoes, the temperature so shockingly cold that it made several people gasp for air. Within seconds, the rising tide covered their ankles.
Someone near the lower tier screamed in pure panic.
The dark water rose violently. It erupted through the concrete floor in several places at once, forcing itself upward with such immense hydraulic pressure that freezing droplets struck their bare legs like thrown gravel. People stumbled backwards against their iron restraints, the water soaking into their trousers and tearing at their balance. Someone began pounding their fists frantically on the iron bars of their cage. Another prisoner threw his entire body weight against a sealed metal door, screaming for help until his vocal cords tore and his voice broke into a pathetic wheeze.
The water rapidly reached their knees. There was absolutely nowhere to run. The towering chamber that had seemed so enormously vast when they first awoke suddenly appeared horribly, claustrophobically small. The dark ceiling seemed to lower. The rusted walls seemed to close in on them. Through the rising chaos, every single camera remained fully functional, their tiny red lights glowing steadily, bearing silent witness to the drowning. Someone on the upper tier began praying aloud, their voice cracking with hysteria.
The freezing water reached their thighs. Then, their waists.
The cold became an intense, biting physical pain that seized their muscles. Heavy, saturated clothing clung desperately to their shivering bodies, dragging at them like lead weights every time they attempted to move. The dark water carried unseen bits of sharp, floating debris that scraped violently against their legs. Something soft and unidentifiable brushed heavily against one prisoner's ankle beneath the surface, and he shrieked in terror, kicking violently at the water until a man beside him shouted furiously at him to stop wasting his energy.
"Find the exit!"
"There is no fucking exit!"
"Break the bars! Break them!"
"How? With what?"
"Just do something!"
The filthy water reached their chests, the immense pressure squeezing the breath from their lungs.
Then, the concrete floor violently moved.
A deep, deafening metallic concussion travelled upward through the flooded chamber, vibrating through the water and shaking their teeth. One massive section of the flooded floor suddenly split apart with a horrific grinding noise, and something enormous began rising slowly through the churning black water. The terrified prisoners instantly fell silent, paralysed by the sheer scale of the machinery. A massive, black metal structure emerged slowly from beneath the surface, shedding torrents of water. Then a second structure breached the surface. Then a third. Then a fourth.
They rose one after another in a crooked, terrifying vertical arrangement, each piece broad and curved at the end like the heavy, iron hoof of some gigantic, mechanical beast. The foul water poured continuously from their rusted surfaces as they locked violently into place above one another, forming a grotesque, staggered staircase that climbed sharply toward a dark, open passage high above the flooding chamber. Nobody understood the architecture immediately.
Then someone at the front shouted, "Up! Climb up!"
The flooded room instantly exploded into a frenzy of violent movement. The iron restraints binding their ankles and wrists suddenly clicked open, releasing them into the rising tide. They surged forward toward the mechanical staircase, climbing desperately over one another in a state of absolute, blind panic. The rusted metal was incredibly slick beneath their bleeding hands. The water was already cresting above their stomachs, and every passing second seemed to aggressively shrink the distance between the black surface and their gasping mouths. People who had spent their entire privileged lives carefully stepping around one another now violently clawed and trampled over their peers. A man shoved another prisoner brutally aside to secure a handhold. The displaced prisoner fell backwards into the freezing water with a splash, dragged beneath the surface before two others reluctantly pulled him back up. Someone was crying hysterically. Someone was laughing in pure madness. Someone was screaming a prayer to a god that had long since abandoned the room.
And then, Rajiv fell.
His shattered, bullet-torn arm had been hanging uselessly at his side since Karam's horrific first demonstration. The makeshift, bloody bandage they had wrapped around it had become completely saturated with the foul water, and the wound had begun bleeding profusely again, leaving a dark crimson trail in his wake. His face had lost every ounce of its colour, replaced by a sickly, grey pallor. He tried desperately to haul his heavy frame up the steep metal hoof with his one good arm, but his traumatised body could no longer coordinate the massive effort required.
"I can't," he gasped, his fingers slipping from the iron.
He said it quietly, almost apologetically, the arrogance entirely drained from his soul. The freezing water had already reached his ribs.
One prisoner scrambling up the stairs looked back at him. Then another paused. There was a terrible, suffocating hesitation that lasted only a few seconds, but those brief seconds contained the ugliest, most primal calculation any of them had ever been forced to make. If they stopped to haul his dead weight, they might easily lose their own fragile chance to climb before the water consumed them. If they left him behind, he would undoubtedly drown in the dark. Nobody knew which choice was morally correct because the brutal architecture of the tower had instantly reduced human morality to cold, unforgiving arithmetic: one person's life weighed directly against another person's life, measured entirely in fading seconds.
Finally, with a grunt of exertion, two men turned around on the staircase and reached down.
"Give me your hand, damn it!"
Rajiv tried to lunge upward. His knees buckled entirely beneath the surface. The two men caught him roughly beneath the shoulders, their fingers digging into his soaked suit, and violently hauled him toward the metal edge. Another prisoner reached down from a higher rung and seized his wrist to stabilise him. Rajiv cried out in agony when his shattered arm struck the unyielding metal step, but absolutely nobody let go. They dragged his heavy body upward, step by agonising step, while the black water climbed relentlessly around their waists. For the very first time since waking in the rusted tower, they were acting together as a single unit. It was not because they suddenly trusted one another. It was not because they had forgiven the crimes that had brought them here. It was certainly not because the terror had suddenly made them good people. They were simply beginning to understand, with a chilling clarity, that if they remained entirely alone, none of them would survive the night.
Then, from the darkness below, somebody screamed Gourab's name.
He was still trapped below the surface level of the stairs. He had been violently separated from the rest of the group during the initial, panicked rush and was now pinned heavily against the iron bars on the opposite side of the flooded chamber. The powerful current was too strong for him to cross. The freezing black water had already reached his chest.
"Help me!"
His desperate voice echoed pathetically through the iron cage. Nobody on the staircase moved to go back down. The water was rising too fast. Gourab gripped the rusted bars with both of his hands, his knuckles turning white.
"Please!"
He looked frantically upward toward the escaping group. For one agonising, suspended moment, his wide eyes found Ashish's looking down from the staircase. Ashish froze, staring back at the drowning man. Something profound and terrible passed between them in that brief second, something that neither man possessed the vocabulary to name. Gourab was staring at him, asking him for the same thing that thousands of desperate, flooding victims had once asked men like him for: help, a simple intervention, an order, a vehicle, just a fraction of the immense power that wealthy men always possessed until the exact moment they desperately needed it to save themselves.
Ashish took one hesitant step downward toward the water.
Then, the tide violently surged again, splashing against his knees.
"Come on!" somebody shouted urgently from the top of the stairs, pulling at Ashish's shoulder.
Ashish looked back up at the safety of the dark passage. He looked back down at Gourab. There was absolutely no accusation left in Gourab’s face anymore. That absence of anger was somehow infinitely worse. He simply looked like a terrified, helpless child.
"Please," Gourab whispered over the roar of the flood.
Ashish stopped moving. He hardened his jaw, turned his back, and continued climbing. The moment of salvation passed, lost to the rising tide.
Gourab let go of the bars and tried desperately to swim across the gap. The violent current immediately seized his heavy body and threw him violently back against the iron cage. His head struck the solid metal with a sickening crack. He cried out in pain, swallowing foul water, and clung to the cage, gasping desperately for breath. His wet fingers slipped on the iron, found the bars again, and slipped once more.
Then, he looked down at his own hands. His expression changed from terror to absolute, uncomprehending confusion.
The prisoners clinging to the stairs above him watched in mounting horror as Gourab’s fingers began to aggressively swell. At first, the physiological change was subtle, almost imperceptible beneath the bloody red light of the emergency lamps. Then, it accelerated with a terrifying, unnatural speed. The joints in his fingers thickened drastically. The skin stretched violently across his knuckles until it became pale, translucent, and unnaturally smooth. His hands rapidly lost their natural human shape, bloating into grotesque, swollen masses. The dark veins beneath the stretching skin rose darkly to the surface, looking exactly like black roots trapped beneath translucent, dying earth.
"What is happening to me?"
Gourab sounded genuinely, childishly confused. He raised one bloated, trembling hand toward his face. The fingers shuddered violently, unable to bend.
Then, the aggressive swelling moved rapidly up into his palms and his wrists.
He screamed. The sound was not a theatrical expression of fear. It was the raw, guttural, involuntary cry of a human being whose own physical body had suddenly become a terrifying, unfamiliar prison. He struck his swollen arm frantically against the iron bars, trying to beat the sensation away, then immediately cried out again in agony and cradled the bloated limb tightly against his chest.
"Help me! God, help me!"
Nobody on the stairs answered him. They could only watch. His forearms continued to enlarge rapidly, reacting to whatever chemical compound had been laced into the freezing black water. The skin stretched tighter and tighter, threatening to burst. A sickening, dark purple discolouration spread rapidly beneath the surface of his skin. Gourab began beating both of his grossly swollen arms against the metal bars as though trying to forcefully expel the searing pain from his flesh, his breathing becoming frantic and shallow.
The rising water finally reached his broad shoulders. He tried desperately to climb the bars to escape the tide, but his bloated, misshapen fingers could no longer grip the iron. He tried again, screaming in frustration. They slipped uselessly. He looked one final time upward. The mechanical staircase was crowded with the others, staring down at him in horrified silence. Absolutely no one came back for him. And perhaps that was the final, devastating psychological punishment Karam had meticulously prepared for him: not merely the physical agony of drowning, but the crushing, undeniable understanding of exactly what it meant to be abandoned by the people who had the power to save you.
The foul water reached his trembling chin. Gourab began sobbing, his tears mixing with the flood.
"I'll give it back."
Nobody above him understood what he was saying.
"The money. I'll give all of it back."
He was no longer speaking to the prisoners. He was looking directly into the glowing red lenses of the cameras, begging the machine.
"I'll tell them everything. I swear to God, I'll tell them."
The water rose ruthlessly over his mouth, cutting off his desperate confession. His massive, swollen arms floated uselessly beside him.
Then, the stretched skin of his right forearm violently split open. A wet, horrific tearing sound echoed through the chamber, disappearing almost immediately beneath the deafening roar of the churning water. Gourab stared down at his own ruptured arm in absolute disbelief, his mouth opening soundlessly to scream, but only water rushed in. He did not fight this time. His face had become strangely, terrifyingly vacant, as though his conscious mind had fully retreated to a quiet place where his dying body could no longer reach him. The black water rose over his lips, filling his lungs. His wide, vacant eyes remained above the surface for three long seconds, staring directly up at the survivors.
Then, even his eyes disappeared beneath the black surface, leaving only a gentle swirl of bubbles in his wake.
The towering machinery continued to breathe, groaning beneath the floor. The terrified survivors continued to climb. Absolutely nobody spoke a single word while they hauled themselves up the steep, rusted incline. Their hands slipped repeatedly on the wet metal, and their knees struck the jagged edges of the iron structures hard enough to deeply bruise the bone, but nobody slowed down. Rajiv had become almost entirely dead weight, dependent upon the frantic pulling of the others. His injured arm hung uselessly against his side, moving with the heavy, sickening sway of something that no longer fully belonged to a living body. Twice he slipped on the grates. Twice, someone instantly caught him by the collar and dragged him upward. Nobody complained about the burden. They had finally begun to understand the ruthless rules of the tower.
The dark opening high above them grew gradually larger. Finally, the first desperate prisoner pulled himself over the iron lip and collapsed into the passageway. The others quickly followed, hauling themselves over the threshold. Rajiv was dragged roughly over the edge and lowered onto the cold concrete floor. Several people collapsed instantly beside him, coughing violently, freezing water pouring from their ruined clothes. One woman crawled to the corner and vomited foul river water onto the floor. Another man sat rigidly against the wall with his knees pulled tightly to his chest, staring at absolutely nothing.
Nobody felt remotely victorious. They had successfully escaped the water, but the escape did not feel like survival. It felt entirely like a brief, cruel postponement of their execution.
Deep below them, the heavy machinery shifted gears again. The water began to rapidly drain. Slowly at first, and then with a frightening, violent suction, the black surface descended rapidly through the grates in the floor. The exhausted prisoners remained exactly where they were, too physically drained to move, listening to the deafening, sucking roar echoing below them. When the rushing water had almost completely disappeared, one of the men found the strength to crawl slowly toward the edge of the opening and look down into the crimson-lit cage. The others slowly followed, peering over the iron lip.
Gourab's ruined body remained.
It had been forcefully carried by the receding water toward the heavy iron bars and left there, twisted unnaturally against them. His expensive, tailored clothing clung heavily to his bloated frame. His arms were horribly, monstrously swollen, the skin ruptured in several places, his hands no longer remotely resembling human hands. His head was turned partly toward the concrete wall, as though even in the absolute finality of death, he had been desperately trying not to look at the people who had abandoned him.
The prisoners stared down in profound, crushing silence. Nobody spoke his name aloud. Somewhere in the dark chamber behind them, a woman began to cry. She did not weep loudly, and there was no theatrical, dramatic grief in the sound. She simply sat slumped against the cold wall and covered her face with her trembling hands, her shoulders shaking with small, involuntary tremors. The others completely avoided looking at her because they understood exactly what she was feeling. They had watched a man die in agony. They had watched him beg for salvation. And they had consciously, deliberately left him there to drown. The moral distinction between murder and mere survival had never seemed so terrifyingly thin.
Rajiv looked down at Gourab's bloated, ruined corpse, and then he slowly looked down at his own bleeding, injured arm. His face had fundamentally changed. The aggressive arrogance was completely gone. The violent anger had evaporated. He simply looked like a deeply frightened, broken man who had finally understood that whatever horrific trials happened next, he would absolutely not be able to face them alone.
A television monitor mounted high on the wall of their new chamber suddenly switched on with a sharp hiss. Karam appeared on the screen. His face remained tightly wrapped in the yellowed bandages. Only his dark, unblinking eyes were visible.
"You helped one another."
Nobody in the room answered him. Karam waited patiently, letting the silence stretch.
"You did not do it because you suddenly became good people."
His voice was almost unnervingly gentle, carrying the tone of a disappointed teacher.
"You did it because you quickly discovered that your fragile lives now depend entirely upon people you can no longer control."
The screen behind him flashed, instantly displaying a high-angle camera feed of Gourab's ruined body twisted against the bars below them. Several prisoners immediately turned their faces away in disgust. Karam continued speaking smoothly over the gruesome image.
"That is a vital lesson that powerful people usually only learn after they have already destroyed someone else's life."
The image behind him changed once again. The outside world abruptly appeared on the screen. The prisoners saw massive, brightly lit newsrooms filled with people shouting frantically into telephones. They saw senior police officers standing tensely around banks of glowing monitors. They saw powerful government officials crowded into briefing rooms where the exact same footage of their flooded cage played continuously on a loop. Reporters were speaking breathlessly into cameras outside local police stations, while massive crowds gathered outside television studios, demanding answers. High-resolution faces of the prisoners appeared vividly on screens across the entire country.
Gourab Sarkar. Ashish Roy. Rajiv Agarwal.
Their names were being spoken aloud by millions of absolute strangers. Their entire private lives were being ruthlessly dissected and debated in real-time on national television. Their glossy, curated photographs were being displayed side-by-side with old, buried newspaper articles, political speeches, corporate announcements, exclusive charity events, and photographs of people who had never imagined that the entire world would one day be staring at them in connection with a locked concrete room somewhere they could not find. And beneath all of the frantic news coverage remained the undeniable, live image of the brutalist tower. The terrified prisoners. The bloated dead man. The unblinking cameras.
The entire world was watching their destruction.
Karam looked directly into his own camera lens, his dark eyes seeming to pierce through the monitor and into the chamber.
"You have been watched your entire lives."
His eyes seemed almost completely expressionless.
"You simply never noticed until now."
The massive screen snapped to black.
Far away from the absolute terror of the tower, convoys of heavily armed police vehicles were already tearing blindly through the night. Tactical teams aggressively breached abandoned factories, empty warehouses, unfinished apartment towers, decaying industrial compounds, and isolated government buildings. Encrypted digital signals had been traced to false locations deliberately designed to waste their time. Registered addresses led heavily armed men into empty rooms. GPS coordinates ended in barren fields, flooded drainage channels, and dilapidated buildings that had been abandoned for years. Every apparent, celebrated breakthrough inevitably became another carefully, meticulously prepared deception by a mind that had anticipated their every move.
Inside the freezing concrete tower, the surviving prisoners sat closely together, shivering in their ruined clothes. For the very first time since they had awakened, they were not talking frantically about escape. They were talking in hushed, terrified whispers about Karam. They were talking about exactly how he knew. It was not merely their names or their public professions that terrified them. It was the fact that he knew them. He had known exactly what they had hidden in the dark. He knew precisely which corrupt decisions had hurt innocent people. He knew exactly where the dirty money had moved, which official records had conveniently disappeared, which federal investigations had been softened, which powerful institutions had been manipulated, which guilty people had been protected, and which innocent people had been brutally abandoned to die.
He knew all the terrible things they had aggressively convinced themselves that nobody remembered. And that absolute, total knowledge produced a completely different, deeper kind of psychological fear. A strong man could survive a locked door. He could survive intense physical pain. He could even survive the horrific sight of another person dying in front of him. But how did a man possibly survive the crushing, paralysing knowledge that somewhere, in some unseen room located entirely beyond the reach of every police officer and politician in the country, another human being had spent years meticulously studying the private architecture of his sins?
Rajiv slowly lowered his heavy head between his knees, entirely defeated. Ashish remained perfectly still, his eyes drifting upward to look at the mechanical cameras mounted on the walls. One of the small red lights blinked. Then another. Then another. For a long, terrifying moment in the dark, Ashish imagined that Karam was not merely watching them through those distant, cold lenses, but sitting somewhere intimately in the darkness directly beyond them, listening to their uneven breathing, studying the terrified expressions on their faces, and waiting patiently for the exact moment when their fear finally mutated into something much deeper.
The heavy iron doors sealed shut behind them with a definitive, echoing finality, abruptly severing the deafening roar of the receding floodwater. The nineteen shivering survivors found themselves standing in a dimly lit corridor that smelled sharply of industrial antiseptic, a sterile chemical odour that briefly offered a profound, pathetic illusion of comfort. It belonged to a reality they fundamentally understood, evoking the sprawling government hospitals of their past, with their endless tiled corridors, rusted metal beds shoved against stained walls, and exhausted relatives sleeping directly on the cold floor beneath concrete staircases. It was a scent deeply associated with human mortality, but also, at least within the desperate imagination of an arriving patient, with the distinct possibility of salvation. After the suffocating darkness of the water and the horrifying sight of Gourab Sarkar’s bloated corpse twisting against the iron grates, several of the prisoners instinctively wanted to believe they had been delivered somewhere safer. They desperately wanted to believe they had entered a sanctuary governed by recognisable medical rules, a place where a damaged human body might still be treated as something inherently worth preserving.
Then the secondary scent reached them, lying buried beneath the sharp bite of the antiseptic, lingering as though someone had frantically attempted to clean a fundamentally rotten surface. It was the distinct, nauseating stench of damp plaster, oxidising iron, black mould, stale urine, dried sweat, and a faint, sickly sweetness of necrotic decay that seemed to have seeped permanently into the foundational concrete. The stagnant air carried the cold, metallic odour of surgical instruments that had been utilised far too many times and the sour, suffocating smell of floor drains that had not been flushed in decades. Somewhere in the impenetrable darkness beyond the immediate ward, heavy drops of water fell at regular intervals onto a resonant metal surface, producing a hollow, echoing sound that travelled through the blackness and returned as a distorted reverberation. The dripping rhythm was so mathematically precise that it quickly began to resemble the ticking of a grandfather clock. Once that terrifying thought took root in their traumatised minds, several of the captives found themselves listening to it involuntarily, counting the hollow spaces between the drops as though time itself had already begun measuring the remainder of their lives.
The dark corridor eventually opened into a vast, cavernous hospital ward that initially appeared to have been completely abandoned for decades. The high walls were covered with peeling, jaundiced paint and old sea-green tiles, many of which were cracked violently down the middle, while heavy ceiling fans hung entirely motionless beneath thick layers of grey dust. Government public health posters had faded so severely that the smiling doctors and healthy children printed upon them seemed almost spectral, their cheerful faces bleached away by years of fluorescent light and total neglect. Yet, despite the overwhelming decay, there was something fundamentally wrong with the massive room, a sickening detail the prisoners sensed long before their exhausted minds could properly identify it. The grim ward was entirely abandoned, but it had also been meticulously prepared for their arrival.
The floor of the vast hospital ward possessed the terrifying, sterile neatness of a laboratory aggressively scrubbed with harsh chemicals immediately before an imminent experiment. There was absolutely no dust hiding beneath the iron beds, no discarded medical paper, no dead insects, and no footprints upon the tiles except their own wet tracks. There were exactly nineteen beds standing rigidly in two perfectly parallel rows beneath the flickering fluorescent tubes. They were narrow iron frames covered tightly with immaculate, blindingly white sheets, and beside every single bed stood a small, wheeled metal table. Upon every metal table lay the exact same arrangement of chilling objects: a neatly folded white letter, a polished stainless steel surgical tray, a freshly sterilised scalpel, and a sealed electronic biohazard container equipped with a glowing digital scanner. Absolutely nothing had been left to casual chance. As the shivering prisoners slowly counted the narrow beds, the devastating reality of the mathematics settled heavily into their chests. Nineteen of them had survived the flooded cage, and exactly nineteen immaculate beds had been prepared for their flesh.
Before anyone could voice their rising terror, the massive television monitor mounted high upon the tiled wall clicked on with a sharp hiss, and Karam’s masked face instantly filled the screen. His head remained tightly wrapped in the yellowed medical bandages, his posture completely relaxed, and his voice perfectly controlled, contrasting horribly with the shivering, bleeding prisoners staring up at him.
"Welcome to the hospital," Karam’s voice echoed through the hidden speakers, perfectly calm and terrifyingly human. "This is exactly where ordinary people come when their fragile bodies finally fail them. Sometimes, they are fortunate enough to be treated, but more often, they die in these very corridors because there is absolutely no bed available for them. Sometimes, there is a bed, but there is absolutely no medicine in the cabinets. Sometimes, there is plenty of medicine, but nobody on the staff cares enough to administer it to them. Healthcare became a highly lucrative business, and human illness became a wonderful financial opportunity."
The dark screen behind Karam’s head flashed brightly, replacing his bandaged face with crisp, high-resolution close-up portraits of each prisoner, accompanied by highly sensitive medical information displayed directly beneath their faces. Blood groups, known chronic medical conditions, daily prescribed medications, severe allergies, and previous surgical histories scrolled relentlessly across the monitor.
"I know exactly who among you suffers from severe asthma," Karam said softly, his voice drifting over the damning profiles. "I know who hides their diabetes. I know who has a dangerously weak heart. I know who takes vital medication every single morning, and I know exactly who lies to their spouses about taking it. Your arrogance made you believe you were bringing your powerful secrets into this building. You merely brought your bodies."
The massive screens changed once more, splitting into a grid that showed a live camera feed of one of the nineteen immaculate beds. One specific name hovered over each bed, and directly beneath each glowing name was a single, horrifying anatomical instruction. Karam explained the brutal rules without any theatrical flourishes: each prisoner had been assigned a particular, vital piece of their own physical body that had to be physically severed and placed immediately upon the stainless steel surgical tray. The automated hospital system would electronically verify the tissue mass and chemically compare the fresh blood to the database. Once the machine finally accepted the bloody sacrifice, the corresponding heavy door at the end of the ward would unlock.
"You have approximately thirty minutes," Karam concluded, as the heavy iron vents near the ceiling audibly cranked open with a sickening grind, releasing a silent, invisible flow of carbon monoxide into the room. A massive, glowing digital clock appeared on the monitors, the red numbers instantly falling backwards from thirty minutes. "Try to survive."
The television feed shut off with a sharp click, and for several agonising seconds, absolutely nobody in the ward moved a muscle. Then, the hospital ward completely erupted into absolute madness as heavy, mechanised iron bolts slammed violently into place around the perimeter of the room, sealing the heavy exit doors with a rapid series of metallic impacts. The invisible carbon monoxide began to take its toll almost immediately, producing a rapidly growing tightness deep in their chests and a sudden, sharp headache pulsing directly behind their eyes. People ripped open their assigned letters and immediately began hiding the contents from their peers, horrified by the specific demands Karam had placed upon their flesh. Some read their instructions twice, their minds entirely unable to accept what was being demanded, while one prisoner began laughing uncontrollably in the corner, his fragile mind finally reaching the breaking point where pure terror became entirely indistinguishable from hysteria.
