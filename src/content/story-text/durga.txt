Some people become fatally ill without anybody ever knowing. They continue to commute to work, answer mundane questions, cook their evening meals, sit quietly beside their families, and manufacture flawless, hollow smiles whenever a camera is raised. Their physical bodies remain mechanically obedient to the gruelling demands of the day, even while something dark, invisible, and malignant grows quietly inside them, slowly devouring their architecture from the inside out. By the time anyone finally notices the rot, the person they thought they knew may have been dead for years. Durga had become exactly such a person. She had painstakingly learned how to camouflage profound, breaking suffering as mere tiredness, how to lower her eyes at precisely the right moment to avoid triggering wrath, how to keep her voice perfectly steady when everything inside her chest was fracturing, and how to move silently through the rooms of her own house as though she were merely a ghost haunting someone else’s life.
That afternoon, the crushing weight of her existence had finally simply become too heavy for her biology to sustain. It was three o'clock, and the oppressive, suffocating heat of the city pressed relentlessly against the glass of the apartment windows. She had spent the entire morning and early afternoon scrubbing the floors, washing the heavy linens by hand, and preparing the evening meals, her body moving on a fading reserve of adrenaline and fear. Exhausted beyond the limits of human endurance, she had collapsed onto the hard floor beside the bed, lacking the strength to even crawl onto the mattress. She had been blindly flipping through the glossy pages of a lifestyle magazine, her eyes failing to register the bright, smiling women modelling clothes she would never be permitted to wear. She did not fall asleep peacefully. Her body simply failed her. The magazine slipped from her numb fingers, her vision swam into a sickening, narrowing tunnel of black, and she fainted, her cheek striking the cold floorboards as darkness swallowed her completely.
When consciousness began to slowly, agonisingly return, the suffocating heat of the afternoon had bled away into the bruised, heavy purples and greys of dusk.
She had lain unconscious on the floor for hours. The bedroom was cloaked in deep, creeping shadows, smelling faintly of damp, ageing wood, extinguished jasmine incense, and the lingering, stale ghost of the perfume she had worn days ago. Outside, the sprawling city was transitioning into the chaotic noise of evening, but inside the sealed room, every microscopic sound acquired an exaggerated, terrifying importance. A bathroom tap dripped somewhere in the dark, sounding like a ticking clock measuring out the remainder of her life.
Someone touched her shoulder.
"Durga."
She stirred, her mind heavy and disoriented from the blackout.
The hand touched her again, gentler this time. "Durga."
She opened her heavy eyes. Raj was sitting on the floor beside her.
She looked at him for a long, agonising moment without speaking, terrified that a single breath would shatter the fragile illusion. His face seemed perfectly, wonderfully ordinary, almost unbearably so in its comforting familiarity. His dark hair was longer than she remembered, falling untidily near his ears, and he wore the faded olive polo shirt she had once loved so much—the shirt she had once sprayed with her own favourite perfume just before he left her apartment, desperately marking him as hers. There was something profoundly tender in his expression, a deep, sorrowful affection that made her broken heart ache terribly before she could even consciously understand why he was looking at her with such pity.
"You collapsed," he murmured, his voice a soothing balm against her fractured mind.
Durga looked down at the crumpled glossy pages of the magazine resting beneath her cheek. "I was reading."
"You pushed yourself too hard."
"I was just resting."
"I know.
There was a devastating, raw tenderness in his quiet voice that made her desperately want to weep until her lungs gave out. Then, a dark, jagged fragment of memory pierced the warmth. Her smile vanished instantly, replaced by a creeping, icy dread that seemed to freeze the blood in her veins.

"In the magazine. On the page. I saw him.

Raj turned his head and looked toward the billowing curtains. "Did he speak?"
"He called me."
"What did he say?"
"I can't remember." Durga rubbed her forehead, pressing her fingers into her skin as if trying to force the memory out of her skull. The truth seemed to exist both there and not there, distorting like a corpse viewed through deep, muddy water. "I remember his voice."
"What did it sound like?"
She looked back at Raj, her eyes wide with mounting, suffocating panic. "I don't know."
He reached out and took her hand. His palm was impossibly warm, anchoring her to the floorboards. "Don't be frightened."
"I am."
"You're here, with me."
She squeezed his fingers with a desperate, crushing grip. "Stay with me."

The man was standing there.
He was balanced precariously at the very edge of the concrete railing, silhouetted against the dying light of the city. Durga stopped breathing. The evening had become completely dark outside, but she could see the outline of his body clearly illuminated against the distant, blurred streetlights. He wore an olive shirt. His long hair moved wildly in the rising breeze. One hand rested loosely at his side, almost casually, as though he had been standing on the precipice for a lifetime, simply waiting for her to finally notice him.
"Raj," she breathed, the name tearing at her throat.
The man on the ledge slowly lifted his head. She could not make out his facial features in the gloom. Yet, in the very marrow of her bones, she knew him. The horrifying certainty arrived a fraction of a second before the actual visual recognition.
"Don't go."

"Durga," the man beside her whispered, but she did not hear him. She was entirely focused on the figure teetering upon the edge of the balcony.

The man looked directly at her. For one terrible, illuminated instant, his face became clear in the glow of a passing headlight below. It was Raj.
She ran toward the open doors.
He stepped backwards into the empty air.

She could see absolutely everything with the terrible, magnified clarity of a waking nightmare: the unnatural, broken angle of his neck, one arm twisted grotesquely beneath his torso, the dark, almost black blood spreading slowly into the dry cracks between the paving stones, and one lone shoe resting several feet away from the wreckage. His ruined face was turned partly upward toward her, and from that dizzying height, she could not tell whether his dead eyes were open.
Then, the people appeared.
They emerged from the surrounding shadows of the courtyard like insects crawling from the walls, gathering around his shattered body in a rapidly widening circle. Durga recognised every single upturned face. She knew them all so completely that she felt she ought to immediately remember their names, their voices, the precise circumstances in which they had met. At her.

Raj's protective arms were still wrapped tightly around her, his hand resting securely against the back of her head. Wood.

She stared at her own white knuckles without immediate comprehension. Her cheek was pressed painfully against the cold, hard surface of the floorboards, her fingers locked around the polished wood as though she had been terrified the earth itself would drag her away. For several disorienting seconds, she remained frozen exactly like that, still half-submerged inside the phantom warmth of the vision. Then, the brutal, sterile truth entered her slowly, like a paralytic poison moving through a vein. The warmth beneath her cheek was entirely gone. There was no olive shirt beneath her desperate fingers, no gentle hand moving through her hair, no familiar, comforting breath against her ear. The dark room swam sickeningly before her eyes. "Durga!"

He carried himself with the terrifying, effortless authority of a man who was utterly accustomed to wielding absolute power without consequence, and there was something almost obscene, deeply sociopathic, in the pristine perfection of his appearance compared to the broken, terrified woman cowering before him.

Durga instantly lowered her eyes, a trained animal submitting to its master. "I'll make it."

He told her in graphic, sickening detail that Raj was dead. He told her exactly how he had pushed him, how the man had looked as he plummeted toward the concrete. He told her that nobody in the world would ever know the truth unless he personally permitted them to know it. He reminded her, over and over, that men like him were completely insulated from consequence—protected by iron-clad uniforms, powerful offices, and respected names that appeared in the heroic columns of daily newspapers. Absolutely nothing was said, and the heavy, complicit silence between the three of them seemed to contain a horrific, suffocating truth that nobody in this world would ever dare to name.
After a while, the maid stopped mopping, wrung out the sponge, and looked nervously toward the front door. "I'm leaving now."
Durga looked at her from the floor. The woman waited, shifting uncomfortably, perhaps expecting some kind of answer or permission, but Durga did not know what she could say to a woman who would step over her bleeding body to keep her job. Desperate questions were screaming somewhere inside her, but they had become so tangled with profound exhaustion and despair that she could no longer separate one from another. The maid quickly gathered her things, kept her head down, and left. A moment later, the front door clicked closed.

The pressure cooker suddenly whistled, a bright, shrill scream in the morning quiet, and the sound seemed almost sadistically cruel in its relentless normality. Durga remained exactly where she was on the floor until the whistle finally faded into a hiss. Then, she tried to stand.

Her trembling hand slipped against the bloody mattress, her knees buckled, and she sank heavily back down, closing her eyes tightly until the sickening dizziness passed. She waited for a long time, gathering whatever fractured pieces of her will remained, before trying again. On the second agonising attempt, she managed to haul herself upright, though blinding pain travelled immediately up through her bruised legs and settled heavily in her hips and lower back. She clung desperately to the wooden bedpost until the room finally stopped violently spinning.

There was dried, crusted blood smeared near her split mouth and a massive, dark purple contusion blooming dangerously along her cheekbone. Nitin's voice floated casually from the balcony, light and entirely unbothered. "Bring me tea."
She filled the heavy metal kettle. Her bruised hands were shaking so violently that she nearly dropped it as she placed it on the gas stove, and when the blue flame caught beneath it with a hollow rush of air, she stood completely paralysed for a moment without moving. The pressure cooker hissed angrily on the adjacent burner. Somewhere in the hallway behind her, the grandfather clock continued its patient, indifferent ticking. From the sunny balcony came the faint, relaxing rustle of the newspaper fluttering in the morning breeze.
Then, very softly, cutting through the smells of ginger and burned food, she smelled the perfume.
She turned around slowly.
Raj stood silently in the kitchen doorway.
He wore the faded olive polo shirt. His dark hair moved gently in the warm air drifting through the open window. He looked exactly as he had before the madness and the violence had been unleashed, before absolutely everything had been violently stolen from her: he was whole, calm, and devastatingly familiar. There was no blood upon him, no broken neck, no shattered body, no terrible balcony beneath his feet. There was only Raj—the gentle man she had loved with her whole soul, the man who had once held her as though the cruelty of the world could never possibly reach her.
Durga stared at him, her heart shattering all over again.
He did not speak. There was absolutely nothing frightening or ghostly in his warm face. For one aching, beautiful instant, she allowed herself to desperately believe that he had fought his way back from the grave simply to stand in her kitchen and remind her that somewhere in her ruined life, there had once been a place where she was safe.
She closed her swollen eyes, a single tear cutting a clean path through the dried blood on her cheek.
When she opened them, the doorway was empty.
The kettle began to shriek on the stove.
Durga numbly poured the boiling water, prepared the tea, and carried the steaming cup outside. Nitin sat comfortably in a wicker chair on the sunlit balcony with the newspaper spread open before him, the bright morning sunlight falling heroically across his crisp police uniform. He did not even bother to look up at her battered face when she placed the saucer delicately beside his hand.
"More sugar," he commanded flatly.
Durga picked the cup back up without answering. She returned to the dark kitchen, mechanically added the sugar, stirred it, and carried it outside again. Nitin took a sip without ever taking his eyes off the article he was reading, then casually turned another page.
Durga remained standing silently behind his chair.
Far below them, the massive city was waking up, entirely oblivious to the monster sitting on the balcony above. Crowded buses moved sluggishly along the main road, heavy metal shop shutters rose violently one after another, and somewhere in the distance, a bicycle bell rang brightly through the crisp morning air. A woman on a balcony directly across the street vigorously shook dust from a bright yellow cloth. A milkman called out a greeting to someone walking below. The massive, ordinary machinery of the day had begun turning again, perfectly indifferent to the blood that had been spilt, the lives that had been crushed, and the horrors that had occurred during the night.
Nitin lifted the newspaper slightly higher, blocking out the sun.
Durga looked at the broad expanse of his back, clad in the uniform that made him untouchable.
Then, she looked past him, at the low concrete railing of the balcony.
For one terrible, paralysing moment, she saw Raj falling all over again. She saw the hard stone courtyard rushing up to meet him, the dark blood spreading across it like a stain, and the wide circle of people standing mutely around his broken body, their faces horrifyingly familiar yet completely nameless, their fingers raised toward her in silent, eternal accusation. She could never remember who they were, because they were everyone. She only knew that they had been there, that they had watched, and that somewhere deep inside her fractured mind, they had become the unforgiving witnesses to a brutal murder for which she, the victim, had never forgiven herself.
She heard Nitin's sadistic voice echoing in her skull again.
I pushed him.
Her bruised hands closed slowly into tight fists at her sides.
Nitin casually turned another page of the paper, humming a quiet tune.
Durga looked at the back of his neck for the last time, the urge to push him surging and dying in her chest in a single breath, then turned away and walked back inside like a ghost. The kitchen waited for her exactly as it had before: the towering pile of dirty dishes, the burned food, the whistling kettle, the cold floor, and the pale, indifferent morning light falling across absolutely everything. She picked up the first ceramic plate and began to wash it under the cold tap, her movements slow, mechanical, and careful, as though she were performing some ancient, tragic ritual that had been handed down to her long before she ever understood what it truly meant to be a woman.
Behind her, Nitin sat comfortably beneath the warm morning sun and read his paper. Outside the balcony, the massive city continued to move, to work, to speak, to laugh, and to blindly admire the respectable, heroic man it firmly believed him to be.
Durga lowered her exhausted, bruised eyes to the water running endlessly over the plate in her hands.
For the moment, that was absolutely all there was left of her life: the cold water, the dirty dishes, the morning light, and the crushing, inescapable knowledge that tomorrow would ruthlessly ask the same things of her that today had demanded.
