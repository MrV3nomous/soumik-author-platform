By the end of his first week as the sole custodian of the parish, Father Daniel had begun to understand that an ancient church could make a man feel newly appointed twice: once by the bishop who formally handed him the iron keys, and again by the building itself, which seemed to silently accept or resist each new occupant according to some inscrutable, older measure of its own. The church stood at the upper end of Ballymore, a small parish town in County Clare where the narrow, uneven streets climbed gradually away from the market square before disappearing into low fields, crumbling stone walls, and the sweeping, grey Atlantic weather beyond them. It was not a wealthy place, nor had it ever been particularly prosperous, but it possessed the grim and unrelenting stubbornness of towns that had endured for generations without ever expecting the wider world to notice their existence. Rain came often to Ballymore, sometimes softly enough to be mistaken for an evening mist, and sometimes driving hard against the stained-glass windows and slate roofs, carrying with it the bitter smell of wet earth, coal smoke, and salt from the distant, churning coast.
The church itself sat brooding behind a low stone wall at the end of a street lined with ageing terraced houses, a grocer, a pharmacy, and two pubs whose windows glowed a sickly amber when the dark came early. It had been built more than a century before, though crude repairs and additions had been made so many times that its architecture no longer belonged neatly to any particular period of history. The imposing stone façade was darkened by decades of relentless rain, and the carved figures of saints above the entrance had lost the sharpness of their features beneath soot, severe weather, and the patient erosion of time—leaving them with smooth, featureless faces that seemed to gaze blindly into the street. Two iron lanterns hung on either side of the great wooden doors, both slightly crooked, though only one still possessed a working bulb. The local parishioners scarcely noticed any of it. They arrived in the damp mornings carrying umbrellas, shopping bags, school satchels, and the private, invisible burdens that people brought instinctively into houses of worship, and they left again with the very same hollow, ordinary expressions with which they had arrived.
Daniel had always felt a profound, almost possessive affinity for that enduring stillness. There was no grandeur left in the nave except the kind that belonged strictly to survival, the quiet dignity of a sanctuary that had remained standing because generations of ordinary people had continued to care for it without ever possessing quite enough money to keep it perfect. The heavy wooden pews had been polished so often by nervous human hands that their arms were smooth beneath the fading varnish, and the floorboards between them had acquired a dull sheen where thousands of desperate shoes had worn them down to the grain. The altar cloth had been mended more than once by elderly women with failing eyesight. One of the stained-glass windows along the eastern aisle carried a jagged hairline crack through the image of Saint Joseph; when the dying afternoon light found that particular pane, the crack passed violently through the saint's face, dividing it into two pale, severed halves. Behind the altar stood a heavy brass tabernacle whose true age Daniel had never managed to determine, while a stone niche beside it held a vase that somebody faithfully replaced with fresh flowers every Monday, entirely regardless of the decaying condition of anything else in the chapel.
It was not a beautiful church in the manner of those grand cathedrals that appeared in glossy photographs, where dustless shafts of golden sunlight fell through richly coloured glass and every worn surface seemed a deliberate choice of aesthetics. Its beauty was far less flattering and therefore, to Daniel, infinitely more convincing. It belonged to the kind of sacred place that had absorbed generations of desperate voices, whispered sins, and echoing footsteps without ever being asked to remember a single one of them. It was a space where people had wept bitterly beside strangers, married beneath the bloody feet of the same crucifix, buried their parents, baptised their squalling children, and returned years later carrying terrified children of their own. Daniel often mused that the church's immense age was not something one merely saw, but something one felt in the heavy, oppressive patience of its objects—in the way nothing in the sanctuary seemed hurried and nothing seemed surprised by the passing of another relentless year.
He had been ordained for more than fifteen years, a long and quiet tenure spent largely in the shadow of older priests, mastering the practical, unsavoury disciplines that were never taught properly in the pristine halls of the seminary. He had learned how to tell from the uneven sound of a woman's footsteps that she had been weeping long before she entered a room, how to listen in the confessional without making a terrified parishioner feel clinically examined, and how to persuade a devastated widower to eat when grief had turned the very concept of food into a physical nausea. He had mastered the art of remembering the names of children whose apathetic parents appeared only at Christmas, and how to repair the stubborn brass latch of an old confessional booth without humiliating himself before a sacristan half his age. Most of those essential things, the true psychological mechanics of shepherding a broken flock, he had learned directly from Father Michael.
Michael had ruled the parish for thirty-eight uncompromising years. He had baptised fragile children whose own children now knelt in the same creaking pews, buried men who had once stood beside him as an usher at weddings, and sat through enough vicious family quarrels to know that the noble causes people gave for hating one another were rarely the petty, festering causes that actually mattered. He knew exactly which brothers had stopped speaking over a contested inheritance, which respectable husbands were drinking far more than they admitted to their wives, which women were deathly frightened of a presence they would never dare name in public, and which elderly, rigid parishioners would violently object if the flowers beside the Virgin were moved even an inch from their accustomed position. To Daniel, who had first met him as a nervous adolescent grappling with far more doubts than genuine faith, Michael had seemed almost impossibly patient, a towering monolith of grace and absolute authority.
Their first real conversation had lasted nearly two hours. Daniel had come to the church because somebody told him that Father Michael was willing to listen to difficult questions, and Michael had listened without trying to turn the boy's uncertainty into a rigid lesson. He had not hurried him toward certainty or treated doubt as a moral failure. Years later, when Daniel entered the seminary, Michael wrote him a letter every month, never long but always deeply personal, and when Daniel was finally ordained, Michael had stood in the front pew with tears running openly down his weathered face. Daniel had teased him about it afterwards, and Michael had laughed, stating that a man was allowed to cry when someone he had watched grow up finally became exactly what he had hoped to become.
The relationship had shifted fundamentally when Michael's daughter, Clara, entered Daniel's life, a courtship that had culminated in a devoted marriage now well over a decade in its maturity. Their union had not been overtly arranged by Michael, though Daniel had always suspected that the old priest had quietly, perhaps desperately, hoped they might tether themselves to one another. Clara had initially been indifferent to that possibility and had once told Daniel that if her father had ever attempted to arrange her marriage, she would have left the country simply to spite him. She had first noticed Daniel because he was always the one carrying heavy boxes while everyone else seemed suddenly to discover some urgent reason to be elsewhere. What began as a comfortable familiarity within the church grounds blossomed into a steady affection, and what became affection eventually solidified into marriage with such seamless naturalness that Daniel could no longer remember precisely when he had ceased being merely a priest and had become a husband.
Their wedding had been modest and quiet. There had been no grand reception and no distant honeymoon, only a rented car, a lunch crowded with relatives, a small cake, and a series of photographs in which Clara kept laughing at the wrong moments. The photographer had asked her twice to look at the camera, and both times she had failed because something Daniel had whispered had genuinely amused her. Michael had stood behind the photographer pretending to look terribly annoyed while smiling more broadly than either of them.
Now, years later, Daniel had become both Michael's rightful successor and his son-in-law, an arrangement that occasionally amused them but more often cemented a deep, unspoken bond. Michael had never made the distinction between the two relationships particularly clear. At church, he treated Daniel as a capable equal who still had sacred mysteries to learn; at home, he loudly criticised the way Daniel overcooked the Sunday potatoes, forgot to buy enough tea, and carelessly left open books lying spine-up on the kitchen table. Clara claimed that this was undeniable proof that her father respected him, because Michael had never wasted such intimate criticism on anyone; he did not consider his own blood.
That morning, the rain lashing against the sacristy windows, Clara appeared at the church just after nine carrying two steaming paper cups of coffee and a canvas bag whose strap had been wound tightly around her pale wrist. Daniel was bent closely over a decaying parish ledger in the small, cramped office behind the altar, trying to decipher a spidery handwriting that might have been forty years old, when she placed one of the cups beside his hand. She looked at him with the exasperated, affectionate expression of a wife who already knew exactly what his defensive answer would be before she even asked the question.
“You forgot breakfast again,” she said.
Daniel looked up at her, rubbing his tired eyes. “I didn't forget, Clara.”
“You did.”
“I postponed it until the accounts were settled.”
“That is precisely the sort of empty lie people tell when they forget,” she replied softly.
She took the chair opposite his desk and watched him with the amused patience of somebody who had heard the same tired defence a thousand times before. Daniel reached across the battered wood of the desk and took her hand. She allowed him to keep it for a lingering moment before her gaze moved away, sweeping toward the heavy ledger.
“So?” she asked.
“So what?”
“How does it feel?”
“To be working in my own church?”
“To be working in my father's church.”
Daniel smiled, a slow, quiet expression. “I thought it was mine now.”
“It was never his either, Daniel.”
Her gaze moved slowly around the shadow-draped office. Michael's old oak desk occupied the exact same place it had occupied for nearly four decades, though one corner had been poorly repaired so many times that the wood had become a bruised, unnatural dark brown. A framed photograph of Michael stood on a high shelf beside a heavy brass crucifix. He was perhaps fifty in the picture, still fiercely dark-haired, his expression stern and unyielding enough to make Clara laugh whenever she caught sight of it.
“He would absolutely hate that you moved his chair,” she murmured.
“I didn't move it.”
“You did. It's angled toward the door now.”
“He hasn't been here for three months, Clara.”
“He knows,” she said, her smile fading slightly at the edges. “He always knows.”
Daniel laughed gently, and Clara smiled into her coffee before asking how her father was doing. The change in her expression was slight, but he noticed it instantly because he had learned to recognise the microscopic movements around her eyes that belonged intrinsically to a daughter’s deep worry rather than casual conversation.
“He is still terribly tired,” Daniel offered quietly. “The doctors at the hospital are adjusting his medication again today.”
“He sounded worse yesterday.”
“I know.”
“Did he ask about the church?”
“Before he even mentioned his own failing lungs.”
She lowered her eyes to the rim of her paper cup, tracing it with a trembling finger.
“That sounds exactly like him,” she whispered.
Daniel understood the heavy weight of what she was not saying. The church had occupied so much of her father's long life that his sudden absence had left behind something much larger and more terrifying than an empty room. There were objects throughout the ancient building that seemed to belong to him simply because he had touched them for so many consecutive years. There were elderly parishioners who still asked when he would return to the pulpit. There were shadowy places in the sacristy where Daniel instinctively expected to find him standing in the gloom. Even Clara, who had spent her entire childhood watching her father leave for work in the same immaculate black cassock and return home with the smell of incense and rain upon him, seemed entirely uncertain what to do with the fact that he could no longer simply get up and walk back through those heavy wooden doors.
“I'll visit him after lunch,” Daniel said.
“Take some real food with you.”
“He won't eat it, Clara. You know that.”
“Then bring it back. It will make him feel normal to refuse it.”
She stood, brushing a trace of invisible dust from the heavy black shoulder of his cassock.
“You're getting dust on yourself again.”
“I work here.”
“You're a priest, Daniel. You're not supposed to look like a carpenter.”
“I think God can manage the distinction.”
She leaned down and kissed his forehead, and for a fleeting moment the oppressive room seemed less like a church office and more like the bright kitchen at home, where ordinary things had a way of becoming precious precisely because nobody considered them worth remarking upon. Then Clara stepped toward the dark corridor and glanced back at him.
“Come home tonight before nine,” she instructed.
“I'll try.”
“Don't try. Come home.”
She disappeared down the echoing stone corridor before he could formulate a promise. Daniel listened to her hurried footsteps recede into the distance, and then heard the heavy outer door close softly against the storm. He remained at his desk with his hand resting near the coffee she had brought, unexpectedly conscious of how much fragile comfort there was in being known through such small, unremarkable domestic devotions. Clara knew when he skipped breakfast, which stiff shirts he disliked wearing beneath his cassock, where he usually misplaced his reading spectacles, and how he always forgot an umbrella until he was already standing in the freezing rain. None of those things had anything to do with faith, and perhaps that was exactly why they felt so precious to him.
By the middle of the week, the exhausting, ceaseless rhythm of the parish had settled heavily upon his shoulders. There were endless morning Masses to deliver, screaming infants to baptise, convoluted accounts to balance, and frantic calls from devastated families asking about funeral arrangements. Children needed certificates for school, and there were endless small, human matters that could never be anticipated until they were suddenly urgent. He heard confessions almost every afternoon, sitting in the stifling dark of the wooden booth. Some unburdening lasted a mere five minutes; others stretched agonisingly toward half an hour. An elderly woman whispered her minor sins so softly that Daniel had to press his ear against the cold lattice to hear her. A young, exhausted mother began sobbing violently halfway through her prayer of contrition because she had struck her child in a moment of blinding frustration. A factory worker spent several tense minutes describing a vicious argument with his brother, ending by asking whether anger itself could be a mortal sin when the thing that caused it had been fundamentally unjust.
Daniel listened to all of them with the profound, unwavering seriousness Michael had instilled in him fifteen years ago. One of the first essential lessons the older man had given him was never to hurry a penitent simply because the sin itself sounded dull or familiar. There were no identical sins, Michael had said, because there were no identical human hearts. A lie might be childish foolishness in one person's life and an absolute catastrophe in another. Betrayal could arise from cowardice, sheer desperation, greed, or a suffocating loneliness. What mattered was not merely the sterile facts of what had been done, but the agonising psychological burden the person had carried into that claustrophobic room before finally finding the courage to speak it into the dark.
Daniel thought of those words whenever the atmosphere in the confessional became difficult to breathe. He had begun to understand that people rarely came to the church to tell the objective truth in its simplest, ugliest form. They came carrying carefully curated versions of events, narratives designed to allow them to remain the fundamentally good people they desperately believed themselves to be. His task was not to judge those polished versions but to listen patiently until, sometimes, the terrified person on the other side of the screen managed to reach the rotting truth buried underneath them.
On Friday evening, the rain began in earnest shortly after six, beating a frantic, rhythmic tattoo against the slate roof. By then the last lingering parishioners had vanished into the gloom, the heavy oak doors of the sacristy had been bolted, and the church had entered that deep, suffocating quiet hour in which the ancient building seemed to withdraw entirely into itself. Beyond the stained-glass windows, the roofs of Ballymore had disappeared beneath a fine, impenetrable silver curtain, and the streetlamps had come on early, their distorted reflections trembling in the rainwater gathered along the kerb. Daniel remained seated in the dark confessional for several long minutes after the final penitent had departed. He was not waiting for anyone; he had simply grown accustomed to the sensory deprivation, the smell of old pine, cold wax, and stale breath.
The confessional was older than he was. Its heavy wood had been darkened by decades of sweating hands, nervous shoulders, and damp clothing, and the wooden lattice separating priest from penitent had been worn completely smooth where generations of desperate fingers had pressed against it. A faded red velvet curtain hung across the entrance, heavy with dust. Daniel had thought more than once about replacing it, but whenever he imagined a new, pristine piece of cloth in its place, the idea seemed somehow violently unnecessary, as if changing that one curtain would be more intrusive than shattering a stained-glass window.
He finally pushed through the heavy fabric, closed the door, and returned to the dimly lit sacristy. On the small table beside his desk was a neat stack of parish envelopes that had been left there during the chaotic afternoon. He began sorting them mechanically until his hand hovered over the uppermost envelope. It did not bear his name. Nor did it bear the formal title of his predecessor.
Not Father Michael.
Not Father Michael Mercer.
Simply, Michael.
He picked the thick, cream-coloured paper up and turned it over slowly under the yellow lamplight. There was no return address, no official parish seal, and absolutely no indication of who had dared to leave it there in the sanctity of his private office. The quality of the paper was unnervingly expensive, heavy and textured. Breaking the wax seal with his thumb, Daniel unfolded the single sheet inside.
The handwriting was impossibly small, meticulous, and perfectly legible.
Tonight. 7 PM. Wait in your confessional. Do not come out. Do not look at me.
Daniel read the familiar words, tracing the precise ink with a quiet, almost reverent stillness. There was no signature, no further explanation, and no indication of whether the writer was a parishioner, a total stranger, or someone who had been watching the church from the shadows since his arrival.
He stood beside the desk with the heavy paper between his long fingers, listening to the relentless rain against the stained glass, savouring the enormous, suffocating quietness that had settled over the empty church. The building had become so profoundly still that he could hear the faint, arrhythmic ticking of the clock above the sacristy door and, somewhere deep within the ancient walls, the slow settling of timber that sounded uncomfortably like a footstep. For a moment, a lesser man might have considered the possibility that the note was a cruel prank, the work of somebody amused by the transition of power in a parish that had known only one ruler for nearly four decades. But the specificity of the instructions, addressed strictly to the dying man who had kept this town's secrets for thirty-eight years, carried the cold, undeniable weight of an instruction that fully assumed it would be obeyed.
Daniel folded the paper carefully, running his thumb over the sharp crease, and slipped it deep into the inside pocket of his cassock. He considered telephoning Clara, but rejected the idea with effortless ease. There was absolutely nothing she could do with such a thing, and he had no desire to introduce this particular fragment of terror into her evening. More than that, he knew she would ask him what he intended to do, and he required no guidance on the matter.
He closed the heavy ledger, switched off the office lamp, and stood for a moment beneath the dim, flickering light from the corridor. The church felt like a tomb tonight, cold and expectant. His thoughts turned inevitably toward the hospital, toward the frail, terrified old man lying in a sterile bed somewhere beyond the edge of town, and toward the precise, devastating moment he would place this insignificant-looking piece of paper into his trembling hands. Daniel took his dark coat from the back of the chair, locked the office behind him, and made his way toward the front doors through the black nave of the church.
He would show the letter to Father Michael.
The hospital stood like a pale, diseased monolith on the desolate western edge of Ballymore, looming beyond the last row of respectable houses and the flooded fields that sloped gradually toward the churning sea. By the time Daniel arrived, the driving rain had weakened into a fine, clinging mist that silvered the windscreen and turned the streetlamps into blurred, weeping circles of yellow light. He parked his car beneath a row of skeletal, rain-whipped trees and remained there for a moment in the dark, both hands resting lightly upon the steering wheel. The heavy, cream-coloured letter was still tucked inside his coat, folded twice and pressed flat against his chest. During the brief drive, he had withdrawn it more than once, only to slide it back into the dark fabric. He had read the meticulous words so often that they had begun to lose the ordinary appearance of handwriting, acquiring something infinitely more disturbing in its place: the cold, undeniable simplicity of an instruction that fully assumed it would be obeyed.
Inside, the hospital was stiflingly warm, causing the coastal dampness to cling uncomfortably to Daniel’s heavy black coat. The air smelled intensely of astringent antiseptic, boiled institutional linen, wet wool, and the faint, metallic scent of life-support machinery—an odour that seemed to belong to every place of dying and yet to no particular room. The long corridors were brutally lit and painfully clean, but the people moving through them carried a profound weariness that the sterile white walls could never disguise. Exhausted relatives sat huddled together beneath windows streaked with silver rain. Nurses passed quietly with clipboards pressed against their chests, speaking in hushed, reverent voices that seemed instinctively suited to a place where death was a daily visitor. From somewhere behind a closed door came the steady, electronic tone of a machine measuring something invisible and vital, its rhythmic sound repeating so regularly that after a while it ceased to be a medical signal and became, instead, a part of the building's own laboured breathing.
Daniel knew the exact path to Michael's room without needing to ask the nurses' station. He had been making this grim pilgrimage often during the past few months, sometimes walking shoulder-to-shoulder with Clara, sometimes arriving alone. He had come to know the third-floor corridor by the melancholy little details that marked it: the faded landscape photograph hanging crookedly beside the elevator, the ancient vending machine that hummed aggressively after midnight, the elderly man in the flannel robe who always sat near the reinforced window in the afternoon, watching the empty car park without seeming to wait for anyone in particular. Michael had been relocated into a private room near the dead end of the corridor, where the unopenable windows looked out toward the flooded fields beyond the hospital and, on the rare, clear days, toward a thin, bruising strip of the Atlantic.
The heavy door was ajar when Daniel reached it. He paused before entering, allowing himself a moment to simply listen. The television was murmuring in the corner, turned far too softly for the words to be deciphered, and beneath that meaningless static came the measured, desperate hiss of supplemental oxygen. For a fleeting moment, Daniel thought the old priest had finally succumbed to sleep. Then, the frail figure shifted against the sterile pillows and turned his sunken face toward the doorway.
“You came,” Michael rasped, his voice barely rising above the hiss of the machinery.
Daniel offered a gentle, flawlessly sympathetic smile and stepped inside, closing the door softly to seal them in.
“Clara thought you might be hungry.”
Michael’s rheumy eyes flicked toward the paper bag in the younger man's hand.
“She still believes, after all these years, that food can solve everything.”
“She believes you're more likely to actually eat it if I am the one who brings it to you,” Daniel replied, pulling a vinyl visitor's chair closer to the edge of the bed.
A faint, trembling smile appeared at the cracked corners of Michael's mouth, though it vanished instantly when a violent cough seized his frail chest. Daniel waited with perfect, unmoving patience until the fit had passed, placing the bag of food on the small rolling table beside the bed.
“How are you feeling tonight, Father?”
“I have been better,” Michael whispered, sinking back into the pillows.
“You said that exact thing yesterday.”
“And I was telling the absolute truth yesterday, too.”
For several minutes, they exchanged the hollow, comforting dialogue of ordinary matters. Daniel spoke of the broken heating in the church office, and of a particularly stubborn parishioner who had insisted on moving the heavy stone statue of Saint Brigid closer to the altar without permission. Michael offered a weak, breathy laugh at that, complaining that Daniel, after fifteen years, should have known better than to allow the congregation to rearrange the sanctuary. They spoke of Clara’s relentless worrying, of the upcoming Sunday service, and of a bright boy from the parish who had recently been accepted into a university in Dublin. It was the well-worn script of two men who shared a family, neither needing to dig deep for conversation because they had accumulated so much common life between them over the passing decades.
Then, the pleasantries expired, and the atmosphere in the room seemed to thicken. Daniel reached into his dark coat, his long fingers closing around the folded paper. For a fraction of a second, he allowed a perfectly calibrated look of hesitation to cross his features, portraying a man reluctant to burden a dying priest with a troubling mystery.
Michael, possessing the sharp instincts of a lifelong confessor, noticed the shift immediately.
“What is it, Daniel?”
Daniel slowly withdrew the heavy cream-coloured envelope and held it in the space between them.
“I received this at the church this afternoon. It was left on my desk, but it was not addressed to me.”
Michael looked at the envelope for a hesitant second before he trembled, age-spotted hand reached out. Daniel placed it carefully into his palm. The old priest unfolded the heavy paper, bringing it close to his failing eyes. He read the first line.
In an instant, whatever faint trace of life and colour had remained in his grey cheeks vanished entirely, leaving behind a pallor that belonged exclusively to the grave. Daniel watched with cold, clinical fascination as Michael's frail fingers tightened convulsively around the page. For several agonising seconds, the old man sat perfectly, terrifyingly still, his dilated eyes locked upon the meticulous handwriting. He read it a second time, much slower now, moving his lips slightly as though checking a devastating fact he already knew and desperately wished to find altered.

“Where did you get this?” Michael gasped, the words tearing out of his throat in a raw panic.
“It was delivered to the sacristy this afternoon,” Daniel answered evenly, making sure to emphasise the impossible detail. “It had your name on it, Father. Just ‘Michael’.”
Michael’s eyes snapped up, wide with an unutterable dread.
“Who brought it?”
“I don't know. There was no return address, and I found it mixed with the parish envelopes.”
“Did anybody see you receive it?”
“Not that I know of, Father.”
Michael lowered his gaze once more, his trembling thumbs folding the thick paper perfectly along its original crease. The television continued murmuring senselessly from the corner of the room, and beyond the closed door, a medical trolley rolled slowly down the linoleum corridor before fading from hearing. The ordinary hospital noises continued around them, yet Daniel could feel the distinct, suffocating shift in the room's atmosphere. The air had grown heavy, expectant, and cold.
“Father?” Daniel prompted, keeping his voice carefully pitched to a low register of concern.
Michael placed the folded letter on the hospital blanket, treating it as though it were a venomous insect.
“You shouldn't have shown me this, Daniel.”
“Why?”
The old priest did not answer immediately. His eyes remained fixed on the paper, viewing it as an object capable of becoming physically dangerous merely by being looked at for too long.
“Have you seen this specific handwriting before?”
Michael swallowed heavily, his Adam's apple bobbing in his ruined throat.
“Yes.”
The absolute simplicity of the answer was profound. Daniel leaned forward, resting his forearms on his knees.
“When?”
Michael drew a slow, rattling breath through the plastic oxygen tube.
“A very long time ago.”
“How long?”
The old priest slowly turned his face toward the dark window. Outside, the rain had thickened again, and the glowing windows of the buildings across the hospital grounds were dissolving into the grey, weeping weather. For several seconds, he simply watched the storm, gathering the courage to speak into the dark.
“Twelve years.”
Daniel allowed a mask of quiet disbelief to settle over his face.
“Twelve years ago?”
Michael nodded, a small, jerky movement.
“And you received the same letter?”
For a while, the dying man said nothing at all. His right hand crept over the folded paper, resting upon it almost protectively.
“Not the same. The wording was identical. The instructions were identical. But the name was different.” He turned his head back to Daniel, his eyes shining with unshed tears of pure terror. “It was addressed to me, Daniel. Just as this one was.”
The sentence seemed to hang suspended in the sterile air long after he had spoken it. Daniel looked down at the letter and then back at Michael, allowing the silence to stretch until the paper seemed to acquire the massive weight of something ancient and malevolent.
“What happened, Michael?”
Michael's hand shot out, his bony fingers closing fiercely around Daniel's wrist. The movement was so sudden, so shockingly desperate, that Daniel instinctively stiffened in his chair.
“Don't go to the church tonight,” Michael begged, his voice cracking into a sob.
“I wasn't planning to,” Daniel lied smoothly. “I only wanted to know what the letter meant.”
“You don't need to know. You must never know.”
Michael released his desperate grip and turned his face toward the window again, weeping silently. Daniel had never seen fear in the man like this. Over the fifteen years he had known his father-in-law, he had seen Michael exhausted, deeply grieving, fiercely angry, and occasionally plagued by theological uncertainty, but raw, animalistic fear had always seemed entirely incompatible with the monolith of a man he knew. Michael had stood unflinching beside open graves in the dead of winter, entered hospital rooms where people were screaming in agony, sat with families who had lost their infant children, and spoken calmly to men who had just been told they would not live to see another spring. He had always possessed the peculiar, unshakeable calm of a man who had spent a lifetime accepting that there were things no human being could control.
That impenetrable calm had been entirely shattered.
“Father, what happened twelve years ago?” Daniel pressed, his voice a soft, probing instrument.
Michael squeezed his eyes shut, pressing his head back against the pillows.
“Nothing I can ever explain to you.”
“Then simply tell me what happened.”
“I can't.”
“Why not?”
His father-in-law opened his eyes and looked at him, the despair in his gaze absolute.
“Because I should have confessed it to someone years ago, before it was too late.”
Daniel leaned back slowly, feigning the dawning comprehension of a younger priest realising his mentor's fallibility. He understood perfectly that they were not dealing with a simple anecdote Michael had casually chosen never to tell. There was something deeply, terribly intimate in the old man's suffocating silence—something that had been buried in the dark for so long that speaking it aloud had become synonymous with physical danger.
“You are my son-in-law,” Michael whispered after a long, agonising pause. “You are the husband to my only daughter. You are also the priest who took my place at the altar. I suppose that means you have inherited far more from me than just the keys to an empty building.”
Daniel managed a faint, encouraging smile. Michael did not return it.
“I have spent the vast majority of my adult life telling broken people that confession is the beginning of God’s mercy,” the old man continued, his voice trembling. “I believed that. I still believe it for others. But there are hideous things a man can become so accustomed to hiding that, after enough years pass, the hiding itself begins to feel like a sacred duty. You convince yourself that your silence is protecting other people. Then, one day, you look in the mirror and realise you were only ever protecting yourself.”
Daniel remained perfectly quiet, offering no absolution.
Michael reached for the plastic cup of water on the bedside table. His hand shook so violently that water spilt over the brim before he could lift it. Daniel smoothly took the cup from him and held it to the old man's lips while he drank. When he finished, the old priest collapsed back against the pillows, closing his eyes until his ragged breathing had finally settled.
“I received that exact letter twelve years ago,” Michael said, his voice dropping to a hollow monotone. “It came to the church on a Friday, exactly as yours did. No return address. No explanation. Just those precise instructions to wait in the confessional after the doors were locked.”
“What did you do?”
“I followed them.”
Daniel watched him with predatory stillness.
“You sat in the confessional in the dark?”
“Yes.”
“And someone came?”
Something passed across Michael's grey face that Daniel could not immediately name, though he recognised the flavour of it. It was no longer simply fear. It looked much closer to the expression of a man who has heard a familiar, terrifying sound coming from a locked room where absolutely no one should be.
“Yes,” Michael whispered. “Someone came.”
“Who?”
“I don't know.”
“Was it a man?”
Michael swallowed dryly.
“Yes. It sounded like a man.”
“What did he look like?”
The old priest lowered his eyes, shame warring with terror.
“I don't know. I never saw his face.”
“Why?”
Michael looked at the folded letter once more, and when he finally answered, his voice had become a fragile thread of sound.
“Because he brought the dark with him. Because I was too terrified to look.”
Daniel felt the heavy silence of the hospital room settle securely around them, trapping them in the memory. The rain moved softly against the reinforced window glass, and somewhere in the distant corridor, a nurse laughed brightly at a joke before the sound was sharply severed by a closing door.
“What happened in the confessional, Michael?”
Michael did not answer immediately. His thumb moved slowly, rhythmically along the edge of the folded paper, tracing it back and forth, over and over, in the absent gesture of someone tracing the jagged outline of a wound they had spent twelve years trying not to touch.
“When he first came into the booth,” Michael said eventually, his eyes staring blankly at the wall, “I thought I was about to hear a confession. I thought I was going to sit in the dark and listen to another frightened, broken person tell me the worst thing he had ever done in his miserable life.”
Michael drew a slow, shuddering breath, and for the very first time, Daniel heard something beneath the weakness of the old man's voice that sounded unmistakably like profound, existential dread.
“I was entirely wrong.”
Michael closed his eyes tightly. And then, he began to remember.
“He did not kneel,” Michael whispered, the hospital room fading around them as the memory took absolute control. “He simply sat in the darkness beyond the wooden lattice. The moment he entered, the air in my side of the booth turned to ice. It was not a physical draft, Daniel. It was the suffocating chill of a predator stepping into a locked cage. I could not see him, but I could feel him looking at me. Not looking at my silhouette, but looking straight into the rotting core of my soul.”
Michael’s hands began to shake again, his knuckles turning white against the blanket.
“I tried to speak the customary words to begin the sacrament, but my throat seized. A terror so absolute, so entirely unnatural, flooded my lungs that I physically recoiled. I pressed my spine against the far wall of the booth, trying desperately to pull myself away from the lattice. My hand shook violently as I reached up and dragged the small, heavy purple veil across the wire mesh. I wanted to hide. I wanted to put even a pathetic scrap of cloth between myself and whatever malevolent thing was breathing on the other side of that wood.”
Daniel did not move a single muscle, drinking in the old man's terror.
“But the veil did nothing,” Michael sobbed, a tear slipping down his deeply lined cheek. “His presence was a physical weight crushing the breath out of me. He was so incredibly frightening, Daniel. He did not need to shout or threaten me. His voice bypassed my ears entirely and resonated inside the bone of my skull. He did not ask for my absolution. He demanded my destruction.”
“He forced you to confess?” Daniel asked softly.
“He dragged it out of me,” Michael wept. “I didn't want to speak. I clamped my jaw shut. I swore to myself I would stay silent. But the silence became an agonising physical pressure in my head, a shrieking pressure that built until my lips parted against my own will. I had to speak. The terror he radiated commanded it. I was carrying memories I had buried long before that night, but he didn't put them inside me. He simply knew exactly where to find them.”
Michael paused, opening his eyes to turn his head slightly toward Daniel.
“I was fifteen years old when the first true cruelty was committed.”
“Made?” Daniel echoed softly.
Michael offered a faint, entirely humourless smile.
“Yes. Made. Because I had a conscious choice, even then. I have spent a great many years lying to myself, claiming that I was too young and foolish to understand the gravity of what I was doing. I was not. I understood perfectly. And the thing in the booth made me admit it.”
He lowered his exhausted gaze to the hospital blanket.
“There was a girl.”
Daniel waited, letting the word hang in the cold air.
“Eileen,” Michael whispered. The name seemed to settle heavily between them like a physical weight. Michael's fingers moved slowly across the edge of the sheet, and when he spoke again, his voice possessed the distant, echoing quality of a man recalling not merely a historical event, but the monstrous stranger he had been when it happened.
“She lived near us in Ballymore. A small white house with bright green shutters. Her father was a quiet man who kept beautiful roses along the front iron wall. I remember those mundane things because I remember how perfectly ordinary and safe her life looked from the outside. That is what haunts me most clearly now. Absolutely nothing about her was strange. Nothing about her family was provocative or extraordinary. They were simply decent people living a perfectly ordinary life in a small, damp town.”
He drew a breath with great difficulty, his lungs rattling.
“And I was a bitter boy who desperately wanted something that wasn't mine to take.”
The words lingered for a long moment before he found the strength to continue.
“I wanted her attention. I wanted her to look at me and choose me above all others. And when I realised with absolute certainty that she preferred somebody else, I found that I could not bear the searing humiliation of simply losing her.”
Michael's voice had become steadier now, though the steadiness seemed to draw from a deep well of remembered pain.
“I thought about that terrible summer for years afterwards. I had categorised it in my mind as youthful jealousy, which made it sound almost romantic, almost innocent. But jealousy wasn't what it mutated into. Jealousy was only the doorway.”
He looked directly into Daniel's eyes.
“The rest of it was pure, calculated cruelty.”
Michael turned his face toward the ceiling, his next words coming much slower, heavy with the weight of confession.
“I was fifteen when I learned the intoxicating secret that a person could be profoundly wounded without ever being physically touched.”
The memory gathered thickly around his words, pulling the hospital room away until there was only the past.
It had been summer in Ballymore, though the dreary weather there rarely deserved the warm title. The mornings were often bright for a deceptive hour or two before the heavy grey clouds returned from the Atlantic, and the rough stone walls around the schoolyard remained damp and cool beneath a boy's hands even on the warmest days. Michael was fifteen, acutely conscious of girls, of his own awkwardness, and of the humiliating, unbridgeable gulf between the two. He wanted to be noticed. He desperately wanted to believe that there was something inherent in him worth choosing, and because he was fifteen, he had not yet learned the fundamental truth that wanting to be loved does not give a person any divine right to demand it.
Eileen had been on the periphery of his life for years before he ever thought of her as anything more than a familiar face. She lived three streets away in a whitewashed cottage with green shutters and a narrow, manicured garden enclosed by a wrought-iron fence. They had attended the same cramped school, sat in the same echoing church, and sometimes walked the same muddy road home, but there had never been a single spark between them that belonged to romance. The agonising shift took place one afternoon when they were dismissed early from their lessons. Michael was walking home with two boys from his class when Eileen appeared on the road ahead of them, her heavy leather schoolbag hanging casually from one shoulder. One of the boys called her name, and she turned. Her bright eyes passed indifferently over all three of them before settling on Michael for a brief, electric moment. She offered a small, polite smile and continued walking.
He thought about that meaningless smile for the rest of the evening, turning it over in his mind until it became a promise.
After that day, Michael began engineering reasons to pass her white house. He lingered near the church steps on evenings when he knew her devout family would attend Mass. He obsessively catalogued the coloured ribbons she wore in her hair, the specific books she carried against her chest, and the heavy wool coat she wore when the bitter autumn winds finally arrived. He hoarded those little details because little details were all he possessed, and gradually, through sheer repetition, he began to mistake his own obsessive attention for evidence that she must have been secretly paying attention to him as well. It was an ordinary, pathetic adolescent foolishness, but he fed it in the dark until it swelled into something far more dangerous than hope.
Then, he saw her with Thomas.
Thomas was seventeen, broad-shouldered from playing hurling, and possessed of the effortless, infuriating confidence of someone who had never once had to question whether he belonged exactly where he stood. They were standing intimately outside the wrought-iron school gates. Thomas leaned in and said something low to her, and Eileen laughed—a bright, genuine sound that cut Michael to the bone. When Thomas casually reached out and touched her arm, she did not pull away; she leaned into it. Michael was standing across the wet road with his schoolbag slung over one shoulder, watching them from the shadows, and he understood with a sudden, suffocating bitterness he had never experienced before that she would never, ever look at him the way she was looking at Thomas.
There was nothing inherently extraordinary about the rejection that followed. Michael could have simply walked home. He could have been deeply disappointed, deeply ashamed, profoundly unhappy for a month, and eventually forgotten her entirely. He could have learned one of the brutal, necessary lessons of adulthood while he was still a boy: that other human beings are not property, and that rejection, no matter how excruciating, does not magically transform into an injustice merely because it breaks your heart.
Instead, he went home to his bedroom and began systematically finding reasons why Thomas did not deserve to live happily.
He whispered to himself that Thomas was arrogant and cruel. He convinced himself that Thomas treated the younger boys badly and believed himself superior to everyone in Ballymore. He told himself that Eileen would inevitably see through the handsome facade and be destroyed by it. He repeated these fabricated flaws like a dark rosary until they became convenient enough to believe, because it was infinitely easier to cast himself as the tragic protector saving her from a villain than to admit he was simply furious that she had found him invisible.
The first rumour he planted was small, a single venomous seed.
He casually mentioned to a boy in his class that he had overheard Thomas was secretly seeing an older girl from the next village. He provided a specific name—someone distant enough to make the story difficult to verify quickly—and he delivered the lie with a masterful performance of reluctance, acting as though it pained him to repeat such slander. Michael remembered that exact detail with sickening clarity, because he was already discovering a terrifying psychological truth: if you want a destructive lie to travel at lightning speed, the best method is to disguise it as a secret you wish you had never been burdened with.
Within days, the seed had taken root, and the story had grown into a monstrous weed.
By the following week, the whisper network of the town had decided Thomas had been sneaking away to meet this mysterious girl for months. Somebody cheerfully added the detail that Eileen already knew and was suffering in silence. Someone else furiously countered that she was entirely ignorant and being played for a fool. Then, inevitably, the mob brought Eileen directly into the crosshairs, and the story ceased to be about Thomas's infidelity at all. It mutated into a judgment of her character.
The first time Michael truly saw the physical manifestation of what he had done was outside the local bakery.
Two older women were gossiping excitedly beside the foggy window when Eileen and her mother approached along the pavement. The women's voices abruptly dropped to a harsh whisper. One of them stared pointedly at Eileen, then looked at her mother with naked pity, and the older woman's face immediately hardened as she understood exactly what they had been discussing. She grabbed her daughter's arm with a vice grip and dragged her hurriedly past the shop without speaking a word.
Michael watched the humiliation unfold from the safety of an alleyway across the street. He should have felt a crushing wave of shame. He should have been horrified by the poison he had released. Instead, he felt something that he would spend the rest of his life trying desperately to disguise under another name.
He felt immensely, terrifyingly powerful.
It was not a loud, triumphant feeling. It was not the manic excitement of winning a game. It was a cold, quiet sensation, and because it was so quiet, he entirely failed to recognise its demonic ugliness. He had breathed a lie into the world, and now dozens of other people were eagerly doing the heavy lifting of destroying a life for him. He could stand in the shadows and watch Eileen suffer without a single soul knowing why he was watching. He could orchestrate the devastating consequences of his own words while remaining completely, immaculately untouched by the fallout.
The rumour continued to fester, and Michael eagerly allowed it to grow.
Thomas was no longer just unfaithful; he was a predator. Eileen was supposed to have known, and then the narrative violently shifted to claim she had been sleeping with someone else out of spite. A boy claimed she had been seen behind the school hall with a stranger. Another swore on a Bible it had happened multiple times. The vicious details became increasingly elaborate because bored, cruel people always prefer a vivid, scandalous lie to a dull, complicated truth. Soon, nobody in Ballymore cared where the story had originated. The destruction of the family's reputation had become the town's favourite entertainment.
Eileen stopped coming to school.
Michael saw her one dreary afternoon, peering through the rain-streaked kitchen window of her white house. She was sitting rigidly at the wooden table with her head bowed, her shoulders shaking, while her mother stood helplessly beside her. Even through the distorted glass, Michael could see that she had been weeping for hours. Her father entered the room and stood behind them, his face sagging with an exhaustion that made him look suddenly, tragically old. The three of them remained trapped there in their kitchen, entirely isolated from the town they loved, and Michael watched from the muddy road with the peculiar, breathless stillness of a god observing a plague he had engineered, but had not yet decided whether to feel guilty about.
Eileen slowly raised her head and wiped her red, swollen eyes.
And Michael was glad.
There are certain traumatic memories that soften and become less painful with age, as time affords a person grace and distance. That specific memory never did. Michael could still recall the exact, metallic taste of the ugly satisfaction he felt standing in the rain. She was deeply, profoundly unhappy because of a few words he had whispered, and a black, rotting part of his soul found immense comfort in knowing his will had been done.
Her family gradually withdrew from the suffocating judgment of the town. They stopped attending Sunday Mass, unable to bear the whispers echoing in the pews. Her father began leaving for his work long before dawn and returning well after dark to avoid making eye contact with his neighbours. Her mother completely abandoned the garden, and the beautiful roses behind the iron fence were left to wither and grow wild. Eileen's younger brother, confused and angry, started getting into violent fights in the schoolyard. Once, Michael watched him hurl a heavy stone at two older boys who had been laughing loudly outside their house. The boys ran away bleeding, and the little brother stood entirely alone in the middle of the road with his small fists clenched, shaking with an impotent, heartbreaking rage.
The next morning, someone scratched a vile word deep into the paint of their pristine green fence.
Michael knew exactly where that word had begun. It had begun in his mouth.
By that point, he could have stopped the avalanche. He could have cornered the first boy he had lied to and confessed the truth. He could have walked up to Eileen's devastated parents and admitted what his jealousy had done to them. He could have marched into the headmaster's office and sworn that the entire story was a vicious fabrication. Instead, he chose the cowardice of silence. He watched the family retreat further into their own misery and quietly told himself that the monster had grown too large for him to slay.
That was the pathetic lie he used to survive his own conscience in the years that followed. He called it a tragic accident. He called himself a foolish, naive boy who didn't understand consequences. He shifted the blame onto the gossips who had eagerly repeated the rumour. He did everything in his psychological power to avoid admitting the one damning truth: he had stood in the rain, watched the damage destroy her, and been fiercely pleased by it.
When the family finally broke under the pressure and decided to pack up and leave Ballymore for good, Michael went down to the railway station.
He told himself he had just happened to be walking by. He had not. He went to watch his victory.
Eileen stood shivering on the damp platform beside her mother, a battered leather suitcase resting at her feet, while her father silently loaded their meagre remaining belongings into the luggage car of the train. She looked fundamentally different. She had lost all the soft, youthful openness in her face, replaced by a guarded, haunted look as she watched the few people moving around the station. It was the look of a girl who had just learned the brutal lesson that a neighbour’s smile could easily conceal a knife.
Then, through the mist, she saw him.
She stopped moving. They locked eyes across the wet, grey platform. Michael braced himself, fully expecting to see burning hatred. Some twisted part of him actually craved her hatred, because hatred would have confirmed his power over her, made his sin easier to categorise, and therefore easier to eventually repent. But she did not look at him with hatred. Instead, she looked profoundly confused, her brow furrowing in uncertain sorrow, as though she were frantically searching her memory to remember if Michael had ever actually been kind to her.
Her mother called her name sharply. Eileen turned away, breaking the connection.
The heavy train lurched forward, carrying them out of Ballymore beneath a sky the colour of bruised iron. Michael stood alone on the platform long after the last carriage had disappeared down the tracks, watching the black coal smoke dissolve into the freezing air above the fields. He stood there and waited for the crushing weight of guilt to finally arrive. But guilt did not come. What came first, washing over him like a warm tide, was an overwhelming sense of relief. They were gone, and with them went the only physical reminder of the monster he had allowed himself to become.
The old priest's voice faded into the sterile hum of the hospital room.
Daniel looked at him, his face an unreadable mask of shadows, and saw that Michael's eyes had opened, though they were focused on a point floating somewhere near the ceiling.
“That is the part I never confessed to a single living soul,” Michael said quietly. “Not the cruel rumour. Not the fact that I had lied. I eventually found the strength to admit those petty sins to myself. What I never, ever admitted was the absolute truth that I had enjoyed watching her suffer.”
Daniel did not speak.
Michael looked down at his trembling hands.
“When the stranger compelled me in the confessional twelve years ago, I gave him the story exactly as I have just given it to you. But I tried to soften the edges to spare myself the agony. I told him I had been a stupid boy. I told him I hadn't meant for the family to be driven out of town. I told him the other people in Ballymore had made the lie worse than I intended.”
He paused, swallowing hard against the memory.
“He sat in the dark. And he listened.”
Michael's breathing became painfully uneven.
“Then... the voice behind the lattice asked me whether I was truly sorry.”
“Yes,” Michael whispered to the empty air of the room, answering a question that was seemingly still echoing in his mind. “I was.”
The old man shut his eyes tightly against the horror.
“And the voice in the dark said, ‘No.’”
Daniel watched him with the unblinking, terrifying patience of a predator perfectly disguised as a priest.
“He told me I was only sorry because I was an old, dying man looking back at a foolish boy. He said that wasn't what he had asked me. And then... then the punishment for lying began.”
Michael's frail fingers curled slowly, agonisingly into the fabric of the hospital blanket.
“He knew.”
Daniel leaned in just a fraction of an inch, his voice dropping to a hypnotic, chilling whisper.
“He knew exactly what you had felt?”
Michael opened his eyes, the terror entirely unfiltered now.
“He knew the darkest part. The part I had deliberately left out to save my soul. The compulsion wasn't enough for him; he brought physical agony when I tried to hide.” He looked desperately toward the hospital window, trapped back in that pitch-black wooden box. “I finally told him the absolute truth,” Michael sobbed, the tears flowing freely into the creases of his face. “And when I finally screamed it aloud in the dark—that I had wanted her to suffer, that I had revelled in her destruction—the burning pain stopped.”
He turned his head slowly back toward Daniel, his eyes hollowed out by the sheer weight of the memory.
“That was the first confession.”
The hospital room was tomb-quiet, save for the relentless rain lashing the glass and the measured, synthetic breathing of the oxygen machine.
Michael looked down at the folded, cream-coloured letter lying upon the blanket like a death warrant.
“And it was only the first.”
“There was another thing,” Michael said at last, his voice dropping into a hollow, echoing register that seemed to scrape against the corners of the hospital room. “I was older by then. Seventeen, perhaps. Old enough to know better, though I do not think my age was what truly mattered. What mattered was that I had begun to discover a subterranean part of myself, a malignant appetite that had been sleeping just beneath the surface. And once I discovered it, I desperately wanted to know exactly how far it went.”
Daniel watched him from the shadows, sitting perfectly still, offering absolutely no comfort.
“It had nothing to do with Eileen,” Michael continued, his breathing shallow. “That is what frightened me so profoundly in the years that followed. With her, I could still spin a pathetic narrative and tell myself that adolescent jealousy had made me cruel. With the others, there was no excuse of that kind. I hunted them because I wanted to know what raw, unfiltered fear looked like when I was the one who had engineered it.”
He paused, turning his sunken face toward the reinforced window. The hospital lights had begun to glow weakly against the driving rain, and his own reflection floated faintly in the dark glass, looking pale, indistinct, and entirely ghostlike.
“There was a boy named Peter.”
The name left Michael’s lips like a curse.
“I had known Peter for several years, though we had never been friends. He was a year younger than I was, terribly small for his age, and quiet by survival instinct rather than temperament. He had learned how to make himself completely inconspicuous, the way some neglected children learn to read or count, because attention in our brutal schoolyard was rarely given kindly to boys who stood apart from the pack. His father worked at the stone quarry outside Ballymore, coughing up dust, and his mother took in sewing from the wealthier women around the town. Peter wore the exact same brown wool coat through the entirety of every freezing winter. The sleeves had become smooth and shiny at the elbows, and the frayed cuffs were permanently dark with ground-in dirt. His leather boots were cracked straight across the toes. He knew that his family had far less than most of the others, and the other boys knew that he knew it. He carried his poverty like an open wound.”
Michael closed his eyes, the memory rushing back to fill the sterile room.
“But what interested me was not his poverty. It was his fear.”
“I discovered it one damp afternoon behind the school when a group of us were aimlessly throwing stones at an old, rotting wooden fence. Peter had been sent to retrieve a muddy football that had gone over the wall, and one of the older boys shouted viciously after him as he climbed down. Peter turned so quickly, so frantically, that he lost his footing and stumbled hard on the other side. The other boys laughed, a cruel, thoughtless sound, but I was not looking at them. I was watching Peter’s face. There was something in his expression that immediately caught my attention—not simply the ordinary fear of being mocked or struck, but the agonising uncertainty of not knowing what was going to happen next. He looked frantically from one boy to another, trying desperately to calculate whether he was in actual physical danger. I remember standing there, watching his wide eyes, and realising that this terrible uncertainty was entirely different from pain. Pain merely told a person what had already happened. Fear made him wait for it.”
“I took his schoolbag when he finally came back through the iron gate. He reached for it, fully expecting me to hand it over, and I held it just an inch beyond his grasping fingers. At first, he offered a weak, pathetic smile because he thought I was simply joking. I smiled back at him. Then, I stepped backwards. He followed me. I stepped back again, keeping the bag out of reach, and I watched the exact, devastating moment when his mind understood that I was not playing a game anymore.”
Michael’s hands trembled against the white sheets.
“Nothing physical had happened to him. I had not struck him. I had not threatened him with a single word. I had only made him uncertain about what I intended to do with his property, and yet the physical change in his face was immediate and absolute. His narrow shoulders tightened. His eyes darted toward the other boys, searching for an ally he knew he would not find. Then he looked back at me, waiting helplessly. That was the precise moment I became fascinated by him.”
“I gave him the bag after making him beg for it twice. The entire ordeal lasted less than a minute, but long after I went home, I found myself obsessively thinking about his expression—about the instant when he had realised that something vital belonging to him was entirely in my hands, and that I alone possessed the divine power to decide when, or if, it would be returned. I did not think of myself as a cruel boy in that moment. I thought I was a scientist who had just discovered a fascinating new element.”
“After that day, I began paying meticulous attention to the specific things that frightened Peter. I learned that he hated dark rooms and that he would never enter the windowless cellar beneath the parish hall alone. I learned that he became visibly nervous when older boys stood too close to him, because he could never calculate which one might strike him first. I learned that if someone spoke to him in a very quiet, measured voice while others were laughing, he listened with an unusual, panicked concentration because he instinctively believed that a quiet voice must be carrying a serious threat.”
“Once, just after the school day ended, I told him I had found a fountain pen he had lost near the equipment shed.”
The hospital room seemed to grow colder as Michael dragged the memory into the light.
“The afternoon was nearly over, and the weak grey light was already fading rapidly through the dirty, cobwebbed windows. Peter followed me inside without questioning it. The small shed smelled heavily of rotting damp wood, old fibrous rope, cracked leather, and the decaying rubber of footballs that had been stored in the damp for years. I pointed toward the darkest corner at the back and told him the pen was probably buried among the wooden boxes. He knelt in the dirt and began frantically searching.”
“I walked outside. And then I pulled the heavy door shut and threw the iron latch.”
“Peter tried the brass handle almost immediately. The wood rattled.”
“‘Michael?’”
“I stood perfectly still on the other side of the heavy wood, listening.”
“‘Michael, open it.’”
“I said absolutely nothing.”
“At first, his voice sounded merely irritated. Then it shifted to uncertainty. And then, the true, unadulterated panic set in. He asked me how long I intended to keep him trapped in the dark and promised, his voice cracking, that he would not tell the headmaster. He called my name several times, each time louder and more frantic than before, and I stood there in the freezing air, listening without offering a single word of reply. I remember resting my bare hand against the rough wood of the door and feeling almost nothing at all. I was not angry with him. I did not dislike him in the slightest. I simply wanted to hear what his voice would decompose into after another full minute of believing that I was never going to let him out.”
“When he finally broke down and began to sob, a deep, wracking sound of total despair, I felt a profound, settling calm wash over me. I opened the door soon afterwards.”
“He stumbled out of the gloom without daring to look at my face. He bent down, picked up his filthy schoolbag, wiped his snot and tears with the back of his trembling hand, and walked quickly away. I remained exactly where I was, watching his small figure disappear through the iron school gates. What I remember most clearly is the cold, analytical thought that crystallised in my head as I watched him leave.”
“I wanted to know whether the fear had followed him home.”
“It had.”
Michael drew a rattling breath, his eyes staring blankly at the foot of his bed.
“That dark discovery fundamentally changed the nature of what I was doing. Until that afternoon, I had thought of fear as a fleeting moment, a chemical reaction that happened only while a person was actively being frightened. Peter taught me that fear was a parasite; it continued to live and breed long after the event was over. It altered the rigid way a person walked home in the twilight. It made him turn violently at ordinary sounds he would normally ignore. It made him hesitate for a fraction of a second before opening a door, forcing him to imagine terrible things standing right behind him when the road was empty. I had tormented him for only a few minutes, yet the terrifying architecture I had built remained standing inside his mind long after I had walked away.”
“I found that lingering contamination infinitely more satisfying than the act itself.”
“I began inventing elaborate, quiet reasons to frighten him. Sometimes I casually told him that a violent older boy was waiting for him outside the school gate. Sometimes I walked past him and whispered that I had heard heavy footsteps following him on the road home the night before. Once, I hid in the tall reeds beside the river and called his name in a low, carrying voice when he passed. He spun around so quickly that he nearly slipped and cracked his skull on the wet, mossy stones. I watched him frantically search the empty road, his face pale and slick with sweat beneath the fading light, and I never came out of hiding. I followed him all the way home instead, keeping just far enough behind that he could never see me, but close enough that he could feel the weight of being hunted.”
“I wanted to know exactly how long he would remain afraid.”
“That became the private, sickening experiment of my adolescence. I discovered that there was a peculiar, twisted intimacy in knowing exactly what another human being feared. You could put the knowledge aside, keep it in your pocket like a loaded weapon, and simply wait. You did not have to strike them or shout at them to exercise your power. You only had to quietly suggest that the thing they dreaded most in the world might be standing nearby, and their own terrified mind did the rest of the butchery for you. I had learned how to plant a seed of pure dread inside somebody and then walk away, leaving them alone in the dark to water it.”
Daniel sat in the vinyl chair, his face a perfect portrait of chilling, absolute stillness. He did not blink.
“One afternoon,” Michael rasped, “another boy named Matthew came behind the parish hall to collect a tin lunchbox he had forgotten earlier in the day. He was much younger than the rest of us and still possessed the soft, unfinished face of a child who had not yet learned how to conceal his embarrassment. One of the older boys snatched the tin and laughingly suggested that we make him earn it back. We all laughed, and Matthew laughed too, because he genuinely believed we were playing a harmless game.”
“We began by asking him rapid, foolish questions. But the moment he answered one correctly, we changed the rules.”
“We told him he had failed because he had answered too quickly. Then we punished him because he had answered too slowly. We made him stand on one foot until his leg shook. We 
n in the wet dirt in his school uniform. We told him to close his eyes tightly and guess which of us was standing directly behind him. Every single time the poor boy thought the humiliating game was finally ending, someone invented another impossible condition.”
“Eventually, he stopped looking at the other boys entirely.”
“He only watched me.”
“I understood exactly what his terrified mind had realised. The others might be laughing; they might be the ones shouting the orders, but I was the one silently orchestrating the horror. I was the one deciding whether he would ever be allowed to leave.”
“When he finally broke, whispering through trembling lips that he just wanted to go home, one of the boys beside me grew uncomfortable and muttered that we should stop. I could have agreed. I could have ended it there. Instead, I stepped closer and calmly asked Matthew why he wanted to leave so badly.”
“He began sobbing uncontrollably.”
“I remember the deep, narcotic satisfaction of that moment with terrible clarity. It had absolutely nothing to do with anger, and nothing to do with hatred. I simply loved the intoxicating fact that he needed something so desperately from me. For those few agonising minutes, I was a god to him. I was the sole person who possessed the power to make the terror continue, or to make it stop.”
“When we finally returned the battered lunch tin, Matthew thanked us.”
“That pathetic gratitude stayed with me like a brand.”
“He was deeply grateful because we had ended a nightmare that we ourselves had created. Even then, as a boy, I understood that he was not thanking us for being kind. He was thanking us because he was so thoroughly broken that he was relieved we had simply decided he was allowed to survive and go home. I went home to my bed that evening thinking obsessively about the strange, dark power contained in that specific distinction—about the way absolute helplessness could miraculously make a person grateful to the very monsters who had made him helpless in the first place.”
“I had begun to understand that cruelty was not always about wanting to see another person suffer pain. Sometimes, it was about wanting them to know, in the deepest marrow of their bones, that you possessed the absolute power to destroy them if you chose to.”
Michael’s voice dropped to a hoarse whisper, the shadows in the hospital room seeming to stretch and lengthen around his bed.
“The last thing I did to Peter happened near the very end of that school year.”
“Rain had fallen violently the previous night, and the cracked asphalt of the schoolyard was still slick, wet, and cold when I saw his boots sitting outside the changing room. They were pathetic even then, the cheap leather cracked, peeling, and softened by years of excessive use. I took them while he was somewhere else and carried them behind the far brick wall. A few of the other boys eagerly followed me, already laughing because they understood exactly the kind of humiliation I intended to inflict.”
“When Peter came around the corner looking for them, wearing only his thin socks, we said absolutely nothing.”
“He searched beneath the wooden benches and around the perimeter of the shed. He asked, his voice trembling, whether anyone had seen them. After a while, he realised we had taken them, and when he looked directly at me, there was something in his expression I recognised immediately. It was the look of a trapped animal.”
“‘Give them back.’”
“I remember the chilling quietness of his voice far more than the words themselves. I knew, in that exact second, that I could end the cruel game and walk away clean.”
“I chose not to.”
“He was forced to search again while the other boys laughed at him. The ground was so wet and freezing that his thin socks became instantly soaked and dark with freezing mud. He looked so incredibly small and fragile standing there in the dirt without his shoes. After a few minutes, the humiliation broke him, and the tears finally came. One of the boys beside me grew visibly uncomfortable and whispered that we should stop.”
“I turned and told him to shut his mouth.”
“Peter looked directly into my eyes.”
“‘Please.’”
“I looked at him, smiled, and threw one of his boots deep into the thickest, foulest patch of mud.”
“He immediately ran for it, slipping in his wet socks.”
“And while he was desperately bending over to pick it up, I threw the other boot in the exact opposite direction.”
“The boys howled with laughter again, but Peter did not cry this time. He stood slowly in the middle of the freezing yard, holding one mud-caked boot against his chest while the other lay several yards away in a puddle. And something fundamental shifted in his face. The paralysing fear finally gave way to a pure, unadulterated hatred.”
“He hated me. He wished I was dead.”
“I knew it. And God forgive me; I loved knowing that I had been the one to carve that hatred into him.”
“That specific memory has followed me longer than any other from those years because it finally showed me the exact monster I had been seeking in the dark. I had not merely wanted to frighten Peter. I wanted to burrow into his thoughts. I wanted to become a dark entity he carried with him when I was not even in the room. I wanted the intoxicating knowledge that I could permanently alter the landscape of his mind simply by deciding to act.”
“That was the moment I finally understood the terrifying truth I would never confess for many decades.”
“I profoundly enjoyed destroying people.”
The hospital room was completely silent, save for the mechanical hiss of the oxygen.
“When the stranger sitting in the dark of my confessional asked me about the sins of my youth, I told him the story about Peter. I told him there had been other boys, too. I told him that we had played cruel games and that nobody had ever been seriously, physically injured. I described the psychological torture in the harmless, sanitised language I had spent years meticulously constructing to protect my own conscience. I told the shadow behind the lattice that I had been young. I told him I had not understood the damage I was causing.”
“He listened. He did not interrupt.”
“When I finished, he did not ask whether Peter had been injured. He asked me a single question: Why do you still remember him?”
“I told him I did not know.”
Michael’s body suddenly went rigid in the hospital bed, his eyes widening as though the memory was manifesting physically in the room.
“And then, the pain began beneath the skin of my right forearm.”
“It was not like being cut with a blade. At first, it was a sickening, narrow pressure moving slowly beneath the flesh, like a living, burrowing insect searching for a place to tear its way out. I clamped my other hand over it in the dark, but I felt absolutely nothing beneath my fingers. Yet the pressure sharpened, burning like battery acid injected directly into my veins, until I could no longer deny that something supernatural and horrific was happening to me inside that locked booth.”

“The agony beneath my skin instantly doubled, feeling as though the bone itself were splintering.”

“I tried to scream an answer, but my throat had tightened so violently I was choking on my own saliva.”
“‘You remember him because you loved watching him become afraid.’”
“I said nothing, weeping in the dark.”
“The pain moved deeper, crawling up my arm toward my heart.”
“‘You loved knowing that his fear belonged entirely to you.’”
“My breathing became rapid, shallow gasps. I was suffocating.”
“‘You loved knowing that you were a god to him. That you could make the terror continue, or make it stop.’”
“I closed my eyes, and the whole memory violently returned at once: Peter standing barefoot and shivering in the wet schoolyard, one muddy shoe clutched in his freezing hand, the other lying in a puddle several yards away. The other boys laughing like hyenas. And myself, standing among them with the intoxicating, divine knowledge that I could stop his suffering whenever I chose, and actively choosing to prolong it.”
“‘I liked it!’ I screamed at the lattice. ‘I liked it!’”
“The pain instantly stopped.”
Michael lay back against the pillows, his chest heaving, his face slick with a cold, greasy sweat.
“I remained slumped in the darkness of the booth, my hand clamped desperately over my arm, waiting in absolute terror for the next excruciating question.”
“Instead, the stranger was completely silent for a long, agonising moment.”
“Then, his voice came through the wood, colder than the grave. ‘You keep trying to make yourself innocent by making yourself young.’”

“‘You were seventeen years old,’ the entity continued, its voice vibrating inside my skull. ‘You were old enough to know what fear was. Old enough to understand the agony of helplessness. Old enough to recognise exactly what you were doing when you saw it reflected in another human being's face.’”

“‘And still... you chose it.’”
“I had absolutely no answer. The darkness in the booth seemed to close more tightly around me, compressing my lungs.”
“‘You were not becoming a cruel man, Michael.’”
“There was a dreadful pause before he spoke the final verdict.”
“‘You already were one.’”
Michael stared at the hospital ceiling, his eyes hollow and dead.
“I sat perfectly still, too terrified to even breathe.”
“Then, almost gently, the shadow added the sentence that destroyed my entire life.”
“‘And after knowing all of that... you decided to become a priest.’”
Michael’s voice faded into the steady hum of the machinery.
“For a long while, there was no sound in the confessional except my own ragged breathing. I had become acutely aware of the freezing dampness of my palms against the wooden seat, and of the faint, suffocating smell of old, rotting timber rising up around me, mingled heavily with cold candle wax and the stale, sickening sweetness of flowers that had begun to rot beside the altar. I could not see the man sitting on the other side of the lattice, but I could feel that he was waiting with the terrifying patience of a creature who had absolutely no need to hurry. It was not the ordinary impatience of a listener waiting for a story to end. It was the vast, crushing stillness of an entity who already knew that there was far more blood to be spilt.”
“‘I became a priest,’” Michael whispered, the words coming out with a self-loathing bitterness that seemed to poison the air in the hospital room. “I thought that was the moment I became someone else. I thought I had washed the monster away.”

“He said nothing in return. And I found myself continuing to speak, simply because the supernatural silence in that booth demanded it. It dragged the sins out of my throat.”
