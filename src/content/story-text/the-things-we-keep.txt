Lily kept flowers on the narrow balcony primarily because she could not abide the suffocating finality of an empty space. Every morning, before George had even finished his first bitter cup of tea, she carried her tarnished brass watering can from the kitchen, moving from pot to pot with the patient, meticulous attention of a woman greeting fading friends. Roses, hibiscus, marigolds, and jasmine crowded the rusting iron railing, tangled alongside several leafy, parasitic strays whose names George had never bothered to learn. The balcony caught the pale eastern sun for only a few fleeting hours, but Lily insisted that the meagre light was sufficient; she had always held a quiet, unyielding belief that most living things survived on far less than people imagined.
On that particular morning, the winter air possessed a biting, ominous sharpness, and the leaves hung heavy with a cold, pale dew. George found her standing barefoot among the damp terracotta pots, a frail figure wrapped tightly in a faded cotton sari and a moth-eaten woollen shawl. He lingered in the doorway, a silent observer watching her pour water into a pot that was already dark and bloated with moisture, the soil refusing to swallow any more.
"Ma, you watered that yesterday," he observed, his voice rough with morning sleep.
Lily merely glanced over her shoulder, her expression serene but resolute. "They are thirsty."
"They were thirsty yesterday too?"
"They are still thirsty today," she replied, without breaking her rhythmic pouring.
George walked over, gently but firmly prying the heavy brass can from her freezing fingers. "You have been saying that for thirty years. And you have been watering them incorrectly for thirty years."
She looked down at the drowning plant, and then up at her towering son. "What exactly is the incorrect way to water a plant?"
"You pour entirely too much."
"I pour too much?" she asked, a familiar defiance kindling in her dark eyes. "You told me yesterday to pour more."
"I changed my mind," George laughed, shaking his head at the predictable dance of their morning routine. "Convenient."
Lily smiled, surrendering the task with the quiet satisfaction of a mother who had successfully unearthed another excuse to supervise her grown son. Though George was thirty-six, she still surveyed him with a lingering anxiety, as if he might forget to button his shirt should she momentarily look away. She frequently lamented that he worked far too much, slept perilously little, and trusted the cold certainty of books far more than the fickle nature of people. George would counter that books were considerably less troublesome, to which Lily would predictably reply that literature could not cook a warm lunch. Their arguments had long since ceased to be actual conflicts; they had fossilised into a private dialect, spoken in the heavy pauses between breakfast and work, across the expanse of the kitchen, or while one tended to the suffocating greenery and the other pretended a casual indifference.
The apartment itself seemed to have grown organically around them like a creeping vine, a large, shadowed, and uncomfortably quiet space choked with dusty volumes, fading photographs, heavy mahogany furniture, and ailing plants that Lily violently refused to discard, even when they appeared more dead than alive. George’s study occupied the furthest, darkest end of this labyrinth, concealed behind a door that hovered perpetually ajar. Within it, books stood in precarious, unstable towers beside his desk, research papers lay scattered in untidy, sprawling piles, and leather-bound notebooks crammed the sagging shelves. To Lily, the room was a portrait of disaster; to George, it was a perfectly orchestrated sanctuary.
"You are going to lose something one day," she would warn him, her voice echoing slightly whenever she crossed the threshold into his gloom.
"I know precisely where everything is," George would murmur, his eyes locked on the glowing screen.
"You said that about your passport, yet I found it inside the freezer."
"That was one time."
"One time is quite enough."
George smiled, finally tearing his gaze away from the monitor to look at her. "Why, exactly, were you looking in the freezer?"
"Because you explicitly told me to find it."
"I did not tell you to find it there," he argued, amused by her relentless logic. "I told you to find it wherever I had left it."
"That," she sniffed, turning on her heel, "is not the same thing at all." Lily shook her head and abandoned the room, carrying his empty, stained porcelain cup with her into the hallway.
She had always existed in the margins of his intensive work, rarely demanding his undivided attention, yet remaining an inescapable presence in every shadowed corner of his daily existence. When George became entirely consumed by a complex problem, she would silently leave a steaming cup of tea outside his door like a peace offering to his intellect. When he forgot the physical necessity of eating, she would rap her knuckles once against the wood and stand motionless in the doorway until his intense focus finally broke. If he ever dared to complain that she was fracturing his concentration, she would casually remind him that concentration was a useless trait in a corpse. He would laugh, and she would feign innocence, pretending she had not made a joke at all.
Yet, the insidious change in their lives began on an afternoon so utterly ordinary it felt like a cosmic betrayal. George emerged from his study only because a sudden, oppressive silence had settled over the apartment; he realised, with a faint prick of primal unease, that he had not heard a single sound from the kitchen for nearly an hour. He found Lily sitting awkwardly on the cold linoleum floor beside the heavy wooden cabinet, staring vacantly at a shattered porcelain cup. A jagged, gleaming shard rested perilously near her knee, and a bead of dark crimson blood welled from a small, precise cut on her trembling finger. The air in the room felt suddenly thick, suffocating.
George was kneeling beside her in an instant, his heart executing a sudden, violent lurch against his ribs. "What happened?"
"Nothing," she whispered, her voice uncharacteristically frail and hollow.
"You are bleeding."
"It is merely a scratch."
"Did you fall?" he pressed, his eyes frantically scanning her fragile frame for unseen damage.
"No."
"Then why are you sitting on the floor in the dark?"
Lily did not look at him; her gaze remained rigidly, obsessively fixed upon the broken porcelain ruins scattered across the tiles. "I dropped it."
George crouched closer, gently enveloping her hand in his own. Her fingers felt profoundly, unnaturally cold, as if the blood had already begun to retreat permanently from her extremities. "How long have you been feeling unwell, Ma?"
She attempted a feeble tug to free her hand from his tight grip. "George, please."
"How long?" he demanded, the sharp edge of panic bleeding into his tone.
She released a long, shuddering sigh that seemed to drain the last of her colour. "A little while."
"How long is a little while? I don't know what that means."
"You do know," she murmured. Lily finally looked up, offering a faint, ghostly smile that did not manage to reach her tired eyes. "You ask far too many questions."
"I am a researcher. It is what I do."
"You are my son."
"I am that, too," he insisted, refusing to release her icy fingers.
She looked down at their joined hands, her narrow shoulders slumping in absolute defeat. "I have been so very tired. For a few weeks, perhaps."
"And you chose not to tell me?"
"You were working. You are always working."
George stared at her, the sudden, crushing weight of his own negligence pressing heavily against his chest. Lily sighed again, a sound akin to dry leaves scraping across a stone floor.
"Don't make that face," she chided gently, reaching up with her uninjured hand to touch his cheek. "The one where you immediately start imagining the worst."
"I am not imagining anything," he lied, his throat painfully tight.
"You always do," she replied, allowing him to pull her slowly, unsteadily, to her feet.
Two interminable weeks later, a grim-faced physician uttered the single, devastating word that neither of them had ever wished to hear, letting it drop into the sterile air between them like an executioner's blade: Cancer. Liver cancer. The cramped consultation room smelled oppressively of sharp antiseptic, stale coffee, and decaying paper—a scent George would forever associate with the exact moment his reality fractured. He remembered the doctor coldly detailing aggressive treatment options, bleak probabilities, and a terrifying array of biological complications. George countered the terror by asking rapid, clinical questions, scribbling every sterile answer into his leather-bound notebook as if the mere act of recording the grim data could somehow alter the inevitable outcome. Beside him, Lily sat entirely motionless, her thin hands folded neatly in her lap, a silent, hollow-eyed spectator to the morbid negotiation of her own remaining days.
By the time they finally emerged from the suffocating labyrinth of the hospital, the bitter winter afternoon had already begun to darken into an impenetrable grey dusk. They walked with agonising slowness towards the parked car, the silence between them heavier and colder than the freezing air.
Lily carefully slipped her trembling hand through George's rigid arm. "Don't look like that," she instructed softly, her breath pluming in the twilight.
"Like what?"
"As if someone has already died."
George stopped walking, his boots scraping harshly against the frozen asphalt. Lily paused with him, her eyes searching his pale, stricken face under the flickering glow of a streetlamp. He swallowed hard, fighting the violent constriction in his throat. "You're sick."
"I know."
"It can be treated. We will find the very best doctors."
"I know."
"We'll do absolutely whatever is necessary, Ma."
She smiled gently, a heartbreaking expression of pure maternal pity. "I know, George."
He forced himself to look away, unable to bear the quiet, terrifying resignation pooling in her eyes. She squeezed his arm, her grip surprisingly firm for a woman who was slowly fading away. "I haven't died yet."
Not yet, George thought, a sickening, icy wave of dread crashing over him, but he could not bring himself to voice the terror aloud.
From that terrible afternoon onward, the atmosphere within the apartment warped and shifted without either of them daring to acknowledge the mutation. George frantically seized control of the household, cooking whenever his schedule permitted, memorising the labyrinthine, toxic schedule of her medications, escorting her to every grim medical appointment, and jerking awake in the dead of night at the slightest rustle of her movements. Though she protested his suffocating vigilance at first, accusing him of pacing outside the bathroom door like a neurotic sentry, she eventually yielded to his desperate, clawing need to hover. Even as the sickness hollowed her out from the inside, leaving her fragile and translucent, Lily stubbornly continued to mother him. She brought him lukewarm tea during his midnight research vigils, folded the clothes he abandoned in the dryer, and scolded him for the noxious cigarettes he aggressively consumed in the freezing exile of the balcony.
It was during one of these tense evenings that she caught him smoking, stepping out into the cold night air to observe him. "You are diligently poisoning yourself while aggressively trying to save me," she remarked, her voice dry with irony.
George looked from the burning ash of the cigarette to his dying mother.
"Poor logic," she added, shivering slightly in her shawl.
He smiled, a fractured, weary expression, and crushed the ember against the railing.
"You see?" she said, her eyes softening. "I am still useful."
"You're impossible."
"And you are impossibly stubborn."
"I get that from you."
"No," she said softly, her gaze drifting out over the dark city. "You get that entirely from your father."
The strained smile vanished from George's face in an instant, replaced by a rigid, haunted mask. Lily noticed immediately; she possessed a terrifying perception when it came to the ghosts that haunted her son. "Go inside," she instructed quietly, placing a cold hand on his arm. "It is freezing."
George lingered on the balcony long after she retreated, enveloped by the toxic, lingering smoke and the suffocating ghosts of his past. His father, Jorah, had been dead for several bleak years. Jorah was a quiet, melancholic man who had deeply loved the monsoon rains, a man whose very name meant autumn rain, and who, according to Lily, had spent his entire life trying to live up to its gentle gloom. Jorah had collected antique fountain pens, despised loud music, and believed fervently that tea possessed a superior, earthy flavour when storm water violently lashed against the windowpanes. George had inherited the tarnished pens, but absolutely none of the man's placid patience.
Jorah's terminal decline had cruelly coincided with a period when George was incessantly absent, chasing projects, deadlines, and arbitrary accolades, always fiercely convinced that there would be ample time afterwards to be a dutiful son. On the awful night Jorah finally succumbed, George had been hundreds of miles away in a sterile, unfamiliar hotel room, heavily intoxicated and smoking by an open window when the hospital telephone began its shrill, accusing ring. He remembered the deafening silence that followed the call, the blurred, reckless drive through the pitch-black night, and the crushing, absolute finality of arriving to a room that held only an empty bed. For years, the mere scent of wet earth or the smooth, heavy barrel of a fountain pen was enough to resurrect the blinding, suffocating guilt of that hospital corridor. He had never confessed to Lily that he was drunk while his father took his last, ragged breath, but the invisible penance had driven him to abandon liquor entirely. He had forged a silent, ironclad vow within the darkest recesses of his own mind: if he remained endlessly alert, meticulously careful, and perpetually prepared, he would never again be absent when the grim reaper came to collect someone he loved.
Now, the encroaching shadow of death had fallen over Lily, and George was fanatically determined that this time, he would stand as an impenetrable wall between his mother and the grave. At first, this desperate vigilance was born purely of love, but as the grim weeks bled together, it mutated into a rigid duty, then into a suffocating, paralysing fear, and finally into a dark, obsessive compulsion that neither of them recognised until it had dragged him far beyond the boundaries of reason. George began conducting frantic, obsessive research late into the dead of night, the bluish glare of his monitor the only light in the cavernous apartment. He consumed endless, dense volumes on hepatic carcinomas, experimental chemotherapies, fringe clinical trials, and radical emerging therapies, filling his leather notebooks with a frantic, nearly illegible scrawl. He absorbed the sterile, terrifying lexicon of the disease so completely that morbid medical terminology soon felt more natural on his tongue than ordinary conversation. Yet, every frantic avenue of inquiry eventually terminated at the same impenetrable, maddening wall: there were promising probabilities and aggressive treatments, but there were no guarantees. Death resolutely refused to be factored out of the grim, biological equations.
One particularly bleak night, as a torrential downpour began violently lashing the windows of the study, George finally ceased his frantic reading. From the darkness of the adjacent bedroom drifted the faint, rattling sound of Lily coughing—a wet, terrible, scraping sound that seemed to drag heavily against George’s raw nerves. He stared blankly at the antique fountain pen resting heavily beside his open notebook, the ghost of his father sitting in the room with him, listening to the rain, silently condemning him for his past failures.
Driven by a sudden, manic surge of desperation, George clicked away from the oncology journals and opened an entirely different, darker avenue of research: Cryopreservation.
He read feverishly until the grey, sickly light of dawn crept through the blinds. The literature coldly dictated that while isolated biological tissue could theoretically be preserved at unfathomably low temperatures, suspending an entire, complex human organism was a logistical nightmare fraught with horrifying complications. The lethal formation of microscopic ice crystals could systematically shred delicate cellular structures from within, turning organs to mush, while the necessary cryoprotective chemical agents introduced their own devastating toxicity. Vitrification might theoretically reduce the ice, but the catastrophic osmotic injury and the sheer, monumental impossibility of successfully reviving complex organs to their normal, beating function remained insurmountable barriers to the medical establishment. No scientist on earth had ever solved the monstrous puzzle of dragging a frozen corpse back across the threshold into the warmth of ordinary life.
Staring at the glaring screen, his mind teetering dangerously on the precipice of a terrible, ungodly resolve, George picked up his father's pen and inscribed a single, damning truth into the centre of his notebook: Preservation is not resurrection. He pressed the metal nib down hard, underlining the terrifying words twice, the dark ink bleeding deep into the fibrous paper like a spreading, malignant stain.
The grim, pale light of morning had already seeped through the blinds when Lily found him slumped over his desk, his face bathed in the sterile glow of the monitor. She stepped into the quiet study and placed a frail, trembling hand upon his shoulder, grounding him in the waking world.
"You worked all night," she observed, her voice raspy with sleep.
George opened his bloodshot eyes, blinking against the harsh light of the screen. "What time is it?"
"Seven."
He stared blankly at the digital clock in the corner of the monitor. "I thought it was two."
"You need sleep, George."
"I'll sleep tonight," he promised, rubbing a hand across his jaw.
"You said that yesterday."
"I meant tonight."
"And tomorrow?"
George offered a weak, unconvincing smile. Lily did not return it. Instead, her dark eyes drifted to the chaotic sprawl of papers covering his desk, the complex chemical diagrams and chilling biological schematics laid bare.
"What are you studying now?" she asked, a slight frown creasing her forehead.
"Cryopreservation."
"Why?"
"Research."
"Research into what?" she pressed, her intuition flaring.
George hesitated, the heavy silence stretching between them. "Preservation."
"Of what?"
He finally looked at her, his expression carefully guarded. "Biological tissue."
Lily was quiet for a long, terrible moment, staring at the son she had raised, as if trying to recognise the stranger sitting in his chair. Then, she slowly picked up his empty, coffee-stained cup. "You are becoming strange."
"I have always been strange."
"That is true," she murmured, turning away.
George sat motionless in the dim light, watching her frail silhouette disappear into the hallway toward the kitchen. The moment she was gone, the guilt evaporated, replaced by the cold, mechanical drive of his obsession. He turned back to the screen and returned to his research.
At first, he told himself he merely wanted to understand the theoretical science. Then, the pursuit mutated into action. He began visiting clinical laboratories, corners of the scientific world where morality was often eclipsed by ambition. He interrogated researchers and technicians, consuming volumes on preservation systems, deep-freeze storage mechanics, and the catastrophic problems involved in maintaining complex biological structures over indefinite periods. Every expert he cornered gave him essentially the same bleak answer, couched in different, sterile terminology: It is not possible. Not reliably. Not yet.
But George’s deteriorating mind latched onto only the final two words. Not yet.
Driven by a desperate, frantic energy, he quietly rented a small, subterranean basement space several kilometres from the apartment and began secretly assembling the necessary equipment. He did not tell Lily; he did not tell a single soul. He worked like a ghost in the margins of his own life, ordering heavy industrial components, liquid nitrogen dewars, and complex monitoring systems under assumed names, keeping meticulous, coded records. What had begun as academic research metastasised into preparation, and preparation gradually hardened into a terrifying, unyielding plan.
The more Lily weakened, her body consuming itself from the inside out, the less George could see the monstrous boundary he was preparing to cross.
One evening, he returned home to find her standing alone on the darkening balcony. She was staring down at her beloved flowers. Without her constant care, several leaves had yellowed, curling inward like dying insects.
"You haven't watered them," she noted softly, not turning around.
"I was busy."
"You?" she asked, a gentle mockery in her tone.
George stepped out into the chill air, looking at her fragile profile. "You always water them, Ma."
"I forgot."
Lily smiled faintly at the irony. "That is serious."
Guilt pricking at him, George reached for the heavy brass watering can resting near the wall. But she raised a thin hand, stopping him.
"Leave them."
"They need water, or they will die."
"They'll be fine."
He stared at the wilting foliage, a knot tightening in his chest. "Nothing is fine for very long."
Lily turned slowly toward him, her dark eyes piercing straight through his carefully constructed armour. "You are thinking about death again."
George did not answer. He couldn't.
She sat down heavily in one of the wicker chairs, the effort clearly exhausting her. "Come here."
He obeyed, sinking into the chair beside her. For a long while, they sat in silence, watching the vast, indifferent city bleed into the encroaching night.
"I am not afraid of dying," she said eventually, her voice steady and clear.
"I am."
"I know."
"I don't want you to die."
"I know."
"I can do something," he blurted out, the secret clawing at his throat.
Lily looked at him, her expression unreadable in the twilight. "Can you?"
"I can try."
"That is exactly what I am afraid of," she whispered.
George frowned, a flare of defensive anger rising within him. She reached across the small distance and took his hand, her skin feeling like dry, cold parchment against his.
"You have spent your entire life believing that absolutely everything has to be solved, George."
"Not everything."
"Almost everything," she corrected gently.
"I couldn't solve Baba's illness," he said, the old, festering wound tearing open.
"No, you could not."
"I wasn't there."
"You were not responsible for his death."
"I should have been there, Ma."
"You were his son, George," she said firmly, squeezing his fingers. "You were not his doctor. And you are not God."
He looked away, staring fiercely into the dark skyline so she would not see the moisture gathering in his eyes.
Lily continued, her voice possessing a weary, tragic wisdom. "You cannot bargain with time."
George tightened his grip on her hand, a desperate, crushing hold. "I can try."
She looked at him for a long, devastating moment, seeing the madness taking root behind his eyes. Then, she leaned her head back and closed her eyes. "Don't spend your life trying to keep me here."
George did not reply. The promise remained unspoken, already broken in his heart.
Later that night, long after she had finally drifted into a troubled sleep, he went downstairs to the secure room he had arranged beneath the apartment building. The air was bitingly cold, smelling faintly of ozone and metallic sterility. He stood before the towering, cylindrical chamber for a long time, the polished steel and thick reinforced glass gleaming under the harsh fluorescent lights. He placed his warm hand flat against the icy glass. For the first time, he did not view the monolithic machine as a scientific experiment. He viewed it as a door. And whatever the cost, whatever the sin, he decided he would force it open.
The following weeks evaporated into a strange, suffocating quietness. George maintained two separate notebooks. The first was a clinical ledger of madness, containing dense research on cellular preservation, ischaemic damage mitigation, cryogenic temperatures, vitrification storage, and theoretical revival mechanics.
The second notebook contained Lily.
He wrote down things in its leather-bound pages that no scientist, doctor, or God would ever need to know, yet were the only things that truly mattered:
She likes jasmine tea.
She hates coriander.
She sings softly when she cooks, off-key.
She absolutely refuses to throw away old clothes.
She calls me her boy only when she is deeply worried.
She still folds the bath towels incorrectly.
She cries during old, black-and-white songs but always pretends she just has dust in her eyes.
Sometimes, George would open the second notebook in the dead of night, read a single line, and find himself unable to continue through the blurring tears.
Lily knew there was some sort of heavy equipment humming downstairs, vibrating faintly through the floorboards, but she did not know why. By then, the cancer had consumed so much of her that she was simply too weak to ask.
One evening, as the shadows lengthened, she stood unsteadily at the doorway of his study, leaning heavily against the frame.
"Come sit with me," she asked.
"I have work, Ma."
"You always have work."
"I'll come out later."
"Later," she murmured, "is a very dangerous word."
George finally looked up from the glare of the monitor. She smiled, a frail, heartbreaking expression that held no anger, only an infinite, weary patience.
"Come."
Defeated by her grace, he closed the laptop. They walked slowly to the balcony. The city below them had already begun disappearing into the heavy velvet of evening. A scattered constellation of lit windows appeared across the monolithic buildings opposite their own. Somewhere in the distance, a solitary dog barked into the dusk and then fell silent. Lily lowered herself into her chair, leaning her head back with a heavy sigh.
"Do you remember when you were little?" she asked, her gaze lost in the fading sky.
"Which part?"
"You used to dig massive, muddy holes in my flower pots."
"I was making things."
"You were murdering my plants."
"I was four years old."
"You were seven."
George laughed, a rare, genuine sound that briefly shattered the gloom. "I planted that little mango seed," he recalled affectionately.
"And it never grew."
"Only because you took it out."
"I took it out because you planted it upside down, you foolish boy."
"I did not."
"You certainly did."
He looked at her, his heart aching at the clarity of her fading mind. "You remember absolutely everything."
"Someone has to." Her voice had grown incredibly soft, brittle as dried leaves.
George reached across the space between them and gently took her hand. She turned her head, her dark eyes locking onto his with a sudden, piercing intensity.
"George."
"Yes?"
"Promise me something."
"What is it?"
"If I become too sick... when the end comes... don't turn my life into a hospital. Let me go."
He swallowed the heavy lump in his throat, saying nothing.
"Promise me."
"I don't want to lose you."
"I know."
"I can't just sit here and watch you fade away."
"You don't have to watch."
"What does that even mean?"
"It means you live," she whispered, squeezing his hand.
He looked away, staring blindly into the dark. Lily shifted, resting her tired head gently against his broad shoulder. For a long while, neither of them spoke, listening only to the distant hum of the city. Then she said, her voice heavy with the pull of approaching sleep, "Sometimes, loving someone means accepting that you cannot keep them."
George closed his eyes. He knew exactly what she was asking of him. He knew exactly what he was going to do. And in the silent, rotting darkness of his own heart, he did it anyway. There was no dramatic, thunderous moment when the decision mutated into something evil. No divine voice warned him. No heavy door slammed shut. There was only a tired, dying woman implicitly trusting her son, and a son who had arrogantly convinced himself that love granted him the supreme right to decide what death was allowed to take.
The preservation began quietly, shrouded in deceit. When the final collapse came, George stayed right beside Lily, holding her hand, lying through his teeth. He told her everything would be all right. He told himself the same lie.
Afterwards, once the grim, unspeakable work was done, the massive chamber hummed relentlessly beneath the apartment, a mechanical heartbeat replacing the human one he had lost. For months, George reorganised his entire, hollow existence around that low, vibrating frequency. He wrote manic, obsessive research papers. He tirelessly modelled theoretical revival scenarios on his computer. He monitored every microscopic fluctuation in the chamber's internal readings. He slept on a cot beside the control panel on the worst nights, jerking awake in a cold sweat at the absolute slightest change in the machine's steady drone.
Sometimes, when the silence of the basement became too maddening, he stood before the thick, frosted glass and spoke to the shadow suspended within.
"The jasmine is flowering again," he whispered into the freezing air, his breath pluming. He paused, waiting for an answer that would never come. "You'd like it."
Another evening, he pressed his forehead against the cold steel. "I found one of Baba's old books today." He smiled, a broken, ghostly expression. "You would have told me not to keep it, because the pages are practically falling apart." He slowly placed his open palm against the glass, right over where her face would be. "I am so sorry."
The machine offered no absolution. It only hummed.
Above them, the seasons ground forward with cruel indifference. The balcony flowers bloomed in vibrant defiance, then withered into brown husks, and then stubbornly bloomed again. George kept watering them, a mindless, robotic homage to a ghost. With every passing month, the skin around his eyes grew darker, his face hollowed, and he began to look like a man rapidly ageing out of his own life.
Then came the storm.
It arrived late one humid night with a sudden, apocalyptic violence that seemed to shake the very foundations of the concrete building. Rain struck the plate-glass windows in heavy, deafening sheets. Lightning repeatedly strobed the apartment in blinding, skeletal flashes of white, followed by thunder that rattled George's teeth. Outside, the ancient trees bent and screamed under the punishing wind. George was sitting in his study, staring blankly at a monitor, when the electricity finally failed.
The apartment plunged into absolute, suffocating darkness.
He sat frozen in his chair; his breath caught in his throat. He waited. The heavy-duty emergency generator system in the basement should have activated within three seconds.
One.
Two.
Three.
Nothing.
Then, over the roar of the storm outside, he heard a sound from deep beneath the floorboards. A low, grinding, mechanical groan—the sound of a massive system failing.
George ran. He scrambled down the narrow, pitch-black stairwell, his heart hammering violently against his ribs. The basement was a tomb of absolute blackness. As he burst through the reinforced door, the emergency battery lights flickered weakly, casting long, macabre shadows across the concrete, before dying completely. He lunged toward the chamber, pulling his phone from his pocket to cast a trembling beam of light across the digital display.
The temperature digits were flashing red, rapidly climbing out of the cryogenic deep freeze.
"No," George gasped, panic seizing his lungs. "No, no, no."
He frantically wrenched open the heavy metal emergency override panel, his fingers slipping on the cold switches. He slammed the manual restart sequence. Nothing. He ripped out the bypass wires, trying to bridge the circuit. Nothing. To his absolute horror, he realised that heavy, muddy rainwater had begun violently leaking through a crack in the compromised basement wall, shorting the primary junction boxes in a cascade of sparking destruction.
George worked completely blind, his hands shaking so violently he could barely grip the tools, screaming curses into the dark as he tried to resurrect a dead machine.
And then, he heard it.
A dull, heavy, wet movement inside the chamber.
George froze, the screwdriver slipping from his numb fingers, clattering loudly against the concrete.
Thump. Another sound.
He stumbled slowly toward the monolithic cylinder, raising the trembling light of his phone. He wiped a thick layer of freezing condensation from the heavy glass observation port.
Something was moving behind the frost.
His heart seized. A primal, suffocating terror washed over him. "Ma?" he whispered, his voice cracking.
The dark shape shifted again, thrashing weakly against the confines of the tank. George stumbled violently backwards, tripping over a coil of wire, right as the primary atmospheric seal of the chamber released with a sickening, heavy mechanical click. The massive door drifted open, and thick, billowing clouds of freezing white vapour spilt heavily across the flooded concrete floor.
Through the parting mist, Lily opened her eyes.
For one impossible, blindingly euphoric moment, George felt a rush of happiness so absolute and complete that it miraculously erased the nightmare. The science was wrong. He had done it. She was alive. He laughed, a hysterical, sobbing sound, and fell to his knees. "Ma!"
Lily moved, a jerky, unnatural spasm. Her bare, frostbitten hand reached out, pressing flat against the still-frozen inner surface of the steel door as she tried to pull herself up.
But the moisture on her skin immediately bonded to the sub-zero metal. Something held her fast. Confused, still trapped in a waking nightmare of ice, she violently pulled her hand away.
The frozen skin of her palm adhered to the steel. It tore away in one brutal, sickening strip of wet red tissue.
