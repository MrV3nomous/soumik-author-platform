There are certain subtle tragedies a man notices only long after they have already come to pass, quiet absences that hollow out a life from the inside. For Jack, this quiet ruin manifested in the complete disappearance of his wife's footsteps. He could still perceive almost everything else within the suffocating familiarity of their apartment: the low, ceaseless murmuring of the living room radio, the heavy brass clock methodically ticking above the kitchen doorway, the fragile rattle of porcelain cups whenever Julia prepared tea, and even the soft, unsettling scrape of a doll's wooden shoe against the shelf as she meticulously rearranged her silent audience. But the actual sound of her walking had devolved into a phantom—a thing he remembered rather than heard. In the vibrant years of their youth, he had known the distinct cadence of her approach without a conscious thought. He knew the quick, impatient rhythm with which she crossed the floorboards when she was furious with him, the lighter, bounding steps that announced her happiness, and the slow, deliberate approach that signalled she was coming to bed. Now, when Julia navigated the narrow confines of the apartment, there was a heavy, agonising pause between one step and the next, a dragging silence that reminded him of everything they had lost.
Their home was situated on the third floor of an imposing concrete monolith that was nearly thirty years old—a structure modern enough to have entirely forgotten the optimism of its youth, yet old enough to have cultivated a dark, oppressive personality of its own. The plaster walls were kept meticulously clean, the concrete staircases were ritually swept, and the four apartments crowding each floor possessed the heavy, lived-in stagnation of places where people had spent decades quietly surrendering to their routines. Jack and Julia had relocated to this shadowy building only recently, driven by the grim necessity of being closer to the hospital. Though the daily commute to the steel factory had become a grinding, exhausting ordeal, Jack had accepted the burden without a single vocalised complaint, swallowing his fatigue like a bitter medicine.
The apartment itself was small, though Julia waged a relentless war to keep it pretty and unusually tidy. It consisted of a single, cramped bedroom, a kitchen that bled directly into a dim living room, and a narrow, windowless bathroom where the sickly yellow paint had long ago begun to curl and peel away from the damp ceiling. Faded photographs populated the sagging shelves, a radio murmured perpetually into the void, and a heavy landline telephone sat like a gargoyle beside the worn sofa. Most unnervingly, the living room was dominated by an extensive collection of porcelain dolls, their glass eyes staring blankly as Julia obsessively dressed them in varying garments whenever she grew weary of their stagnant appearances. Nothing in their possession was particularly expensive or luxurious; almost every object had been acquired purely out of desperate utility, or simply because it offered a fleeting moment of comfort in an otherwise arduous existence.
In the encroaching evenings, when the weak sunlight finally bled away from the dirty windows, the apartment sometimes managed to project an illusion of warmth. The kitchen would swell with the rich, heavy aromas of whatever meal Julia had managed to assemble, and the radio would whisper to itself like an invisible guest. Jack would return from the towering steel factory with the harsh, metallic stench of iron and machine oil clinging fiercely to his skin and clothes, prompting Julia to complain that he was dragging the bleakness of the industrial world into their sanctuary.
“You smell terrible,” she would declare from her chair.
“I work in a steel factory,” Jack would reply, shrugging off his heavy coat.
“That isn't an excuse.”
“It is the best excuse I have.”
She would laugh, a fragile, trembling sound, and he would lean down to press a kiss against her warm forehead, ignoring the faint shadow of exhaustion behind her eyes.
They had been married for fourteen quiet, eroding years, but before the weight of the world had settled upon them, they had been wildly, beautifully young together. They had met in the chaotic halls of high school, at an awkward age when admitting to love felt like a humiliating defeat, and trivial arguments served merely as convenient excuses to linger in each other’s orbit for just a few minutes longer. They had fought passionately over ridiculous, forgotten things, reconciled without ever remembering the origin of their fury, and slowly, inextricably fused their lives until neither could pinpoint the exact moment when adolescent friendship had mutated into absolute devotion. By the time they finally stood before an altar, Jack had long since lost the capacity to imagine a future that did not have Julia squarely at its centre. Those early years had been loud, careless, and fiercely bright. They possessed very little money, bore no children, and yet harboured an almost unreasonable, intoxicating amount of happiness. They could squander an entire evening arguing over a meagre dinner, only to end the night entangled in bed, breathless with laughter. They embarked on long walks through the sleeping city, purchased foolish things they could barely afford, and stayed awake until dawn simply because they could not bear to let the night slip away. There had been a golden, fleeting era when the sheer desire between them felt as inexhaustible and natural as drawing breath.
Then came the catastrophic accident, a violent intersection of metal and bone that required only a few brutal seconds to permanently deform the trajectory of their lives. Julia miraculously survived the impact, but her shattered spine did not grant her the grace of forgiveness. She retained the ability to walk, though it was a painfully slow, agonising process, and there were bleak days when merely crossing the expanse of the small living room entirely depleted her reserves. She had been forced to learn how to navigate her own home like a hostile landscape, her trembling hand often sliding flat against the faded wallpaper to prevent herself from collapsing. The sterile doctors had spoken in comforting, clinical platitudes about recovery, infinite patience, and the healing power of time, but Jack soon discovered that those hollow words morphed into heavy, suffocating chains when dragged into the reality of daily life.
He never once complained about the endless, grinding labour of caring for her—at least, he never dared to vocalise his despair where Julia might hear him. Instead, he quietly learned how to cook edible meals, meticulously washed their clothes, scrubbed the apartment until his knuckles bled, carried the heavy groceries up three flights of concrete stairs, and memorised the bewildering labyrinth of her medical appointments. He knew precisely which bitter tablets required a full stomach, which ones induced a deep, unnatural lethargy, and he could instantly gauge the severity of her hidden pain simply by observing the specific, careful angle at which she placed her teacup upon the wooden table. He had become exceptionally, tragically good at being fundamentally needed. What he had utterly failed to learn, however, was how to articulate the crushing sorrow he felt for the vibrant, careless life they had lost.
At forty-two, Jack remained a strikingly handsome man, though the punishing environment of the factory had eagerly begun carving its grim signature into his flesh. His hands were permanently rough and calloused, his broad shoulders sloped with a heaviness that had not existed in his youth, and deep, branching lines had permanently settled around his eyes. He laboured intensely because their medical debts demanded a constant flow of wages; the modest car they had owned prior to the accident had been reduced to twisted scrap, and replacing it remained a financial impossibility. Every grey morning, he descended into the subterranean subway to travel to the roaring factory, and every dark evening, he returned to the apartment carrying the absolute, crushing fatigue of the day deep within his marrow. Occasionally, he would unlock the door and find Julia slumped asleep in her chair, the radio murmuring a forgotten tune beside her. On those heavy, silent evenings, he would stand frozen in the doorway, quietly observing the woman he had sworn to protect. She appeared fundamentally smaller to him now—not less beautiful, and certainly not less loved, but physically diminished, as though the relentless illness had slowly and systematically eroded the physical space she was permitted to occupy in the world.
And Jack loved her; that was the terrible, unyielding truth that made the suffocation so difficult to endure. He loved the affectionate way she still called him 'Jacky' when the pain retreated enough to allow a good mood. He loved her strange, melancholic habit of meticulously dressing her porcelain dolls and conversing with them as if they were old, trusted confidants. He loved their predictable, worn-out arguments regarding his muddy boots abandoned near the doorway, and he loved the stubborn loyalty that compelled her to wait for his return before eating her dinner, despite his constant protests. But inevitably, there were long, desolate nights when Julia swallowed her heavy medication, closed her eyes, and vanished into a chemical sleep, leaving Jack completely alone in the silent living room with a burning cigarette trembling between his fingers.
Those solitary nights were the most dangerous. The apartment would sink into a profound, suffocating quiet. Beyond their dirty windows, the surrounding neighbourhood ruthlessly continued its ordinary, forward momentum. Golden lights flickered to life in distant windows, subterranean trains rumbled beneath the concrete foundations, exhausted people trudged home from their labours, and young couples walked closely together beneath the glowing halos of the street lamps. In the oppressive silence, Jack would inevitably begin to remember the man he had been before his existence was rigidly measured by pill organisers and blaring morning alarms. He remembered the electric thrill of being young. He remembered the intoxicating sensation of being desired. Cruelly, he remembered the vibrant, unbroken version of Julia, too, and that was the memory that twisted the knife in his gut. He did not crave the touch of another woman because he had ceased loving his wife; rather, in some dark, shameful, and unacknowledged corner of his soul, he possessed a ravenous hunger to simply feel alive again. He desperately wanted the careless hunger, the unpredictable excitement, and the foolish, beautiful illusion that life still possessed the capacity to surprise him. He never breathed a word of this quiet agony to Julia. How could he possibly confess such a betrayal? She trusted him with an absolute, uncompromising faith that absolutely terrified him.
One particularly dreary, rain-swept evening in early autumn, Jack arrived at the apartment considerably later than his usual hour. The downpour had commenced with a sudden, violent intensity, leaving the dark asphalt streets gleaming like polished obsidian beneath the yellow sodium lights. When he finally pushed open the heavy wooden door of the apartment, he found Julia sitting quietly beside the bedroom window, meticulously securing a tiny garment onto one of her glassy-eyed dolls.
“You're late,” she observed, her focus remaining entirely upon the porcelain figure in her lap.
“The factory was an absolute mess today,” Jack sighed, shrugging off his damp coat.
“It is always a mess.”
“That is precisely why they pay me to be there.”
“They certainly don't pay you enough.”
“They don't pay anybody enough.”
Julia offered a soft, genuine smile and lifted the lifeless doll into the air for his inspection. She had clothed the rigid figure in a pale, immaculate blue dress that seemed completely out of place in their dreary room. “What do you think of her?”
Jack paused, studying the blank porcelain face with a feigned, exaggerated seriousness. “She looks beautiful.”
“You didn't even look properly.”
“I absolutely looked.”
“You're lying to me.”
“I have been married to you for fourteen years,” Jack countered smoothly. “I know exactly when lying is necessary for my own survival.”
She laughed, a bright, startling sound that cut through the gloom. Jack walked across the faded rug and pressed a lingering kiss into the crown of her head, inhaling the faint, sweet fragrance of jasmine oil that always seemed to cling to her dark hair.
“Did you eat anything?” he asked, his voice softening.
“I was waiting for you to come home.”
“You know you shouldn't do that. You need your strength.”
“I know.”
He sank heavily into the chair beside her, and for a long, comfortable moment, they simply watched the rain lashing against the dirty glass together, shielded from the elements.
“Do you ever remember our time in school?” Julia asked, her voice tinged with a sudden, wistful nostalgia.
“Unfortunately, I do.”
She smiled, leaning her head slightly toward him. “I liked you quite a bit back then.”
“You aggressively chased me.”
“I did not do any such thing.”
“You absolutely did. It was relentless.”
“I was only fifteen years old,” she defended lightly.
“And you were utterly terrifying.”
She laughed again, and for one brief, suspended moment, the heavy, agonising years of sickness and labour that stood between their past and their present seemed to completely evaporate. But the illusion shattered when Julia reached out and weakly gripped his rough hand, her skin feeling unsettlingly cold.
“Are you happy, Jack?” she asked, her dark eyes suddenly searching his face with an intensity he could barely withstand.
The question was so fundamentally simple that Jack found himself entirely unequipped to answer it. He looked down into the face of the woman he had loved his entire life, trapped in her broken body.
“Yes,” he finally answered softly.
It was not a lie. That was the most terrible, confusing part of his existence. He was genuinely happy. He fiercely loved his wife; he loved their cramped, deteriorating apartment, he loved their ridiculous, repetitive arguments, their mismatched old furniture, the murmuring radio, and even the unsettling dolls staring blindly from the windowsill. He loved returning to this small sanctuary and knowing with absolute certainty that she would be waiting for him. And yet, beneath all of that genuine devotion, something dark and feral inside of him had begun to violently hunger for a life that had long since ceased to exist.
Later that night, long after Julia had swallowed her bitter medication and surrendered to a deep, impenetrable sleep, Jack stood entirely alone in the suffocating shadows of the kitchen. The apartment was completely dark, save for the sickly, fluorescent hum of the small bulb burning above the rusted sink. Affixed to the humming refrigerator was a faded, curling photograph taken during their very first year of marriage. In the image, Julia was laughing joyously at something existing just outside the frame, while Jack stood with his arm draped protectively across her shoulders, looking absurdly arrogant and pleased with his own fortune. He reached out, lightly tracing the contours of the photograph with a trembling, grease-stained finger. He then cast a long, lingering glance toward the bedroom, ensuring that Julia remained trapped in her pharmaceutical slumber. Satisfied by the heavy silence, Jack collected his crumpled pack of cigarettes from the wooden table, eased the front door open with practised silence, and slipped out into the cold, empty corridor, pulling the heavy door shut behind him as gently as he possibly could.
The sprawling, concrete rooftop of the building had unofficially become Jack's private sanctuary long before he possessed any actual, tangible reason to think of it as such. There was absolutely nothing inherently attractive about the bleak, industrial space. It was merely a flat, sprawling expanse of cracked concrete situated above the seventh floor, bordered by a dangerously low parapet wall and claustrophobically surrounded on three sides by the windowless, towering backs of neighbouring brutalist structures. A small, deteriorating concrete canopy stood directly above the narrow stairwell entrance, its ancient plaster blackened and scarred by decades of acidic rain and urban dust. Throughout the daylight hours, the roof remained entirely desolate and ignored. At night, however, when the heavy doors in the floors below were securely deadbolted, and the weary building finally settled into a deep, collective slumber, the rooftop felt completely untethered from the world—a strange, liminal space suspended above reality. Jack routinely retreated there simply because he craved the toxic comfort of his cigarettes without risking waking Julia with the smell of smoke.
On this particular night, the autumnal air was sharply cold, carrying a biting chill that penetrated his thin shirt. The torrential rain from earlier in the evening had left the sprawling concrete slick and dangerously damp, and the sprawling city below exuded the faint, metallic scent of wet dust and concentrated exhaust fumes. Jack stood beneath the sheltering canopy, leaning one heavy shoulder against the rough plaster wall, quietly observing the grid of glowing streetlights below while the glowing ember of his cigarette burned steadily between his calloused fingers. The heavy metal door groaned on its rusted hinges behind him, shattering the quiet.
A young woman stepped out onto the landing, pausing at the very top of the concrete stairs. For several tense seconds, neither of them uttered a single word, suspended in the mutual surprise of the encounter. She was clothed in a remarkably simple, flowing emerald-green nightgown—a garment that might have appeared entirely ordinary had it not possessed a strange, iridescent quality that seemed to actively catch and hold the ambient light of the city. Her dark, heavy hair fell in loose, untamed waves around her pale shoulders, and her wide eyes registered a mild, cautious surprise at discovering another living soul inhabiting the desolate roof.
“Oh,” she murmured, her voice soft and melodious over the distant hum of the city. “I am so sorry to intrude.”
Jack offered an easy, disarming smile. “It's perfectly fine. You aren't bothering me.”
Encouraged, she stepped further onto the damp concrete, migrating toward the low parapet wall to peer over the edge into the dizzying drop below. “I just desperately needed to get some fresh air.”
“Having a bad day?” Jack asked, taking a slow draw from his cigarette.
“Not really, no.”
“Then what drives you up to the roof in the middle of the night?”
She cast a sideways, playful glance in his direction. “It possesses a significantly better view than my ceiling.”
Jack turned his gaze outward toward the sprawling, illuminated grid of the metropolis. “I certainly can't argue with that logic.”
She laughed, and there was something incredibly pure and unrestrained about the crystalline sound that immediately coaxed a genuine smile onto Jack’s weary face. An easy, surprisingly comfortable silence settled between them, devoid of the usual tension that accompanies strangers in the dark. Jack took another long drag from his burning cigarette, noticing from the corner of his eye that she was watching the rising smoke with a subtle, unmistakable hunger.
“Do you smoke?” he asked, holding the cigarette loosely in his hand.
“Sometimes,” she admitted softly.
He extended his arm, offering it to her. She hesitated for only the briefest fraction of a second before reaching out to accept it. As she took the cigarette from his grasp, their cold fingers briefly brushed against one another. It was a fleeting, microscopic point of contact. It was absolutely nothing. At least, the rational part of Jack's brain insisted that it should have been nothing, yet a strange, electric thrill raced up his forearm. She took a slow, practised drag, exhaled a plume of pale smoke into the chill air, and gracefully handed it back.
“Thank you.”
“You're very welcome,” he replied, his eyes lingering on her face.
“I'm Ginny, by the way.”
“I am Jack.”
“I know who you are,” she said, offering a small, enigmatic smile.
He looked at her, suddenly acutely aware of how isolated they were. “You live in this building, then? On the third floor?”
“Yes, I do.”
“It's strange, but I don't think I've ever seen you around before.”
“I haven't seen you around, either,” Ginny countered smoothly. “Though you have probably seen my husband in the stairwell.”
“Maybe. What does he look like?”
“He is usually the one frantically carrying something heavy. Anything he can find, really.”
Jack chuckled, the sound rumbling in his chest. “He sounds incredibly useful to have around.”
“Oh, he absolutely loves being useful.”
There was a peculiar, almost melancholic weight to the way she delivered that final sentence that compelled Jack to study her more closely. She was strikingly young, perhaps no older than twenty-one, and her face radiated a careless, luminous brightness that seemed entirely uncorrupted by the heavy caution and grinding fatigue that Jack had acquired over his four decades of life.
“Have you just moved in?” he asked, attempting to place her in the timeline of the building.
“Just a few days ago,” she confirmed, pulling the emerald fabric tighter against the chill.
“With your husband?”
She offered a single, concise nod.
“Well, congratulations on the new place.”
“Thank you, Jack.”
They remained on the freezing rooftop conversing for nearly an hour, though later, when Jack desperately attempted to recall the specifics, he could remember very little of what they had actually discussed. They spoke of the deteriorating building, the unpredictable autumn weather, the sprawling city, their respective labours, and the universally strange, reclusive habits of their neighbours. Ginny offered almost no concrete details regarding her own existence, and Jack, caught in the intoxicating spell of the moment, found himself asking very few probing questions. The interaction felt strangely, dangerously natural. There was absolutely no awkward tension in the prolonged silences between their words, and neither felt the frantic, social compulsion to fill the quiet void with meaningless chatter.
At some unspoken, seamless point during the hour, she drifted closer and briefly rested her dark head against his broad shoulder. Neither of them moved to break the contact. Jack could vividly smell the sweet, intoxicating scent of her perfume, a floral note rising sharply above the damp, earthy smell of the rain and the acrid tang of his burning tobacco. He briefly thought of Julia, broken and sleeping in the dark apartment three floors below. Yet, as he stood there with this strange, beautiful girl leaning against him, Jack felt genuinely young for the very first time in what felt like a century. He did not merely feel younger; he felt reborn, returned to an era before hospitals, before grinding steel, before the crushing weight of duty.
When Ginny finally pulled away and stood to her full height, she cast a lingering look toward the gaping maw of the concrete staircase. “I really should go back down.”
Jack nodded slowly, mourning the loss of her warmth. “Good night, Ginny.”
“Good night, Jack.”
She descended two steps into the gloom, then paused and turned back to look at him over her shoulder, her dark eyes catching the faint city light. “Will you be up here again tomorrow night?”
Jack smiled, a helpless, eager expression. “Probably.”
She returned the smile, a radiant flash in the darkness. “Then I will see you tomorrow.”
After the heavy metal door scraped shut behind her, Jack remained frozen on the rooftop for several long, quiet minutes. He stared blankly at the smouldering cigarette trapped between his fingers and laughed quietly into the night, mocking his own absolute foolishness. He was a forty-two-year-old factory worker. He had a disabled, devoted wife sleeping in the apartment downstairs. He had just spent an hour in the cold conversing with a young, married woman he barely knew, and yet, somehow, this particular night felt fundamentally different from every single night that had preceded it for the past decade. He finally descended the concrete stairs, a guilty, irrepressible smile plastered across his face, entirely unaware of the nightmare he had just invited into his life.
The very next evening, the young woman in the emerald dress was waiting for him, and after that unspoken confirmation, neither of them ever felt the need to ask for permission. Over the following two weeks, the desolate, wind-swept rooftop transformed into a secluded, private world that belonged entirely to them. Jack would wait in the suffocating quiet of the apartment until Julia had dutifully swallowed her medication and surrendered to a deep, chemical slumber, before slipping silently through the front door like a thief in his own life. Ginny would inevitably appear from the shadows of the stairwell, sometimes carrying a fresh pack of cigarettes, once bearing a stolen bottle of cheap wine. The stolen hours they shared suspended above the sleeping city seemed completely severed from the heavy, grinding reality of the world below.
They never encountered one another during the harsh light of day. Jack knew absolutely nothing about where Ginny went, who she truly was, or how she spent the long hours when she was not standing beside him in the dark. He knew only the barest, superficial facts: she possessed a husband, she had recently migrated into the concrete bowels of the building, and she vastly preferred to deflect any probing inquiries regarding her existence.
"You ask entirely too much," she chastised him one evening, her tone light but carrying an edge of finality.
"I have asked you exactly three things about yourself," Jack protested, leaning against the cold parapet.
"Exactly," she replied, offering a strange, hollow smile.
He laughed, easily charmed by her evasion. She was remarkably effortless to be around—strangely submissive yet playfully elusive, maintaining a quiet demeanour that never felt entirely distant, yet never felt entirely human, either. She never demanded clumsy promises of devotion, never inquired about what would happen when the morning came, and never once spoke of a future that neither of them had the courage or intention to imagine. Perhaps that was the very reason Jack found himself plunging so recklessly and rapidly into the affair. With Julia, every single interaction carried the crushing, monumental weight of their shared history. Every spoken word was tethered to fourteen years of marriage; every touch was laced with the memory of the young, unbroken girl she had once been and the agonising reality of the woman she had become. With Ginny, however, there was only the intoxicating, weightless present. The stolen hour they occupied together seemed to exist in a vacuum, entirely outside the ordinary, punishing laws of consequence.
The first time he finally kissed her, neither of them had consciously orchestrated the moment. They had been laughing softly about some trivial absurdity that neither would possess the capacity to recall later. Ginny turned her pale face toward him, the smile still lingering on her lips, and for a long, suspended moment, they simply stared at one another in the gloom. Then, driven by a sudden, desperate hunger, Jack closed the distance and pressed his mouth to hers. She kissed him back with a cool, yielding eagerness, and the sprawling city continued its mechanical rotation far below them, entirely indifferent to his betrayal.
After that crossing of the threshold, the autumn nights seemed to grow perceptibly warmer. Ginny arrived wearing the same simple, emerald nightgown every single evening, a detail Jack grew intimately familiar with without ever consciously questioning the strange repetition. He learned the slick, synthetic texture of the fabric beneath his rough callouses, the way the deep green hue shifted into something almost black under the weak, flickering yellow light of the stairwell, and how she instinctively gathered it around her slender frame whenever the wind developed a bite. He memorised the specific, musical shape of her laughter, the profound, heavy quietness that always followed it, and the trusting way she would occasionally rest her dark head against his chest without uttering a single word. He knew absolutely nothing else about her, and somehow, trapped in the narcotic haze of his own selfish desires, he did not mind the void.
The daylight hours became agonising stretches of time that Jack merely endured because they eventually bled into the night. He found himself smiling vacantly amidst the roaring machinery of the steel factory, entirely without reason. He would return to the apartment utterly exhausted, his muscles screaming, yet his blood humming with a frantic, restless impatience for the sun to finally set. He began taking meticulous, unprecedented care with his grooming and his worn clothes, washing away the factory grime with a renewed, desperate vanity. Yet, through all of this quiet madness, Julia remained firmly anchored at the tragic centre of the life he returned to. He still dutifully retrieved her bitter medicines; he still cooked their meagre dinners; he still supported her fragile weight as she struggled across the faded rugs, and he still pressed a tender, deceitful kiss to her forehead before she closed her eyes. And every single night, the moment her breathing levelled into the deep, rhythmic cadence of sleep, he abandoned her and climbed the concrete stairs.
On the fourteenth night of his deception, Jack left the apartment considerably later than his usual hour. Julia had been gripped by a restless, phantom pain in her spine, thrashing weakly against the sheets, and it had taken far longer for the sedatives to finally drag her under. He waited patiently in the bedside chair until her breathing became slow, heavy, and even, before quietly lacing up his boots. The heavy metal door to the rooftop opened with its familiar, screeching scrape. Ginny was already waiting beneath the crumbling concrete canopy, perfectly still in the shadows. She smiled brilliantly when she saw him emerge, and Jack eagerly returned the expression. For those few, fleeting seconds—before the fabric of his reality violently unspooled—there was absolutely nothing frightening in the world. There was only the sharp, cool night air, the distant, glittering sea of city lights, the comforting scent of burning tobacco, and a beautiful young woman in an emerald dress looking at him as though she had been waiting an eternity for his arrival.
When Jack finally descended from the rooftop, Ginny's sweet, floral perfume was still caught faintly in the rough collar of his shirt. The night had grown significantly colder while they were entwined, and as he navigated the dim, echoing stairwell, he could still feel the phantom warmth of her body pressed against his own, the soft, musical lilt of her voice lingering pleasantly in his thoughts. There was a foolish, buoyant youthfulness in the rhythm of his descent, a lightness in his bones that he had not experienced in over a decade. For a few blissful moments, he allowed himself the luxury of entirely forgetting the suffocating apartment below, the broken, sleeping woman trapped inside it, and the fourteen heavy years that stood like a wall between him and the careless boy he had once been. The concrete building was tomb-quiet around him. A television murmured incoherently behind a locked door, a leaky pipe dripped rhythmically somewhere deep within the dark architecture, and the solitary echo of his own boots followed him down from floor to floor until he finally reached the third-floor corridor.
His apartment door was standing wide open.
Jack froze instantly, his hand still buried deep in his coat pocket, his heart seizing in his chest. He was certain he had locked it. He vividly remembered pulling the heavy wood shut before ascending the stairs, remembered the sharp, metallic click of the deadbolt turning, and remembered dropping the brass key back into his pocket. Yet, the door now stood ajar by several inches, and the impenetrable darkness waiting beyond the narrow gap seemed to possess a heavy, expectant quality.
"Julia?" he called out softly, his voice trembling slightly in the silent hallway.
There was no answer. He pushed the heavy door open with his fingertips, and a foul, suffocating odour immediately spilt out of the apartment—a scent he could not accurately place at first. It smelled strongly of wet, rotting plaster, decaying cloth, decades of undisturbed dust, and beneath all of that, something faintly, sickeningly sweet, like dead flowers left to rot in stagnant water.
The room beyond the threshold was undeniably his, yet it appeared to have been abandoned and dragged far beyond the reach of ordinary time. The worn sofa remained situated against the familiar wall, the small wooden table still stood dutifully beside it, and the radio occupied its usual corner, but absolutely everything had gone pitch-black with profound, unnatural age. A thick, grey skin of filth and dust coated every surface like volcanic ash. The framed photographs still rested upon the sagging shelves, though their silver frames had tarnished into blackened husks, and the glass had clouded so severely with grime that the smiling faces trapped behind it seemed to be sinking away into a murky swamp. The heavy brass clock mounted above the kitchen doorway had violently seized, its hands frozen at some forgotten, dead hour. The walls were weeping, broken, and damp, large chunks of the plaster having rotted and fallen away to expose the skeleton of the building beneath. Dirty, unrecognisable clothes lay scattered violently across the floorboards, as though a deranged scavenger had inhabited the space for years without ever bothering to clean. The curtains were entirely gone. The windowpanes were filmed with a thick, greasy dirt that obscured the outside world. Nothing had been moved from its original place, yet nothing looked as though it belonged to the living world Jack knew.
He forced himself to step inside, his stomach pitching violently. The warped floorboards gave a soft, sickening grit beneath the soles of his shoes. Somewhere deep within the blackened apartment, heavy drops of water fell rhythmically into a hollow vessel with a patient, maddening sound. Jack slowly turned his head toward the display shelf beside the bedroom door, his breath catching in his throat as he saw Julia's beloved collection of dolls still standing exactly where she had left them. But their delicate dresses had faded into a ghostly, rotting colourlessness. Decades of heavy dust had settled deeply into the folds of the brittle fabric and clung to their painted, porcelain faces. One of the dolls had toppled over against its neighbour, its delicate porcelain head tilted backwards at a grotesque, unnatural angle. For a blinding instant, Jack vividly remembered Julia sitting bathed in the warm, golden glow of the lamp, carefully changing their tiny clothes with the pure, absolute concentration of a child caring for something it truly loved. The memory struck him with such sudden, violent grief that he almost called out to her again. Instead, he merely whispered her name into the ruined, suffocating room, and his own frail voice seemed to travel impossibly far into the darkness before being swallowed whole.
The kitchen was infinitely worse. The rusted sink was overflowing with plates, cloudy glasses, and cracked bowls, all encased in a hard, calcified brown crust, while a thick, gelatinous black sludge had pooled aggressively around the drain. The metallic taps were furred with heavy, orange rust. Jack slowly turned his terrified gaze toward the hulking silhouette of the refrigerator, his eyes locking onto the small photograph still pinned beneath its rusted magnet: himself and Julia, smiling brightly in the summer sun, looking impossibly young and thin as they stood proudly beside the car that had long since been destroyed. The glossy paper had curled and turned a sickly, jaundiced yellow. A massive, dark stain resembling dried blood was smeared violently across one corner, and Jack instinctively reached out toward it before freezing in terror. He could not rationally explain why his hand had begun to shake so violently. He wrenched his gaze away from the ruined memory and noticed the bedroom door standing half-open, a gaping black maw in the wall.
The bed remained exactly where it had always been, but the heavy metal frame had oxidised into a terrifying, blackened husk of rust, and the mattress had severely collapsed in the centre, resembling a shallow grave. The bedsheets were a sickly, mottled yellow—stiff, stained, and encrusted with filth. Dust lay along the ornate headboard in a thick, undisturbed ridge. Jack stood paralysed beside the ruined bed and strained his ears, listening to the crushing silence. At first, there was absolutely nothing. Then, emanating from somewhere incredibly close to the opaque windowpane, he heard a faint, horrifying sound—a high, scratching noise, exactly like a jagged fingernail being drawn agonisingly slowly across a pane of glass. He whirled around, his heart hammering against his ribs.
The bedroom window was almost completely sealed with decades of baked-on dirt, allowing only a sickly, pale-yellow smear of light from the streetlamp below to seep through the gloom. Trembling uncontrollably, Jack raised his palm and desperately wiped away a circle of the greasy grime. The outside world instantly appeared in fragmented, surreal pieces—the towering lamp post, the wet asphalt road, the dark, imposing fronts of the buildings standing opposite. And then, a distinct shape emerged from the shadows directly beneath the halo of the streetlight.
A woman was standing there on the pavement.
Jack leaned closer, his face pressing against the freezing glass.
It was Julia.
She was wearing the same clothes she had been wearing earlier that very evening. Her dark hair cascaded loosely around her shoulders, and she stood positioned directly beneath the harsh yellow light with both feet planted firmly, solidly on the wet pavement. Jack's shaking hand remained glued to the freezing glass. Just that morning, he had watched in agony as Julia fiercely struggled to cross the minuscule distance of their living room. He had watched her desperately grip the heavy furniture to steady her trembling frame, watched the raw pain gather and darken her face after taking only a few agonising steps, and watched her collapse into a chair because her shattered spine simply would not carry her a single inch farther. Yet now, she stood alone in the desolate street as easily and effortlessly as she had in the years before the horrific accident—perfectly straight, perfectly balanced, and perfectly still.
Slowly, deliberately, she raised her pale face.
She was looking directly at him.
Jack could not force his muscles to move. The physical distance separating them was a span of several floors, yet he was seized by the unmistakable, horrifying sensation that she was looking not merely toward the third-floor window, but directly through it, as though the heavy, dirty glass and the suffocating darkness of the room simply did not exist. Then, slowly, Julia smiled. It was not the warm, welcoming smile she offered him when he returned home from the factory, nor was it the shy, embarrassed little grin she displayed when he gently teased her. It was something entirely different—softer, colder, and infinitely sadder. Her dark, unblinking eyes remained fiercely locked onto his, and the expression frozen upon her face possessed the terrible, haunting tenderness of someone finally laying eyes upon a deeply familiar person after having waited for them for a very, very long time.
Jack violently scrambled backwards, his boots slipping on the gritty floorboards. Julia remained perfectly still beneath the glowing streetlight, a silent sentinel in the dark. He looked down at her strong, unbroken legs once more, his mind thrashing desperately, frantically searching for some logical mistake, some rational explanation his fractured mind could latch on to. There was absolutely none. The sheer, impossible reality of her standing there was so fundamentally simple that it became instantly unbearable. She did not twitch. She did not call out his name. She simply continued to aim that sorrowful, knowing smile directly at him from the wet street, while the yellow sodium light above her flickered once, briefly casting long, macabre shadows across her pale face.
Jack stumbled blindly out of the ruined bedroom, hitting his shoulder hard against the rotting doorframe. He ran.
He had no conscious memory of tearing the heavy rooftop door open. He only registered the freezing night air violently striking his sweating face and the ragged, desperate sound of his own breathing echoing loudly against the darkness. For several frantic seconds, he stood paralysed beneath the crumbling concrete canopy, staring wildly out at the sprawling city. It was the same world he had left moments ago. The same flat rooftops stretched away into the gloom around him. The same distant, scattered windows burned hotly through the night. Somewhere far below, a subterranean train passed with a long, metallic, agonising groan. Jack laughed once—a sharp, quiet sound entirely devoid of humour or sanity—simply because the pathetic sound of his own voice was infinitely easier to bear than the crushing silence.
And then, he felt the distinct, undeniable sensation of someone watching him.
It was not a passing thought or a trick of the wind. It was a physical, oppressive presence so incredibly immediate and heavy that every single muscle in his body seized and locked before he could even attempt to turn around. Moving with agonising slowness, Jack reached a trembling hand deep into his coat pocket and withdrew his silver lighter. His numb thumb struck the flint wheel. The small flame roared to life, rising in a trembling, desperate tear of orange light, and in that brief, flickering illumination, a face appeared floating merely inches away from his own.
Julia.
Jack recoiled with a strangled cry, his spine slamming brutally against the rough concrete wall behind him. She stood there before him in the darkness, as utterly quiet and motionless as if she had been patiently waiting for him to arrive all night. Her dark hair shifted slightly, lifted by the freezing wind. Her clothes were the same. Her pale, flawless face was illuminated by the weak, trembling flame, and that exact same sorrowful, knowing smile remained eternally etched upon her lips. She was standing close enough for Jack to see the tiny, intricate details of her dark irises, close enough to perfectly recognise the face of the young girl he had married, the broken woman who supposedly still slept in the apartment only a few floors below him, the person whose frail hand he had held tightly through endless hospital corridors and agonising, sleepless nights. Yet, there was something fundamentally, terribly wrong in the way she stood before him now. She made absolutely no sound. She did not breathe. She did not blink. She simply watched him with an ancient, terrifying patience.
"Julia," he whispered, his voice cracking into a sob.
Her terrible smile did not alter by a single fraction.
The silver lighter trembled violently in his grip. The unlit cigarette slipped from his numb fingers and landed silently on the concrete, its tip rolling once before disappearing into the dark. Jack stared into her unblinking eyes until his own began to overflow with hot, blinding tears. He did not fully understand why he was weeping. He only knew, with absolute certainty, that for one brief, agonising instant, she looked so unbearably familiar, so impossibly beautiful and whole, that it made the sight of her infinitely more terrifying than if she had been some grotesque monster he could not recognise.
The fragile flame flickered and died. The darkness swallowed her whole.
Gasping for air, Jack frantically struck the flint wheel again, sparks showering over his knuckles. The flame flared back to life.
The rooftop was empty. The tiny, trembling light illuminated the crumbling concrete canopy, the slick, damp floor, the low parapet wall, and the gaping maw of the dark staircase. Nothing stood where his wife had been seconds before. Jack kept the lighter burning fiercely until the hot metal scorched his fingers, forcing him to drop his hand. The city below continued its indifferent rotation exactly as before. Cars moved silently along distant, glowing arteries. A train rumbled across the dark horizon. Somewhere in the sprawling labyrinth, a dog barked once and fell completely silent. The world had not changed at all, yet Jack knew his reality had been irreversibly shattered.
He sprinted downstairs, taking the concrete steps recklessly in the dark.
When he reached the third-floor landing, he lunged for his apartment door and found it firmly locked. His hands were shaking with such violent tremors that he dropped the brass key, clattering onto the tiles, before finally managing to jam it into the lock. He twisted the knob and shoved the door inward. Warm, comforting, yellow light instantly spilt across the corridor, and Jack froze on the threshold, unable to take a single step inside.
The apartment was exactly as it had always been. The plaster walls were pristine and clean. The floral curtains hung neatly beside the clear windows. The worn sofa was situated precisely where it belonged, the radio sat quietly humming on the side table, and the framed photographs shone brightly beneath the glow of the corner lamp. Julia's porcelain dolls stood neatly arranged on their polished shelf, clothed in the colourful garments she had painstakingly chosen for them. Wafting from the small kitchen was the faint, lingering, completely normal smell of their evening dinner.
Jack slowly turned his terrified gaze toward the bedroom door.
Julia was asleep.
She lay peacefully on her side beneath the heavy woollen blanket, her face turned away toward the wall, one frail hand tucked safely beneath her cheek. Her breathing was slow, rhythmic, and even. She looked incredibly peaceful. She looked entirely alive.
Jack remained frozen in the doorway for a very long time, his mind struggling to reconcile the horror of the decayed room with the pristine reality before him, before finally forcing himself to walk inside. He crept into the bedroom and slowly lowered himself into the chair beside her bed, terrified that any sudden movement might shatter the illusion. His wide, bloodshot eyes moved obsessively over the shape of her ruined legs hidden beneath the blanket, and then travelled back up to her peaceful face. Julia stirred slightly in the depths of her sleep, letting out a soft sigh, and drew the warm blanket a little closer to her chin. Trembling, Jack reached out and gently laid a hand against her hair. She felt warm. She felt real. She did not wake. He left his hand resting there for a long time, anchoring himself to the slow rhythm of her breathing, while somewhere out in the brightly lit living room, the heavy brass clock began its patient ticking once again.
Jack sat rigidly in the chair beside her until the sun rose. He never slept a single wink.
Morning arrived without offering any tangible explanation or relief. The first weak rays of light entered the bedroom through the floral curtains in a thin, grey band, and for several agonising seconds, Jack remained exactly where he had been all night, listening intently to the incredibly familiar, mundane sounds of the apartment building waking up around him. A heavy transit bus groaned as it accelerated in the street below. Water pipes shifted and clanged inside the plaster walls. From the kitchen came the faint, metallic tick of the cooling stove. Julia slept soundly with her face now turned toward him, her breathing quiet and wonderfully regular, and when she finally fluttered her dark eyes open, she looked at him with the endearing, sleepy confusion of someone who had completely forgotten what time it was.
"You didn't sleep again, Jacky," she noted, her voice raspy.
Jack forced a warm smile and aggressively rubbed his burning eyes. "I slept fine."
"You are a terrible liar."
"I've been practising."
She smiled softly, and the small, familiar joke was powerful enough to make the terrors of the previous night feel suddenly distant and absurd. Jack carefully helped her sit upright against the pillows, retrieved the necessary morning medicine, and threw open the curtains. Bright, golden sunlight flooded across the room, touching the glassy eyes of the dolls on their shelf, glinting off the polished photographs, the brass clock, and the small vase of dying flowers beside the window. Absolutely everything was exactly where it belonged in the world. The apartment smelled wonderfully of fresh coffee and the lingering spices of yesterday's dinner. Julia grumbled that her hair was an absolute disaster, and Jack gently teased her that she had always looked considerably worse in the mornings. She threw a small pillow at his head and missed terribly. For the first time since sprinting down from the terrifying darkness of the rooftop, he laughed—a genuine, hearty sound that filled the room.
He did not venture to the steel factory that day. He telephoned the foreman, claiming a sudden illness, and instead spent the entire morning performing his usual domestic rituals around the apartment while Julia moved painfully, slowly from one room to another. He aggressively washed the dishes until they shone, swept the floorboards, changed the bedsheets, and tenderly helped her bathe. Around noon, they ate a quiet lunch together near the open kitchen window, and Julia spent several long minutes recounting a story about a woman she had once known in high school—a woman whose name Jack could no longer recall. He listened intently anyway. There were vast, stretching years of their marriage that consisted of precisely this quiet intimacy: one person speaking earnestly about something incredibly small, and the other person listening simply because the accumulation of those small things was what truly made up a life.
After lunch, Julia settled into her chair by the window with one of the blank-faced dolls in her lap and began the slow, meticulous process of changing its dress. Jack stood silently in the kitchen doorway, watching her work. The sight was so painfully, beautifully ordinary that it physically hurt his chest. He thought obsessively about the woman standing perfectly whole beneath the yellow streetlight, the terrifying, rotting decay of the alternate apartment, the sorrowful face that had manifested inside the trembling flame of his lighter, and none of it seemed capable of surviving the clean, harsh scrutiny of the daylight. Perhaps it truly had occurred simply because his mind was fracturing under the exhaustion. Perhaps the isolation of the rooftop, the biting cold, the guilt of his betrayal, the cigarettes, and the cheap wine had finally conspired to break something deep within his head. He did not tell Julia a single word about it. There are certain terrifying things a person keeps completely silent about—not because they are unbelievable, but because saying the words aloud gives the nightmare a permanent, physical place in the waking world.
By the time evening arrived, Jack had almost entirely succeeded in convincing himself it was all a hallucination. He cooked a hot dinner while Julia sat comfortably at the wooden table and watched his hands work, occasionally chiding him when he was too generous with the salt. Afterwards, she dutifully swallowed her medication and retreated to bed early, her energy depleted. Jack sat vigil beside her until her heavy eyelids fluttered closed. He waited just a little longer in the dark, listening intently as her breathing settled into the deep, unnaturally heavy rhythm the sedatives always induced, before quietly slipping out of the bedroom.
The rooftop was waiting.
Jack climbed the concrete stairs with a fresh cigarette trembling between his fingers and shoved open the heavy metal door. The night air was surprisingly mild, and the sprawling city below was brilliantly illuminated by thousands of scattered, glowing windows. He stood beneath the crumbling covered section, smoking nervously, and caught himself looking toward the dark opening of the staircase far more frequently than he intended to. He furiously smoked one cigarette down to the filter, then immediately lit another. He desperately told himself he was only lingering up there because he had simply become accustomed to the routine of seeing Ginny.
She did not come.
Ten agonising minutes passed. Then twenty. Jack checked the luminous dial of his watch and laughed softly, mockingly, at his own pathetic desperation. He finished the second cigarette, dropped it, and ground the ember viciously beneath the heel of his shoe. Perhaps she had gone out into the city with her husband. Perhaps she had been preoccupied with domestic chores. Perhaps she had simply grown bored and changed her mind about the affair. The possibilities were wonderfully, safely ordinary, and Jack was profoundly grateful for their mundane logic.
He finally surrendered and went downstairs.
When he reached the third-floor landing, he approached his apartment and retrieved his brass key. He slid it smoothly into the lock, but the cylinder stubbornly refused to turn. Jack frowned, jiggled the key, tried again with more force, and then pressed his weight against the brass handle.
It was locked from the inside.
He stood frozen in the corridor for a long moment, staring blankly at the solid wood.
Julia was deeply asleep; she could not possibly have walked to the door to throw the interior deadbolt.
He knocked softly, rapping his knuckles against the wood. "Julia?"
There was no answer.
He knocked again, significantly louder this time, his anxiety spiking. "Julia? Open the door."
Nothing but silence.
Jack felt the first icy movement of primal uneasiness slither slowly down his spine. He aggressively rattled the brass handle once more, then leaned his ear close to the wooden panel. Through the thick door, he could hear the faint, rhythmic ticking of the brass clock in the living room, marking the seconds.
He raised his fist, preparing to hammer violently against the door, when a hand gently touched his shoulder.
Jack whirled around, his heart leaping into his throat.
