The mornings came cold to Ooty. Before the sun had found the hills, the mist descended upon the town and gathered in the hollows between houses, along the eucalyptus trees and over the narrow roads that wound downward toward the valley. From the window of her little rented room, Aisha could see the roofs of two neighbouring houses and, beyond them, on a clear morning, a dark green slope rising into the distance. On most mornings there was nothing to see at all. The glass would be white with condensation, and the world beyond it seemed to have withdrawn during the night, leaving only the faint sound of buses labouring up the roads and the occasional ringing of a church bell from somewhere inside the fog.
She usually woke a few minutes before the alarm. The room was coldest at that hour, and for a while she remained beneath the blanket listening to the house come awake around her. A tap dripped somewhere below; a woman coughed behind a closed door; a child began crying and was comforted by a voice too soft for Aisha to make out. These sounds had become as familiar to her as the things she carried in her own room. She would eventually stretch one hand beneath her pillow and take out her watch, an old brown-leather thing with a cracked strap and a thin scratch across the glass. Her father had given it to her before she left home for college, when a watch had seemed like a meaningful gift and not simply an object that told a person how late she was becoming. She still wore it every day, though the leather had darkened with years of rain and sweat and the buckle had begun to loosen.
One morning, while the kettle was heating on the small stove, she opened her purse and counted the money inside. There were three notes and a handful of coins. She arranged them on the table, counted them again, then put them back exactly where she had found them. The kettle began to whistle. Aisha poured the water into a cup, added the little milk she had left and stood by the window while she drank. Her phone rang before she had finished. It was her mother, and Aisha answered with the brightness she had learned to put into her voice whenever the call came from home.
“Good morning, Ma.”
“Have you eaten?”
“I’m having tea.”
“You always say tea. You have to eat properly.”
“I will.”
There was the familiar household noise behind her mother's voice: a vessel being moved, a door closing, one of Aisha's brothers speaking from another room. Her mother seemed to be listening to him at the same time she was speaking to Aisha, and when she returned to the conversation, her voice had changed slightly.
“Aisha, I wanted to send you some money this week.”
Aisha looked at the three notes lying on the table.
“Don't send it.”
“What?”
“I have enough.”
There was a pause.
“Your rent is due, isn't it?”
“I've already managed it.”
She had not. Her mother knew enough about her daughter to suspect this, but perhaps not enough to insist. There was another voice calling from inside the house, louder this time, and Aisha heard her mother turn away to answer it.
“Your brother needs to pay his college fees,” her mother said when she returned. “Your father's salary is delayed again.
“I know.”
“I'll send you something next month.”
“Don't worry about it, Ma. I have tuition.”
Her mother was quiet for a moment. “Are you sure?”
Aisha smiled, though nobody could see her.
“I'm sure.”
The call ended with the usual instructions to eat well, sleep properly and take care of herself. Aisha placed the phone on the table and stood there for a while, looking at the money she had just lied about. Then she folded the notes into her purse, put the purse into her bag and left for college.
At St. Joseph's College of Education, the mornings belonged to another world. The corridors filled quickly with the voices of students, the smell of wet clothes and chalk dust, the scrape of chairs across classroom floors. Aisha moved among them quietly, usually with a notebook pressed against her chest and her hair tied back. She was near the end of her B.Ed., and her notebooks were increasingly full of lesson plans, teaching methods and observations of children. There was a seriousness in the way she approached those things that even her classmates occasionally noticed.
During one practical class, the students were asked to take turns teaching a short lesson before the others. When Aisha stood at the front of the room, she forgot for a little while about the money in her purse, the conversation with her mother and the damp patch near the window of her rented room. She held the chalk lightly between her fingers and began to speak. One of her classmates interrupted her deliberately, pretending not to understand the lesson, and Aisha stopped, thought for a moment and explained it again in another way. The girl interrupted once more. Aisha smiled and tried a third explanation, and this time the room laughed because the exercise had become more amusing than difficult.
When it was over, the professor looked at her for a moment before speaking.
“You've taught before?”
“Tuition, sir.”
He nodded. “You're good with children.”
The words were ordinary, but they stayed with her. On the walk home that afternoon, she found herself imagining the classroom she might have one day: rows of wooden desks, children shifting impatiently before lunch, the smell of chalk and old books, her own name written on a timetable outside the door. She imagined receiving a salary at the end of the month and knowing, before she opened her purse, that there would be enough for the next week. It was not a grand life, but there was something deeply comforting about its modest certainty.
Her tuition work began in the evenings. On some days she taught in a flat overlooking the market; on others she walked farther uphill to a house where two brothers were supposed to study mathematics under her supervision. The younger one disliked numbers with an almost personal hatred and would stare at his exercises as though they had been designed to insult him. Aisha would sit beside him, erase the wrong answer and begin again without raising her voice.
“Try it once more.”
“I don't understand.”
“You understood it yesterday.”
“I forgot.”
“Then we'll remember it again.”
He would sigh and bend over the notebook, and Aisha would lean back in the chair while he worked. She liked these children more than she expected to. There was something honest about teaching them. When they did not understand, they said so. When they were bored, they showed it. When they were pleased with themselves, they did not know how to hide it. With them she never had to wonder whether somebody was merely being polite.
One evening, after the lesson, the children's mother came to the doorway while Aisha was putting the notebooks into her bag.
“Aisha, I'll give you the payment next week.”
Aisha looked up.
“Of course.”
“I'm sorry. Some expenses came up.”
“It's all right.”
The woman thanked her, relieved, and went back inside. Aisha finished putting away the notebooks, thanked the children and stepped into the evening.
She did not have enough money for the bus, so she walked.
The market was glowing by then. Shop signs shone through the mist, headlights moved slowly along the wet road, and the smell of frying food drifted from a stall somewhere near the corner. Aisha passed a clothing shop whose entire front window had been given to a single advertisement. A young woman in a deep blue saree stood against a pale background, her head turned slightly away from the camera. She was beautiful in the impersonal, perfected manner of people who had been made beautiful for strangers. The fabric seemed almost to move despite being printed on paper.
Aisha had passed that advertisement many times.
That evening she stopped.
People moved around her, carrying shopping bags and umbrellas, and nobody paid any attention to the woman in the window. Yet Aisha stood looking at her face for longer than she intended. She wondered what the woman had looked like before the photograph was taken, whether she had been nervous, whether someone had adjusted the saree around her shoulders, whether she had laughed between photographs. Then, without meaning to, Aisha imagined her own face in the advertisement.
The thought was so vivid that she almost smiled.
For a few seconds she allowed herself to imagine it properly. Her own face beneath the lights, her hair arranged beautifully, a saree falling perfectly around her, strangers passing the shop and seeing her without knowing anything about her life. She imagined a magazine cover next, and then a cinema poster, and then some expensive advertisement in which a woman like her was required to look into the distance as though she knew something the rest of the world did not. The fantasy was ridiculous, and because it was ridiculous she loved it.
A bus sounded its horn behind her.
Aisha stepped away from the window and continued walking.
By the time she reached her room, she had almost convinced herself she had forgotten the whole thing.
She had not.
There were other places where the same private habit returned. Sometimes it happened while she was travelling to college and saw an actress smiling from a cinema poster. Sometimes it was a jewellery advertisement on the side of a bus, or a shampoo campaign above a shop, or a fashion photograph in a magazine left behind in a waiting room. She never told anyone about it. It was not quite a dream, because she knew too well how unlikely such a life was, but neither was it merely imagination. It was something she carried quietly, like a letter written to herself and never posted.
At night, when she was alone, she sometimes stood before the mirror after washing her face and looked at herself for a little longer than necessary. She did not think herself extraordinary. She knew the difference between her face and the faces that appeared in advertisements, and she knew how much money, styling, lighting and chance could change a person. Still, there were moments when she caught herself from a certain angle and wondered what she might look like through a professional camera.
Then she would laugh at herself and switch off the light.
A few evenings later, the rain came down heavily.
Aisha returned from tuition with wet sandals and the hem of her kurta darkened by the road. She left her shoes outside, changed into an old cotton nightdress and sat on the edge of her bed without lighting the room. Rain struck the window in a steady rush, and beyond the glass the hills had disappeared completely. She could hear water running from the roof into the drain and, farther away, the occasional sound of a vehicle moving cautiously through the wet streets.
She had very little food left.
She made tea instead.
Her phone was beside her on the bed, and after a while she opened Facebook. She moved through the local groups almost without thinking: rooms for rent, tuition requests, second-hand furniture, part-time work, lost pets, people selling things they no longer needed. She had joined so many of these groups that she no longer remembered the names of half of them.
Then a photograph caught her attention.
A woman in an embroidered saree stood against a dark green background. The picture was simple, but the woman's expression held her for a moment. She did not look as though she were smiling for a camera. She looked as though she had been interrupted in the middle of a thought.
Below the photograph was an advertisement.
A photographer was looking for a female model for an ethnic fashion shoot in Ooty. No professional experience was necessary. Clothing would be provided. Payment would be made after the shoot. The photographs were intended for an annual magazine.
Aisha read the post slowly. She looked at the amount being offered. Then she looked at her purse.
She knew immediately what that money could do. She could pay the rent without pretending she had already paid it. She could buy groceries without counting each item before placing it in the basket. She could perhaps send something home instead of always being the one who needed something. The practical part of her mind understood the offer quickly.
But there was another part of her that had already begun imagining the camera.
She opened the photographer's page.
His name was Gustav Fechner.
There were years of photographs. Landscapes first: mountains, lakes, forests, old houses. Then portraits. Women in sarees, women beside windows, women beneath trees, women standing in rooms whose walls seemed older than the people inside them. Aisha moved from photograph to photograph, increasingly absorbed. She could not have explained what made the portraits different from the advertisements she usually admired. Perhaps it was simply that the women seemed to possess private lives even inside the frame. They looked as though they had brought something with them that the camera had not been able to take away.
Aisha enlarged one photograph. The woman's eyes filled the screen.
Without thinking, Aisha lifted her free hand and touched her own cheek.
The gesture embarrassed her. She lowered her hand.
For several minutes she sat listening to the rain, with the telephone number beneath the advertisement glowing on the screen. Her practical mind offered its objections one by one. She had never modelled. She knew nothing about photography. She did not know this man. The shoot was to take place at a private residence. She should probably ignore the whole thing.
She read the advertisement again. Then she looked at the photographs.
The woman in the blue saree came back into her mind. So did the professor's voice telling her she was good with children. So did her mother's promise that next month would be easier. She thought of the unpaid tuition. The three notes in her purse. The old watch around her wrist. She thought of the life she was building, sensible and decent and probably sufficient, and beneath it, almost hidden from herself, she felt the other desire stirring again.
Perhaps there was nothing wrong with wanting to know what it felt like. Perhaps one photograph was all she wanted. Perhaps she could stand beneath those lights just once and discover whether the face she saw in the mirror was the same face a stranger might see.
Aisha looked at the number. Her thumb rested upon it.
She hesitated, then pressed.
The phone began to ring. She listened to it for a moment, almost relieved when nobody answered immediately. On the third ring, she was about to disconnect when the call was picked up.
“Hello?”
The man's voice was calm, low and cultivated, with none of the hurried uncertainty she had expected.
Aisha sat straighter.
“Hello. I'm calling about the advertisement.”
There was a short silence.
Outside, the rain continued to fall over the hills, washing the roofs and the darkened roads beneath them. Aisha held the phone against her ear and looked down at the old watch on her wrist, its scratched glass catching the weak light from the room.
Then the man spoke again.
“Yes. I've been expecting someone to call.”
Aisha smiled, though Gustav could not see her. His voice was calm and measured, neither hurried nor overly familiar, and the slight refinement in his accent gave his words an assurance she found reassuring.
“I'm calling about the modelling advertisement.”
“Yes. Your name?”
“Aisha.”
“Have you done professional modelling before?”
“No.”
“That isn't a problem. The shoot is for an annual ethnic-fashion magazine. Traditional clothing and portrait photography. I will guide you through everything.”
“All right.”
“The shoot will be at my house near Doddabetta Peak. Five o'clock tomorrow afternoon. There is a large red mansion with a steel gate and a sign reading Mr Gustav Fechner. A butler will meet you outside.”
“A butler?”
Gustav gave a quiet laugh.
“Yes. I know it sounds rather formal. You'll get used to it.”
Aisha smiled.
“Do I need to bring anything?”
“Nothing. The clothing, jewellery and everything else will be provided. Come comfortably.”
“And the payment?”
He told her the amount.
Aisha looked down at the three notes lying on her table. The sum Gustav had mentioned was more than she had expected, enough to settle her rent, buy groceries for several weeks and put something aside for the books she needed before her final teaching assessments.
“All right. I'll come.”
“I'll send you the address.”
“Thank you.”
“See you tomorrow, Aisha.”
The call ended.
Aisha remained sitting on the bed for a while, looking at the dark screen of her phone. She had expected to feel nervous after agreeing, but what she felt instead was a strange mixture of relief and anticipation. The money would do more than make the next few weeks easier. It would mean that she would not have to call her mother again and hear that small hesitation before her mother answered, the hesitation that came whenever there was some other expense at home that needed to be considered first. There were two younger brothers, two sets of fees and books and clothes, and a father whose salary had become increasingly uncertain. Aisha had never been angry about any of it. She simply hated the feeling of becoming another calculation in a household that was already calculating everything.
She opened the message Gustav had sent.
The address appeared first, followed by a photograph of the house.
Aisha enlarged it.
The mansion stood beneath a pale sky, its walls an old, deep red that seemed almost brown where the weather had darkened them. It was larger than she had imagined, with long windows, high stone steps and a roof whose edges disappeared among tall trees. One side of the building rose above a slope, and from that height the green folds of the hills could be seen spreading away into the distance. There was nothing ostentatious about it. Its grandeur came from age, from proportion, from the impression that it had been standing there long enough to become part of the landscape.
She noticed one of the upper windows.
A pale shape showed behind the glass.
It might have been a curtain. It might have been a reflection. She enlarged the photograph again, but the image only became blurred. For a fleeting moment she had the peculiar sensation that she was looking not at a window but into a room, and that something inside the room had been standing there when the photograph was taken.
She lowered the phone and stared at the photograph for another few seconds.
Then she laughed quietly at herself.
It was only a photograph.
She saved the address.
The following day seemed unusually long. At college, Aisha found herself reading the same paragraph twice without remembering what it said, and during practical teaching she caught herself wondering what Gustav's photography room would look like, whether the lights would be as bright as those she had seen in films and whether she would know what to do with her hands. She had never considered herself particularly shy, but the prospect of being photographed made her suddenly aware of every small imperfection she usually ignored: the shape of her mouth when she smiled, the way her hair separated near the forehead, the slight unevenness of her eyebrows. Beneath the nervousness, however, there was a private excitement she could not entirely suppress. For once, someone had looked at her and thought she might belong in front of a camera.
By the time she returned to her room, she had changed her mind several times about whether she was doing something sensible. She bathed, washed her hair and dressed with unusual care. She chose a simple salwar suit, checked her purse twice and wound her old watch around her wrist. Before leaving, she stood before the mirror and studied herself. She turned slightly to one side, then the other, lifted her chin and lowered it again, loosened her hair and gathered it over one shoulder. None of the movements felt natural, and after a minute she became embarrassed by herself.
Then she remembered the payment.
She remembered her mother's voice.
She remembered the blue-saree advertisement in the market and the ridiculous private pleasure she had taken in imagining her own face where the model's face was.
Aisha picked up her bag and left.
The road toward Doddabetta climbed steadily.
The afternoon was already beginning to lose its brightness. Ooty had a different character as the day declined; the shops remained open, and vehicles continued to pass, but the hills seemed to withdraw from the town one layer at a time. The familiar sounds grew softer as Aisha travelled farther upward. Houses became less frequent, their gardens larger, their walls older. Eucalyptus trees stood along the roads with their pale trunks shining through the gathering mist, and the smell of damp earth replaced the smells of the market below.
Her cab turned away from the busier road, and Aisha looked through the window at the road winding upward between trees and stone walls. She could no longer see the town clearly. Mist had begun to settle among the branches, and the last daylight came through it in thin, colourless bands. At several points the driver slowed almost to a crawl because the bends were too sharp to take quickly. Houses appeared and vanished between the trees, each one more distant from the road than the last, until the feeling of the town seemed to disappear altogether.
“First time here?” the driver asked.
“Yes.”
“Visiting someone?”
“A photographer.”
He glanced at her in the mirror.
“Photographer?”
“Yes. Gustav Fechner.”
Something changed in his expression. It was slight enough that Aisha might have imagined it, but his eyes remained on the road while his mouth tightened for a moment, and the fingers of one hand shifted against the steering wheel.
“You know him?”
“No.”
He looked ahead again.
The answer should have ended the matter, and it did. Neither of them spoke for several minutes, yet Aisha found herself watching him in the mirror, wondering whether she had heard something in his silence that was not there. She almost asked what he had meant by the expression, but the question seemed foolish before it reached her lips. Perhaps the name had simply sounded unfamiliar. Perhaps he had mistaken Gustav for somebody else. There was always an innocent explanation for a stranger's expression, and Aisha had spent enough of her life learning not to make trouble out of things she could not prove.
The cab continued upward.
Once, as they rounded a bend, she caught sight of Ooty below them. The town lay in the valley like a scattering of dim lights beneath the enormous darkening hills. For a moment it seemed very far away, and the sight gave her an unexpected sensation of being separated from everything she knew. She thought of her little room, her college notebooks, the two boys who would be waiting for tuition later in the week, and the ordinary future she had always imagined for herself. She thought of standing before a classroom with a piece of chalk in her hand, earning enough to live without counting coins before breakfast. That life was still what she wanted, but as the town disappeared below the mist, she felt another possibility opening beside it, smaller and more glittering and much harder to resist.
She checked her watch.
4:43.
She had seventeen minutes.
The cab climbed higher. Then, beyond a bend lined with old trees, the mansion appeared.
Aisha knew it immediately from the photograph.
The house stood behind a long stone boundary wall, its red exterior rising through the mist with an almost solemn grandeur. Several tall windows reflected the last pale light of evening, while others had already become dark squares beneath the trees. The garden beyond the wall was large and strangely still, with old trees standing close together as though they had grown there without ever being disturbed. The steel gate was set between two stone pillars, and on the right-hand side, fixed against the wall, was a small metal sign.
MR. GUSTAV FECHNER
The letters were black.
The cab stopped. Aisha paid the driver and stepped out
For a moment she remained beside the cab, adjusting the strap of her bag while the driver waited with one hand resting on the gearshift. She looked toward the mansion, then back at him.
“Thank you.”
He nodded.
The cab turned around almost immediately. Aisha watched it descend the road, its red taillights becoming smaller between the trees until the mist swallowed them. Only then did she realise how quiet it had become.
The silence was not complete; the wind moved through the eucalyptus trees, and somewhere far below she could hear the faint murmur of Ooty. Yet the grounds themselves seemed to hold their breath. There was no sound of a television, no dog barking behind the wall, no voice from the house, no clatter of dishes or footsteps coming to meet a visitor. Even the garden seemed strangely without life.
Aisha looked at her watch.
4:58.
She waited.
The wind shifted.
Something moved inside the mansion. Behind the nearest upper window, the curtain seemed to draw slowly inward and then fall back into place, although the windows were closed. Aisha watched it for several seconds. The movement had been gentle, almost delicate, and there was nothing remarkable about it except that every other curtain in the house remained perfectly still.
She told herself it was an air current.
Then she heard something else.
