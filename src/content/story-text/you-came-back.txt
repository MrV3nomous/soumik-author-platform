Long before the name Meridian Reclamation Services ever crossed his periphery, Edward had surrendered to the geology of isolation. He understood that silence was not merely an absence of noise, but a physical sediment that gathered over the years, pooling in the corners of empty rooms and settling heavily upon the furniture. An unoccupied house speaks in a desolate, mechanical vocabulary: the sudden, percussive shifting of copper pipes behind the plaster, the low, tremulous hum of the refrigerator waking in the dark, the erratic drumming of rain against the glass, and the agonising groan of floorboards contracting in the winter cold. Edward was fluent in this dead language. He knew precisely which tread on the staircase would betray his weight in the dead of night, and which window casement shuddered when the northern winds descended. Yet, the silence he had grown to tolerate was something infinitely more profound than the mere settling of timber and stone. It was the crushing, atmospheric void left behind by the cessation of another human life.
He occupied a modest, slate-roofed dwelling on the fringes of a Scottish town, perched where the orderly geometry of asphalt yielded abruptly to the bruising elevation of the moors. By the depths of November, darkness aggressively reclaimed the afternoons. At four o'clock, the windows ceased to be glass and became black mirrors, reflecting only the hollow topography of his own existence; by six, the streets beyond were swept entirely bare of life. Edward’s evenings were studies in inertia. He rarely ventured past his front door after supper, opting instead to read pages he would immediately forget, or to sit motionless in the armchair beside the radiator, anchoring himself to a porcelain cup until the tea within it went cold. His wife had been dead for a decade. His children had departed long ago, consumed by the frantic architectures of their own adult lives. For Edward, these were no longer subjects of active mourning; they were simply the architecture of his reality. He had learned that there is no utility in attempting to force grief into neat, manageable prose. When left undisturbed for long enough, loss loses its serrated edges. It petrifies. It becomes as unremarkable and permanent as the heavy oak sideboard in the hall. He had merely learned to navigate the negative space it left behind.
It was on one such rain-slicked evening, amidst the aimless, numbing digital wandering that had come to define his nights, that the advertisement materialised.
It commanded only a fraction of the screen, yet its starkness arrested his scrolling entirely. There were no stock images of clasped hands, no sunlit clouds, no grandiose, spiritual platitudes peddling the afterlife or contact beyond the veil. The advertisement was violently plain, stripped of all sentimentality.
MERIDIAN RECLAMATION SERVICES
Private bereavement consultation and controlled communication.
For certain forms of loss, communication may remain possible under appropriate conditions.
A solitary telephone number sat beneath the minimalist typeface. Edward read the words twice, his pulse quickening in a way he had not felt in years. He closed the browser. He sat entirely paralysed at his desk for the better part of an hour, listening to the rain carve jagged, silver paths across the dark glass of his study window, before his trembling hand reached out and reopened the page.
The website offered no comfort, which is precisely what drew him in. Meridian described itself as a private, highly classified organisation serving a strictly vetted clientele under ironclad non-disclosure agreements. The language was ruthlessly clinical, filled with sterile references to neurological monitoring, induced physiological states, temporary host participation, and acute post-session observation. Nowhere did it explain the actual mechanics of the communication. Edward found this omission deeply anchoring; it lacked the desperate salesmanship of a charlatan. He transcribed the number onto a notepad.
When he placed the call the following morning, a woman answered on the third ring. Her voice was characterised by a brisk, administrative detachment that immediately made Edward regret lifting the receiver.
“Meridian Reclamation Services.”
Edward forced breath into his lungs. “I saw your advertisement. I want to speak to someone who can explain how the process works.”
“Certainly,” the woman replied, slipping effortlessly into a practised, frictionless cadence. “May I ask whether this inquiry concerns a deceased family member?”
“Yes.”
“Have you previously retained our services?”
“No.”
A brief pause hung on the line, filled only by the faint, oceanic static of the connection. “Then I can arrange a preliminary consultation. Please be advised that there is an initial assessment fee.”
She quoted a figure exorbitant enough to deter the merely curious. Edward looked out through his kitchen window at the bruised morning sky. “That will be fine.”
“May I have the legal name of the deceased?”
Edward hesitated. The ancient lie—or perhaps the ancient truth—caught like a rusted blade in his throat. “Gary.”
“And your relationship to the deceased?”
“My brother.”
The illusion of a routine bureaucratic intake shattered the moment she requested documentation. She calmly asked for the date and location of Gary's passing, which Edward provided with mechanical precision. But when she prompted him for the paperwork necessary to establish Gary's identity, a suffocating, leaden silence flooded the kitchen.
“Sir?” she prompted, the professional warmth receding.
“I don't have any,” Edward murmured, the receiver suddenly heavy in his grip. “I don't have any documents.”
“We strictly require evidence of identity and death before a medical consultation can proceed,” she explained, her patience taking on a patronising edge. “Do you possess a death certificate? A photograph? Dental or school records? Formal correspondence? Anything at all that might establish that this individual existed?”
Edward watched a bead of rain race down the glass, tracing an erratic path to the sill. “No.”
The woman audibly paused, seemingly searching her script for a contingency that could force the situation back into the realm of the ordinary. She asked if his brother had been estranged, if he had been a ward of the state, or if he had operated under a legal alias. To each inquiry, Edward offered only a hollow, whispered negation.
“Then I am afraid there is absolutely nothing we can do,” she said with chilling finality. “I sympathise with your situation, sir. But our clinical procedures require empirical evidence.”
Though she offered polite apologies before terminating the call, the click of the disconnected line struck Edward with a sickening, physical weight. He remained seated at the kitchen table for a long time, the dead phone still clutched in his hand. He did not feel the heat of anger. Instead, a cold, creeping dread colonised his stomach. He had just been told, with the utmost administrative efficiency, that a boy whose shadow had followed him his entire life had been erased from the earth without a trace.
When Meridian unexpectedly contacted him three days later to arrange an off-the-record consultation with their director, Edward drove to the provided Edinburgh address without hesitation. The facility was entirely unmarked, occupying the upper floors of a modernist monolith of stone and black glass, flanked by high-end financial firms and private surgical practices. The reception lobby was a sterile, liminal purgatory that smelled faintly of ozone, chemical disinfectant, and roasted coffee. There were no religious icons here, no boxes of tissues, no weeping relatives seeking closure. He was escorted down a featureless, brilliantly white corridor, the oppressive silence punctuated only by the rhythmic, synthetic exhalations of unseen machinery, until he was ushered into the office of Dr Malcolm Venn.
Venn was a severe, meticulously groomed man in his late fifties, possessing the restrained, analytical demeanour of a scientist intimately acquainted with impossible phenomena. He offered no sympathetic platitudes as Edward took his seat—a clinical indifference for which Edward was immensely grateful.
“I have reviewed the transcript of your preliminary intake,” Dr Venn began, interlacing his manicured fingers atop an immaculate, glass-topped desk. “You claim the deceased was your brother, yet you possess zero empirical records establishing his existence. No photographs. No medical history. No grave. Can you explain to me why there is absolutely nothing?”
“I don't know,” Edward answered, the truth sounding entirely insufficient under the weight of the doctor's piercing, predatory scrutiny. “I assume he was legally registered somewhere, but I was a child. He died at fifteen. I was seventeen.”
Venn continued his methodical interrogation, probing ruthlessly for surviving relatives, family friends, or external witnesses who could corroborate Gary's life, only to be met with Edward's quiet, unbroken string of denials.
“You must understand the profound difficulty this presents, Mr Hale,” Venn said, leaning back into the shadows of his leather chair. “What, exactly, makes you so certain he existed?”
The question felt abrasive—not for its audacity, but for the bottomless existential terror it inadvertently brushed against.
“I knew him,” Edward said, his voice hardening into something brittle and dangerous. “I grew up with him. He was my brother.”
Venn studied him with the cold, unblinking curiosity of an entomologist pinning a specimen to a board. He explained, in measured tones, that Meridian was not a spiritualist parlour. They did not deal in mediums or the occult. Their methods relied on highly classified neurological mapping and controlled physiological states, wherein a chemically sedated host would act as a temporary conduit for anomalous, localised consciousness. When Venn reiterated the impossibility of anchoring a session without a verified identity, Edward did not argue. He simply reached into the breast pocket of his coat, withdrew a folded cheque bearing a staggering, ruinous sum, and slid it across the polished glass.
“Because I want to speak to my brother,” Edward said, his gaze fixed on the doctor's eyes.
The subsequent approval from the board came with a terrifying caveat, buried deep within a dense, multi-page legal waiver that Edward signed two weeks later. The document explicitly absolved Meridian of any psychological disintegration arising from the procedure, but it was a single, italicised sentence near the bottom of the final page that made the blood freeze in Edward's veins:
Meridian Reclamation Services makes no representation regarding the origin, identity, continuity, or independent reality of any consciousness encountered during a communication session.
The morning of the procedure, the facility felt suffocatingly calm. Nurses and technicians moved with a mundane, rehearsed efficiency that violently contrasted with the unnatural transgression they were about to facilitate. Stripped of his coat, his watch, and his phone, Edward was ushered into a dimly lit observation room. Beyond a thick pane of reinforced glass lay the consultation chamber. A young man, introduced simply as Phil, lay unconscious on a narrow clinical bed, his unremarkable face entirely slack under the weight of a heavy chemical paralytic. Intravenous lines snaked into his pale arms, and a complex web of neural electrodes mapped the surface of his chest and temples.
“If at any point you become psychologically distressed, you are to instruct us immediately,” Dr Venn commanded through the intercom, his eyes tracking the glowing telemetry on the medical monitors.
Edward nodded, his throat clamped too tight to speak. The overhead lights dimmed, plunging the observation room into heavy shadow. For several agonising minutes, the only sound in the world was the steady, synthesised rhythm of Phil’s heartbeat pulsing through the overhead speakers.
Then, the host's fingers twitched.
His chest hitched in a sudden, desperate inhalation, pulling against the restraints. The heart monitor spiked into a frantic, erratic tempo, a jagged red line racing across the screen. The technicians tensed, leaning aggressively over their keyboards, but before Venn could initiate the abort sequence, Phil’s eyes snapped open.
At first, they were completely vacant, roaming the sterile ceiling tiles with the blind panic of a newborn. Then, they stopped. They locked directly onto the reinforced glass.
They locked onto Edward.
The physical transformation was microscopic, yet it possessed the catastrophic force of a collapsing star. The muscles around Phil's jaw shifted; the inherent, empty tension of a stranger dissolved entirely, replaced in a fraction of a second by an expression of bone-deep, terrifying familiarity. A crooked, knowing smile stretched across the host's face—a viciously confident expression that did not belong to the body wearing it.
“Hello, Edward.”
The voice was little more than a quiet rasp, yet it was textured with a faint, youthful cruelty that Edward had not heard in fifty years. It shattered the sterile air of the clinical room. Behind him, the heavy observation door engaged its magnetic lock with a dull, metallic finality, sealing him inside as the session began.
For several agonising seconds, the entity behind Phil’s eyes simply took inventory of its new surroundings. The gaze that swept across the sterile acoustic ceiling, the pulsating telemetry monitors, and the reinforced observation glass was devoid of any groggy disorientation one might expect from a heavily sedated patient. It was a calculating, predatory appraisal. His breathing, previously dictated by the slow rhythm of the machines, grew shallow and deliberate.
Edward remained frozen in his chair, his lungs refusing to expand.
On the other side of the glass, Dr Venn stepped toward the foot of the clinical bed, his eyes darting between the anomalous monitor readings and the host's face. “Phil?” he prompted, his voice retaining its clinical authority.
The man on the bed did not answer. He did not even flinch. Instead, his gaze slid away from the doctor and locked onto Edward through the glass. The last remnants of Philip Moran—the inherent, muscular tension that makes a face recognisable as belonging to a specific soul—evaporated. The muscles around his eyes relaxed into an expression of chilling, absolute certainty. It was not a facsimile. It was not a trick of the dim lighting. It was the exact, crooked smile of a fifteen-year-old boy, worn like a mask over a thirty-year-old man’s face.
“It has been a long time,” the voice rasped.
It was entirely wrong. The vocal cords belonged to Phil, but the cadence, the pitch, and the faint, arrogant roughness were entirely Gary’s. It was a voice Edward had not heard since the rain-soaked afternoon of his fifteenth year.
Edward gripped the armrests of his chair, his knuckles turning entirely white. “Do you know me?” he finally whispered, the words scraping painfully from his dry throat.
Phil threw his head back and laughed—a bright, mocking sound that sent a physical shockwave of dread down Edward's spine. “You always ask stupid questions when you are frightened, Edward.”
Edward closed his eyes, and for a terrifying moment, the sterile walls of the Meridian chamber dissolved. He was standing beside a rusted wire fence in the freezing rain, fifteen years old, with thick mud caking his shoes and the metallic taste of blood on his split lip. He could hear the jeering laughter of older boys retreating across the sodden football field. And standing right beside him, completely invisible to the cruelty of the world, Gary was laughing too.
When Edward opened his eyes, the host was still watching him with that same terrible amusement.
“You remember,” Gary said, his smile widening.
“Gary?” Edward breathed, leaning forward so violently that the heavy chair scraped against the linoleum floor.
Through the intercom, Dr Venn’s voice cut through the heavy air. “Edward, I must ask you to remain seated and maintain your composure.”
Edward ignored him entirely. “How?”
In the chamber, Gary slowly lowered his gaze to his hands. For the first time since opening his eyes, a flicker of genuine confusion crossed his borrowed features. He turned the hands over, studying the broad palms and thick fingers, flexing them as though testing the tension of unfamiliar machinery. A faint, unnatural tremor ran through the knuckles.
“These are not mine,” Gary murmured, raising one hand to the light. “They are entirely too big. They do not feel right.”
Dr Venn exchanged a sharp, imperceptible glance with the technician stationed behind the monitors, but Edward was blind to their silent alarm.
“You are here,” Edward whispered, the impossibility of the moment fracturing his lifelong grief.
Gary lowered the unfamiliar hand and looked directly at him. “Of course I am here.”
The answer was delivered with such banal, terrifying ordinary grace that it frightened Edward more than any spectral howling ever could have.
Dr Venn moved closer to the head of the bed, invading the host's peripheral vision. “Phil, can you tell me where you are?”
Gary turned his head slowly, regarding the silver-haired doctor with a look of profound irritation. “Who is Phil?”
The frantic clacking of the technician's keyboard abruptly ceased. Dr Venn did not react to the silence, maintaining his measured, authoritative tone. “Your name is Philip Moran.”
Gary frowned, a vicious little crease appearing between Phil’s brows. “No, it isn't.” He looked back through the glass at Edward, his tone dripping with theatrical exasperation. “What is this man talking about? Edward, tell him.”
“You are Gary,” Edward said, his voice trembling.
“Yes.”
Dr Venn turned his severe gaze toward the observation window. “Edward, I need you to understand and acknowledge that the man sitting in front of you is Philip Moran. You must remember the parameters of this session.”
“I know exactly who he is,” Edward snapped, his eyes flicking to the plastic identification band strapped to the host's wrist. It read PHILIP MORAN in bold, black lettering. Yet, when he looked back up, the man lifted a finger and absentmindedly scratched the side of his cheek—a uniquely specific, nervous gesture that sent a fresh wave of ice through Edward’s veins.
“You still do that,” Gary said, his eyes dancing with amusement.
Edward blinked, momentarily unmoored. “Do what?”
“That pathetic thing with your mouth when you do not know what to say.”
Edward’s hand flew to his lips, touching them before he could stop himself.
Gary let out another barking laugh. “You did it when your father was angry, too.”
The room went completely still. Dr Venn’s expression shifted, the clinical mask cracking just enough to reveal a sliver of genuine unease, but Edward saw only the ghost of his childhood staring back at him.
“How do you know about my father?” Edward demanded, his voice dropping to a hollow whisper.
Gary shrugged the heavy, borrowed shoulders. “Because I was there.” He leaned back against the stark white pillow, looking up at the ceiling as though projecting the memory onto the tiles. “He used to come home late on Friday nights. He would leave those massive black boots by the kitchen door. You hated the sound they made on the linoleum.”
Edward ceased breathing.
“You used to count them,” Gary continued, his voice dropping into a conspiratorial, intimate register. “The steps on the stairs. One, two, three, four. Then he would stop outside your bedroom door.”
The skin on Edward’s arms tightened into painful gooseflesh. He had never spoken of those nights to a living soul. Not to his late wife in the dark sanctuary of their marital bed, not to his children, and certainly not to the intake coordinators at Meridian. It was a secret buried beneath fifty years of psychological masonry.
Gary looked at him with an expression that bordered on a twisted, suffocating affection. “You would hold your breath in the dark. You were always so terrified of the fourth step.”
Edward swallowed against the sudden, agonising restriction in his throat. “You remember.”
“I remember absolutely everything,” Gary said, his expression softening into something dangerous and familiar. He let the silence stretch, allowing the quiet, rhythmic beeping of the medical monitors to fill the void, before he spoke again. “Do you remember the blue tin?”
“What blue tin?”
“The rusted one he kept hidden beneath his bed.” Gary’s crooked smile returned. “You stole it.”
“I did not.”
“You most certainly did. I told you to look inside it. I told you there would be money.”
“There was no money,” Edward countered, a strange, breathless panic rising in his chest. “There were only old buttons.”
“You were so incredibly disappointed,” Gary laughed.
And suddenly, despite the sheer, existential horror of the sterile chamber, Edward laughed too. The sound tore from his throat—strange, painful, and rusted from decades of disuse. Gary laughed with him, and for a few suspended moments, the clinical reality of the Meridian facility evaporated. The telemetry monitors grew distant, the harsh fluorescent lights seemed to burn with the warmth of a summer afternoon, and Edward was no longer a fractured man of sixty-two. He was fifteen, then twelve, then eight, tumbling backwards through the fragmented labyrinth of a childhood he had spent half a century trying to forget.
Gary had always been the architect of that labyrinth. Edward could no longer pinpoint the exact genesis of their friendship; Gary had simply seeped into his life, arriving so gradually that his presence felt as natural and inevitable as a shadow. He was two years younger, physically slighter than Edward in the beginning, but possessed a wicked agility with his hands and a venomous quickness with his tongue. He carried a terrifying, unearned confidence that Edward, bruised and perpetually frightened, had clung to like a lifeline. When the neighbourhood boys drove Edward into the mud, it was Gary who hurled a rock into their teeth. When Edward wept in the dark, Gary mocked the tears until they dried. And when Edward’s father came tearing through the house with a leather belt, Gary always knew the darkest, narrowest spaces where they could hide.
The ancient forest bordering the town had been their sovereign territory. It began at a narrow, overgrown gap behind the school's football field—a path Edward had initially discovered while fleeing his tormentors. It descended sharply through the dense pines toward a sluggish stream, where the earth grew spongy with emerald moss, and the air hung heavy with the scent of wet rot and decaying bark. Gary moved through the woods with an animalistic intuition, knowing exactly where the foxes burrowed, which hollows turned treacherous after a storm, and where the blackberry brambles grew thickest over the collapsed ruins of old stone walls.
They spent entire seasons swallowed by those trees. Gary possessed the rare ability to sit beside Edward for hours without demanding the silence be filled, a silent guardian against the miseries of the waking world. Yet, beneath that fierce loyalty lay a cold, unnerving cruelty. Gary delighted in the terror of small things. Edward remembered watching, sickened, as Gary trapped a frantic beetle beneath a heavy glass jar, observing its desperate struggle for oxygen with a detached, clinical fascination until Edward batted the glass away. That same chilling detachment bled into the way Gary spoke of the people who hurt Edward.
“Someday,” Gary had whispered one afternoon, his eyes dark and flat, “we will make them all brutally sorry.” Edward had never dared to ask what he meant.
It was during one of these deep, wandering incursions that they discovered the well.
It sat in the most desolate, lightless heart of the forest, choked by creeping ivy and monstrous ferns. The circular stone wall surrounding the mouth had crumbled, giving it the appearance of a broken, jagged tooth, while a massive, rotting oak tree leaned precariously over the abyss, as though desperate to peer into the dark. It was a forgotten place, devoid of paths or the sounds of woodland birds. They spent countless hours sitting on the ruined stones, dropping pebbles into the lightless void and counting the breathless seconds before they heard the distant, echoing strike of water. Sometimes, the sound never came at all. Gary firmly insisted that the shaft was bottomless. When Edward argued that such a thing was physically impossible, Gary simply sneered, calling him hopelessly boring.
It was upon those broken stones that they forged their childish, unbreakable covenants. They promised they would never grow into the monstrous men their fathers were. They swore they would never abandon one another. But one evening, as the autumn sun bled out behind the tree line and the forest sank into a bruised, terrifying blue, Gary had gripped Edward’s arm with bruising force.
“Whatever happens,” Gary had commanded, his young face etched with an adult severity, “you come back.”
“I will,” Edward had sworn.
“Promise?”
Gary had extended his little finger. Edward hooked his own around it, sealing the pact in the dying light. “I promise.”
The memory violently receded, pulling Edward back into the cold, sanitised reality of the observation room. Phil’s borrowed eyes were locked onto him.
“You remember the well,” Gary said softly.
Edward merely nodded, his breath shuddering.
“I knew you would.”
Through the intercom, Dr Venn’s voice was laced with a taut, professional urgency. “Edward, your pulse has elevated to a dangerous threshold. I strongly suggest we take a brief intermission.”
“No,” Edward snapped, not breaking eye contact with the glass. “I don't care. Leave it.”
Gary turned his head, looking past Edward to the mirrored glass separating them from the technicians. “Who are those people buzzing about out there?”
“They work here,” Edward said.
“Why?”
Edward hesitated, shame colouring his cheeks. “Because I paid them.”
“How much?”
“Too much.”
Gary let out a derisive, snorting laugh. “You were always completely pathetic with money.”
For a surreal, suspended hour, they conversed as though fifty years of silence had never occurred. Gary demanded an accounting of Edward’s life, dissecting his choices with surgical precision. He inquired about the marriage, the children, and the businesses built and lost, correcting Edward on the minutiae of his own history. He recalled Edward’s irrational hatred of onions, the jagged scar on his left thumb from a rusted fishing knife, and the time Edward had slipped into the forest stream and lost a shoe to the current. Every memory produced was flawlessly, terrifyingly accurate.
Eventually, the conversation drifted to the girl.
Gary did not need to ask her name; he already knew it. He remembered the exact shade of the wool coat she wore during the brutal winter Edward had begun courting her. He recalled the silver clasp she used to pin back her hair, and the humiliating evening Edward had stood freezing in the torrential rain outside her flat, paralysed by the fear of knocking on her door.
“You were shaking like a beaten dog,” Gary said, a cruel smirk playing on Phil’s lips.
“It was freezing out,” Edward defended defensively.
“You were terrified of her.”
“I was nineteen years old. Of course I was frightened.”
Edward smiled despite the ache in his chest. “You always believed you knew everything about the world.”
“I did know everything,” Gary countered smoothly. “I knew you.”
The absolute, unshakeable certainty in those three words forced Edward to look away. He remembered the fierce, possessive jealousy Gary had harboured toward the girl. At twenty, she had been the first person to make Edward believe he might be capable of living a normal, unbruised life. She possessed an easy, musical laugh, despised loud, crowded rooms, and tucked her hair behind her ear whenever she grew anxious. Edward had loved her with the desperate, suffocating intensity of a young man convinced he was inherently unlovable.
Gary had loathed her entirely.
“She does not understand you,” Gary had warned him one afternoon, his voice tight with disdain.
“She doesn’t have to understand every piece of me,” Edward had argued.
“She knows nothing. I know enough for the both of us.”
Edward had chuckled, thinking it a game. “You are jealous of her.”
Gary had looked violently offended. “Of her? Why would I possibly envy her?”
“Because I spend my time with her now. Which means I spend less time with you.”
Following that argument, Gary’s presence had become oppressive. He began appearing at the edges of Edward’s life like a haunting. First, it was only in the shadows of the forest. Then, he was lingering outside Edward’s house. Eventually, Edward would see him standing motionless beneath the amber glow of a streetlamp when he left work, or sitting on the brick wall near the railway station.
The situation had fractured entirely on a damp evening while Edward was walking the girl home. He had stopped mid-sentence, his eyes locking onto a figure standing across the desolate street.
“Who are you looking at?” she had asked, following his gaze into the dark.
“Nobody,” Edward lied, but when he looked back, Gary was already gone.
Later that night, Gary had materialised in Edward's bedroom, vibrating with a cold, terrifying fury. “She cannot even see me,” he hissed. “She does not know me. What did you tell her about me?”
“I told her you were my friend,” Edward pleaded.
“That is all? She thinks I am not real, Edward.”
“You are real,” Edward had insisted, but the smile Gary gave him in return contained something deeply malignant.
The girl had eventually noticed the fracturing of Edward's mind. She caught him smiling at empty chairs, murmuring responses to questions no one had asked, and turning his head sharply toward doorways to greet unseen arrivals. Her initial confusion had slowly rotted into genuine terror.
Beneath a flickering streetlamp on a miserable Tuesday night, she had finally stopped walking and gripped his arm. “Edward, who are you talking to?”
“Nobody,” he recited the familiar lie.
“You have been doing it for months. Whenever we are together, you are whispering to someone who isn't there. Whoever this is, you need to let him go.”
Edward had felt a cold, protective fury harden in his chest. “He is my friend.”
“He is not here!” she cried, her voice cracking. “I have never seen him.”
“That doesn't mean anything.”
She had looked at him then, not with love, but with the profound, heartbreaking pity reserved for the deeply unwell. “Edward,” she had whispered gently, her eyes wide with fear, “I think he only exists when you are entirely alone. Why am I the only person in the world who cannot see him?”
“Because you do not know him,” Edward had spat, pulling his arm from her grasp. He had turned and walked away, leaving her weeping beneath the halogen light.
When he reached the end of the dark street, Gary was already waiting for him. The malignant anger was gone, replaced by a soft, victorious smile.
“You chose me,” Gary had whispered, stepping out of the shadows. He wrapped a comforting arm around Edward’s trembling shoulders, guiding him away from the light. “It is all right. You have me.”
And because Gary had been the only constant in a world defined by violence and abandonment, Edward had believed him. He had no logical reason to doubt the boy who had stood beside him at the bottom of the world. Gary was real. He had to be real.
The only thing Edward had never been able to understand was why, as they walked into the darkness that night, Gary had smiled—a smile that suggested he knew that one day, Edward would be forced to prove it.
For the next hour, the sterile boundaries of the consultation chamber seemed to unmoor themselves from the ordinary progression of time. The digital clocks mounted on the walls continued their silent march, the telemetry monitors faithfully recorded the slow, mechanical facts of Philip Moran’s respiration, and somewhere beyond the reinforced glass, the waking world proceeded with its mundane business. Yet for Edward, the sprawling chasm of forty-seven years collapsed entirely into the narrow, electrified space between two chairs. Gary remained firmly anchored within Phil’s body, speaking in that unmistakably familiar cadence, effortlessly excavating memories that Edward had buried so deeply he had forgotten he possessed them at all.
Gary dissected Edward’s adult life with the clinical precision of a coroner, beginning with the girl. He did not bother to ask her name; he simply produced it, rolling the syllables around in Phil’s mouth as though tasting something bitter. He recalled the exact shade of the heavy wool coat she had worn during their first winter together, the intricate silver clasp she used to secure her hair, and the humiliating evening Edward had stood paralysed in the freezing rain outside her flat, entirely devoid of the courage to knock.
“You were shaking,” Gary noted, a cruel amusement dancing in his borrowed eyes.
“It was bitterly cold,” Edward countered, shifting uncomfortably under the scrutiny.
“You were terrified of her.”
“I was nineteen years old. Of course I was frightened.”
Edward allowed a fragile, exhausted smile to touch his lips. “You always believed you knew everything about the world.”
“I did know everything,” Gary replied smoothly. “I knew you.”
The absolute, terrifying certainty in those words forced Edward to look away. On the other side of the glass, the technician continued her vigilant watch over the erratic monitors, while Dr Venn stood near the wall with his arms tightly folded, his attention divided evenly between the struggling equipment and the impossible conversation unfolding before him.
Seeking refuge in safer waters, Edward steered the dialogue toward the mundane architecture of his marriage. He spoke of marrying the girl when he was twenty-four, of their brief relocation and eventual return to the Scottish borders. He described their first house—a cramped, damp property that nevertheless held a profound warmth—and the subsequent arrivals of their two children. He detailed the gruelling years of building a business, the terrifying periods of financial ruin, and the slow, agonising climb back to stability. Gary listened to this litany of adult survival with a chilling patience. Sometimes he laughed softly; other times, he interrupted to correct a minute detail Edward had genuinely misremembered.
“You forgot the dog,” Gary interjected, casually waving a hand.
“We never owned a dog,” Edward corrected him.
“You most certainly did. For three weeks.”
“Three weeks does not constitute owning a dog, Gary. It belonged to the neighbours.”
Gary shrugged his heavy shoulders. “You fed the miserable creature.”
“My wife fed it.”
“But you named it.”
Edward let out a short, hollow bark of laughter, the sound startling him in the quiet room. “You actually remember that?”
“I remember absolutely everything.”
The answer was delivered with such effortless, terrifying velocity that Edward’s laughter died instantly in his throat. He stared at the man on the bed, feeling the sudden, suffocating weight of the silence.
Gary watched him carefully. “What is it?”
“Nothing.”
“You are thinking again, Edward.”
“I have been thinking my entire life.”
“Yes,” Gary smiled, “and that has always been your primary defect.”
For a fleeting, surreal moment, the room felt almost peaceful—two old friends catching up across the void. But then Gary leaned forward against the clinical restraints, his expression hardening into something ancient and unforgiving. “When did you stop looking for me, Edward?”
Edward felt the blood drain from his face. “I never looked for you.”
“That is not what I asked.”
Edward lowered his eyes to the linoleum floor. He had known, in the deepest recesses of his subconscious, that this question was inevitable.
