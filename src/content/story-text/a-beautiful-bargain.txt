Paris was still dark when Jennifer woke, and for a long, heavy moment she did not move. The room was warm and dim, the heavy velvet curtains drawn securely against the biting November morning. Beyond the glass, she could hear the muted, mechanical life of the city beginning somewhere far below: the low growl of a delivery van, the brief and lonely bark of a dog, the hollow, echoing thud of a distant door closing. The air carried the faint scent of lavender from the sachet she kept buried among her clothes, mingling with the clean, dry, almost sterile smell of linen that was changed with rigid punctuality twice a week. She had always liked waking before the city was properly awake. There was a private dignity in those few minutes when Paris belonged neither to the night nor to the day, when the streets were utterly empty, and the windows of neighbouring houses remained blindly dark. Rising at last, Jennifer slipped her cold feet into her slippers and drew back the heavy curtains. A pale, slate-grey light lay over the garden, where the last dead leaves of autumn clung desperately to the branches, and the wet grass shone like shattered glass beneath the skeletal trees.
She was fifty-eight years old, though the number had never seemed particularly important to her. Numbers belonged to doctors, accountants, and people who had nothing more interesting to say. Jennifer dealt in physical realities, in tangible results. A face was either meticulously cared for, or it was a tragedy; a colour either flattered a woman or made her look exhausted; a dress either granted the body grace or condemned it to heaviness. Age, as far as she was concerned, was neither a tragedy nor an excuse. It was simply another condition of life, much like bitter weather, and she had always believed that a sensible woman learned how to armour herself against it.
The house was perfectly quiet when she came downstairs. It was a handsome, detached townhouse in one of the more peaceful residential streets of Paris, close enough to the centre for Jennifer to reach her salon without wasting half her morning in traffic, yet sufficiently removed from the grand avenues to grant her the absolute privacy she guarded so fiercely. She had bought it years before, when the salon first began to prosper, altering it slowly, room by room, refusing the gaudy extravagance that so often accompanied new wealth. The walls were pale, the floors dark wood, the furniture comfortable rather than ostentatious; there were brass lamps, old framed prints, heavy shelves of books, and tall windows that opened towards a small garden she tended more faithfully than she ever admitted.
She found Ana in the kitchen, already awake and sitting at the table in her pyjamas, with her enormous stuffed unicorn lying across the chair beside her. The toy was almost as large as the child, white and impossibly soft, crowned with a silver horn and a mane of pink, blue, and lavender. Ana had received it on her fifth birthday and loved it with the solemn, unwavering devotion children sometimes bestow upon things that adults regard as merely cloth and stuffing.
"Maa."
Jennifer looked at the clock upon the wall. "It is a quarter to seven. What are you doing awake?"
"I waited for you."
"You waited for me?"
Ana nodded. "I was awake, and Alice said you would come down soon."
"Then Alice was right."
Jennifer came around the table and pressed a kiss to her daughter's forehead. Ana smelled of sleep, warm cotton, and the mild soap she used before bed. Her dark hair had been flattened on one side and stood up wildly on the other. Jennifer, who could not look at an untidy element without a physical compulsion to correct it, smoothed the unruly strands gently with her fingers.
"You look like a little bird."
"I don't.
"You do."
"I look like Ana."
Jennifer smiled, a genuine softening of her strict features. "That is true." She put bread into the toaster, opened the heavy oak cupboard, and brought down the glass jar of strawberry jam.
"Maa?"
"Yes?"
"Your hair is white."
Jennifer's hand paused in the air for the smallest, sharpest fraction of a second. "Some of it."
"There is more than yesterday."
Jennifer looked at her, a sudden, irrational coldness pricking at her skin. "Were you counting?"
Ana nodded with complete, unnerving seriousness.
"You should spend less time counting my hair and more time eating your breakfast."
Ana looked at the toast, then back at her mother's face. "I like your white hair."
"Do you?"
"It makes you look like a queen."
Jennifer laughed, a real laugh, the kind that arrived before she could build a wall against it. "What sort of queen?"
"A beautiful one."
Jennifer bent and kissed her again, the sudden coldness dissolving. "Eat your breakfast.
A few minutes later, Alice entered the kitchen, apologising profusely for being late although she was scarcely two minutes behind schedule. She was twenty, slender and quietly self-possessed, radiating the practical competence of someone who had learned early that most things became easier if they were attended to before they soured into problems. She had come to Jennifer two years earlier as a part-time nanny while studying fashion styling through a university distance programme. Jennifer had noticed her almost immediately—not because Alice was especially beautiful, though she undeniably was, but because she possessed an unusual, hungry attentiveness. She remembered things. She listened intently. She understood what Ana wanted before the child had finished explaining it, and she harboured an instinct for clothes that Jennifer recognised as genuine vision rather than mere fashionable enthusiasm. Before long, the arrangement had evolved. Jennifer had asked her to move into the house, given her the bright room overlooking the garden, entrusted her with the running of the household and the care of Ana, and promised that when Alice finished her studies, she would have a secure place in the salon if she still desired one.
Alice had protested that she could not accept so grand an offer.
"I am not offering you charity," Jennifer had told her coldly. "I am offering you an opportunity. There is a vast difference."
"And if I disappoint you?"
"You will." Alice had stared at her in shock, and Jennifer had merely smiled. "Everyone does eventually. The trick is to make yourself useful in the interim."
Alice had laughed then, and the matter had been permanently settled.
Now, she crossed the kitchen and lifted Ana from her chair as naturally as though the child belonged to her as much as to Jennifer. "Good morning, little lady."
"Maa says I look like a bird."
Alice glanced at Jennifer, a conspiratorial gleam in her eye. "She is right."
"Alice."
"I am sorry."
She was not sorry at all. Ana laughed, and Jennifer shook her head, pretending a profound irritation. There were mornings when she looked at the two of them and felt a quiet, desperate gratitude that she rarely expressed, simply because she had never been adept at speaking about the things that mattered most. Alice had become part of the household without either of them quite deciding when the shift occurred. She knew how Jennifer took her coffee, which cupboard Ana would ransack when she craved biscuits, which window stubbornly stuck in damp weather, and which of Jennifer's dark moods meant that conversation was advisable and which dictated absolute silence. More importantly, she loved Ana, and Ana loved her. Jennifer trusted very few people in this world, but she trusted Alice with the child.
By eight o'clock, Ana was dressed, her schoolbag packed, her hair neatly tied, and her breakfast reduced to a few scattered crumbs upon the plate. Alice meticulously checked the contents of the bag while Jennifer stood by the heavy front door, fastening her coat against the November chill.
"Lunch?
"In the bag."
"Water?"
"Yes."
"Homework?"
"Yes."
"Unicorn?"
Ana's eyes widened in horror.
Jennifer smiled thinly. "I am joking."
"That isn't funny."
"It is to me."
The school bus waited at the end of the street, its windows misted faintly by the manufactured warmth inside. Ana kissed Jennifer, then Alice, and ran towards it with the careless, buoyant happiness of a child whose world still consisted largely of people who would inevitably come back when they promised they would. Jennifer watched from the doorway until Ana had climbed aboard and taken her seat beside two school friends. Only then did she turn back to the quiet house.
"Tonight," she said to Alice, her tone shifting back to the professional, "do not let her persuade you to stay awake until eleven."
"She has a book to finish."
"She always has a book to finish."
"She gets that from you."
Jennifer gave her a long, measuring look.
Alice smiled, entirely unbothered. "Have a good day."
"You too."
Jennifer stepped out into the biting cold.
Her salon stood in a handsome, imposing building not far from the city centre. Over nearly a decade, she had transformed it from a modest, struggling establishment with three chairs and one frightened assistant into a sanctuary whose name was whispered among women who graced the covers of magazines. Five employees now worked under her exacting eye. Large cosmetic syndicates sent her experimental products before they were publicly released, and heritage brands utilised her photographs in global advertising campaigns. Actresses, models, television presenters, and immensely wealthy women who violently disliked being seen in ordinary places swept through her doors. Jennifer had built it entirely herself, beginning with agonisingly long days, cheap, bitter coffee, and a bank balance she checked so obsessively that she eventually knew its pitiful sum without looking. She had never forgotten the humiliation of those early years, and perhaps that was precisely why she remained so ruthlessly particular about everything.
The salon smelled of expensive shampoo, warm water, heavy perfume, clinical face creams, and freshly brewed coffee from the little machine near reception. Hair dryers hummed behind half-closed doors like a swarm of mechanical bees, steel scissors flashed brilliantly beneath the harsh white lights, and hushed conversations rose and fell around the mirrors with the peculiar, desperate intimacy that belonged to beauty parlours—places where strangers willingly surrendered their faces, and sometimes their darkest secrets, to people they had met only that morning. Jennifer moved through it all with the brisk, untouchable authority of a woman who knew exactly what she had built and exactly what it had cost her soul.
"No, not that shade." She plucked a small frosted bottle from a trembling assistant's hand and returned it sharply to the glass shelf. "Bring me the other one."
"Yes, Jennifer."
"And Rose is arriving at eleven. Prepare room two."
Rose was nineteen and came faithfully every other Thursday. She was a university student who had casually begun modelling and had recently been offered a small, coveted part in a film. Her face possessed the luminous, unstudied freshness of absolute youth, and there was already a magnetic gravity about her that compelled people to look twice whenever she entered a room. Yet Jennifer liked her for reasons that had surprisingly little to do with her exquisite beauty. Rose trusted her completely. She never arrived waving photographs from magazines or insisting upon some absurd, fashionable treatment she had seen online; she simply surrendered to Jennifer's chair and said, "Do whatever you think is best."
That morning, she arrived wrapped in a long, camel-hair wool coat, carrying a paper cup of coffee and bringing a rush of cold air with her.
"Jennifer."
"You are late."
"Four minutes."
"That is late."
Rose laughed, a bright, chiming sound. "I am sorry."
Jennifer kissed her chilled cheek and smoothly confiscated the coffee from her hand. "Finish that later. Your skin is already dangerously dry."
"I knew you would say that."
"You come here because I tell you the things you desperately do not want to hear."
Rose settled gracefully into the leather chair, shedding her heavy coat. "Then tell me something good."
"You are nineteen. Everything about you is inherently good."
Rose smiled at her own flawless reflection in the glass. "Do you really think I will make it?"
Jennifer began methodically arranging her gleaming instruments. "To the film?"
"To everything."
Jennifer met her eyes through the mirror, her gaze unwavering. "Yes."
"You sound very certain."
"I am."
Rose smiled again, blushing faintly, embarrassed by the immense confidence this older, formidable woman had placed in her.
Jennifer worked with clinical precision, cleansing the girl's face, examining her pores beneath the unforgiving glare of the magnifying lamp, and applying one intricate preparation after another with the practised gentleness that had forged her formidable reputation. She knew faces almost instinctively. She could see the microscopic tragedies that another person missed: the first, faint dryness taking root beneath the eyes, the subtle bruising born of too little sleep, the critical difference between a natural flush and chemical irritation, and the precise, mathematical line at which a haircut stopped flattering a jawline and began to aggressively dominate the face. She knew exactly how much a woman could be transformed with good light, masterful colour, and a skilled hand, and she also intimately knew the crushing limits of all three. Beauty was not magic. It was knowledge, agonising discipline, masterful illusion, and, sometimes, sheer, unmerited good fortune.
"You have been sleeping badly," she murmured.
Rose looked up at her, startled. "How do you know?"
"Your eyes."
"Maybe I am in love."
"That would certainly explain them."
Rose laughed, and Jennifer permitted herself a small, tight smile as she continued her work. For the entirety of her adult life, Jennifer had regarded beauty not as a divine possession, but as a ruthless craft. She knew that faces inevitably changed, bodies warped, hair faded, and that no woman, however staggeringly wealthy, could bribe time to stop. She had never foolishly expected to remain twenty-five. She had simply intended to remain definitively herself: elegant, desirable, highly competent, impeccably dressed, and sufficiently beautiful that the passage of age would register as sophisticated experience rather than a devastating defeat. At fifty-eight, she still fiercely believed she had succeeded. She did not think of herself as an old woman. She thought of herself as Jennifer, who simply happened to be fifty-eight, and who knew rather more about preserving herself than most women half her age.
The party that dismantled this carefully constructed illusion came several weeks later.
It was hosted by an influential television producer whose sprawling apartment overlooked the Seine, and Jennifer had nearly declined the invitation. She had worked all day relentlessly, returned home exhausted in the dark, helped Ana battle through her reading, listened to Alice detail the faulty heating in the upstairs bathroom, and had barely an hour in which to make herself presentable. But the producer was a man of consequence, and Jennifer had spent enough years navigating Paris to understand that professional invitations were rarely merely social affairs. She chose a black dress, architecturally simple and flawlessly fitted, and spent far longer than usual torturing her hair into submission. When she finally scrutinised herself before leaving, she felt a surge of cold satisfaction. She looked elegant. She looked composed. She looked exactly like the untouchable woman she had worked so brutally hard to become.
The apartment was stiflingly warm and blindingly bright, with tall windows thrown open to the black river, the faint, metallic scent of the Seine mingling with expensive perfume, spilt champagne, and sharp cigarette smoke. Jazz drifted lazily beneath the roar of layered conversations. Actors stood shoulder-to-shoulder with producers, starved models beside predatory photographers, and people who had never met behaved with the manic, desperate familiarity of old friends. Jennifer knew many of the faces. She moved seamlessly through the crowded rooms, offering air-kisses, accepting flattery with practised grace, laughing precisely when required, and listening intensely when the information seemed useful. She had always been a master of parties. There was a distinct, professional artistry in knowing exactly how much of oneself to reveal, and how much to keep hidden in the dark.
The actor was there.
Jennifer had worked closely with him months earlier during a gruelling press tour for a film. They had spoken intimately perhaps a dozen times, mostly while she was standing behind him, styling his hair or preparing his skin for the merciless flash of the cameras. Once, when the dressing room had emptied, and they were entirely alone, he had stared at his own reflection, met her eyes in the glass, and said, "You make people look dangerous."
Jennifer had laughed, a low, genuine sound. "Only the ones who desperately need it."
He had smiled, a slow, devastating curve of the mouth. She had hoarded the memory of that smile much longer than any sensible woman should have.
That evening, through the crush of the crowd, they found each other and spoke again. He asked warmly about the salon. She inquired about the script for his next film. He leaned in close to tell her about a chaotic shoot abroad, and she laughed at a punchline she only half-heard over the noise of the room. For twenty minutes or so, they remained anchored near one another, and Jennifer allowed herself the small, dangerous, intensely private pleasure of believing that perhaps the intervening years had not extinguished certain romantic possibilities after all.
Then, a young woman entered the room.
Jennifer noticed her instantly, because absolutely everyone did. The girl was not dressed extravagantly; she did not need the crutch of expensive fabric. Her sheer, unadulterated youth seemed to illuminate her far more effectively than diamonds ever could. The actor noticed her, too. His eyes tracked the girl's movement, his posture shifting imperceptibly away from Jennifer. He murmured a distracted excuse, abandoned his drink, crossed the crowded floor, and immediately began speaking to the newcomer.
Jennifer watched them from where she stood, her crystal glass resting lightly, coldly in her hand. The girl threw her head back and laughed at something he said, exposing the long, flawless column of her throat. He reached out and touched her bare arm. They moved closer, drawn together by an invisible, inevitable gravity. A little while later, they slipped out of the apartment together.
Nothing overtly cruel had happened. That was precisely what made the pain so suffocating.
He had not insulted Jennifer. He had not mocked her, nor had he told her she was no longer desirable. He had simply looked upon a young woman and instinctively chosen to follow her. There had been no malice in his departure, perhaps not even a conscious, weighed decision. He had merely gravitated toward youth as blindly and naturally as a dying flower turns toward the sun.
Jennifer remained rooted to her spot for a long while. Someone spoke to her; she manufactured an answer. Someone else offered her a fresh glass of champagne; she politely declined. Eventually, when she felt she could move without shattering, she left.
Paris was brutally cold when she stepped outside. The night air smelled faintly of impending rain and stagnant river water, and the wet pavements shone like black ice beneath the streetlamps. She drove home in absolute silence, leaving the radio off. The city passed beyond the windscreen in shattered fragments of gold and black: cafés still buzzing with life, bicycles gliding silently through misted intersections, young couples walking tightly intertwined beneath umbrellas, the occasional laughing face appearing briefly in the windows of passing cars. She had navigated these streets for decades and knew them by pure instinct, but tonight they felt entirely alien, as though she were observing a vibrant, living world to which she no longer belonged.
The townhouse was tomb-dark when she finally entered. Alice had thoughtfully left a single, dim lamp burning in the hall. Jennifer peeled off her damp coat and stood rigidly for a moment, listening to the crushing, familiar silence of her own success. Upstairs, safely behind a closed door, Ana was asleep. Jennifer drifted up the stairs and looked into the room. The child lay tangled beneath her heavy blanket with the ridiculous stuffed unicorn pressed tightly against her chest, one small, perfect hand resting in its pastel fur. Jennifer smiled despite the hollow ache in her chest and stepped inside to pull the blanket a little higher over the girl's fragile shoulder. She stood over the bed for a moment longer than was strictly necessary, drawing comfort from the steady rhythm of Ana's breathing, before retreating and closing the door with agonising care.
On her own bedside table, she found a porcelain cup of chamomile tea, now stone cold, with a neatly folded note slipped beneath the saucer.
You forgot to drink this.
Jennifer smiled, a sad, fleeting thing. She mechanically stripped away her armour: she changed into her silk nightclothes, unclasped her heavy earrings, unhooked her necklace, and finally took a cotton pad to wipe the last, stubborn trace of crimson lipstick from her mouth. The bathroom was overly warm and smelled heavily of the expensive rose cleanser she had used hours before. She washed her face meticulously, dried it with a thick linen towel, and returned to the quiet shadows of the bedroom.
Only then did she dare to look into the full-length mirror.
At first, she saw nothing she had not seen a thousand times before. Her hair was still flawlessly arranged, her skin still carried the unnatural, expensive softness imparted by elite cosmetics, and the black dress hung lifelessly from a hook behind her—the discarded skin of the confident woman she had been only an hour earlier.
Then, she leaned closer to the glass.
There were deep, undeniable lines beneath her eyes. She knew them intimately. She had diagnosed them a hundred times on the despairing faces of other women. But the skin beside her own mouth had fundamentally changed. The jawline was no longer a sharp, defiant anchor; it betrayed a subtle, creeping softness. And there, weaving insidiously beneath the carefully dyed hair, several stark white strands had escaped near her temple.
Jennifer lifted a section of her hair with a trembling hand and stared at the roots. There were so many more than she had thought. There were dozens of them, pale and parasitic, hiding just beneath the surface.
She let the hair drop.
For years, she had understood the mechanics of ageing with clinical detachment. She knew exactly what happened to degrading collagen, what occurred when skin elasticity inevitably snapped, how hair pigmentation quietly died, and what horrors befell the human body as it dragged itself from one decade into the next. She knew the chemical composition of every cream that promised a miracle and every surgical treatment that delivered a tragedy. She knew precisely how to manipulate lighting to make a woman appear younger to a camera, how to plaster over a bone-deep exhaustion, how to conjure the fragile illusion of fullness where time had ruthlessly carved it away.
But intellectual knowledge was a sterile, useless thing. Seeing the decay upon her own flesh was a different terror entirely.
She raised a hand and pressed her fingertips to the sagging skin beneath her eye. The silent reflection mimicked the gesture, mocking her.
For the first time in her entire life, Jennifer did not analyse the woman in the mirror with the calculating eye of a professional. She looked at her as a stranger might have looked at her on the street.
A fifty-eight-year-old woman.
The thought did not arrive with dramatic fanfare. It slipped into her mind quietly, with the absolute, unshakeable weight of a tombstone. That quietness was precisely why it terrified her so completely.
She lowered her shaking hand and stood perfectly, rigidly still. Behind her, the sprawling bedroom remained exactly as it had always been: the pale, tasteful walls, the heavy dark furniture, the dying flowers wilting on the dressing table, the ghostly black dress hanging from its hook, the faint, rhythmic ticking of the heating pipes expanding within the walls. Nothing in her sanctuary had changed.
And yet, standing paralysed before the glass, Jennifer felt an overwhelming, suffocating certainty that something vital and irreplaceable had been violently torn from her without her permission.
She stared into the dead eyes of her reflection for a long, agonising minute. Then, she reached out and pressed her palm flat against the freezing glass, as though the withering woman trapped on the other side might somehow be pushed away, or persuaded to remain exactly where she was.
For several weeks after the night of the party, Jennifer moved through her life meticulously pretending that nothing had fractured. She continued to unlock the salon at the identical hour, continued to inspect her assistants' work with a hawk’s eye beneath the merciless halogen lamps, and continued to dispense advice to clients with the absolute, untouchable confidence she had always wielded as a weapon. She offered hollow laughter exactly when it was socially mandated, attended late dinners, answered relentless messages, bought fresh lilies for the house, and sat beside Ana each evening while the child sounded out her reading books. Yet, an irrevocable tectonic shift had occurred within the private, guarded geography of her mind. The woman in the mirror had transformed from a reflection into a bitter adversary. Jennifer began to notice her everywhere, an inescapable phantom haunting the periphery of her vision—in the dark glass of shop windows as she hurried down the street, in the cold, blank screen of her telephone, in the glossy, candid photographs taken at society parties, and most brutally in the infinite reflections of the salon mirrors whenever she passed behind a seated client. The smallest, most insignificant changes suddenly acquired a terrifying, gravitational importance they had never possessed before. A faint line beside her mouth became a screaming warning; a rogue strand of white hair seemed like undeniable evidence of some fatal, advancing rot; the slight, almost imperceptible looseness beneath her jaw, entirely invisible to everyone else, became a physical weight she could acutely feel whenever she simply turned her head.
She began examining herself beneath a battery of different lights, first in the cold illumination of morning and then in the manufactured glow of night. She would pull back her hair severely, turning her face from side to side, studying the fragile skin beneath her eyes with the same ruthless, diagnostic concentration she had once reserved exclusively for the flawed complexions of her clients. Sometimes she would stand paralysed before the mirror for several minutes and find nothing new, and those were the rare nights when she could breathe, collapsing into bed with a profound, almost weeping relief. Other nights, the glass would betray some tiny, fresh alteration that had escaped her previous audits, and she would crawl between the linen sheets furious with herself, consumed by the failure of having not noticed and combated it sooner.
The salon, her proudest creation, rapidly devolved into a chamber of psychological torture. Jennifer had built the entirety of her life around the architecture of beauty, and now beauty had become the cruel language in which her own inevitable decline was being narrated to her every single day. Young women drifted through her doors carrying the fresh scent of rain and expensive perfume clinging to their coats; they laughed carelessly into their telephones, their thick hair utterly untouched by grey, their skin still possessing the resilient, unthinking elasticity of absolute youth. Jennifer began to ruthlessly compare herself with them, a compulsion she despised but could not arrest. She noticed the taut, unyielding firmness of a nineteen-year-old's neck while her hands worked the shampoo through her hair. She stared at the flawless, porcelain smoothness of a twenty-two-year-old's forehead laid bare beneath the treatment lamps, quietly agonising over the effortless way these girls could leave their faces entirely devoid of makeup and still appear fiercely luminous.
She watched Alice, too, observing her from the sharp corner of her eye while the girl casually arranged heavy garments in the dressing room or stood before the hallway mirror, casually throwing together a new combination of colours for her fashion course. Alice never seemed to actively think about her face. She simply possessed it, occupying her youth like a comfortable, permanent home. Even Ana, with her impossibly soft, flushed cheeks and completely unlined skin, occasionally morphed into an unwelcome, painful reminder of the untainted perfection Jennifer had once owned and lost. One rainy afternoon, while Jennifer was carefully trimming the child's hair in the kitchen, Ana looked up from her illustrated book, her large eyes painfully observant.
"Maa, you have another white hair."
Jennifer immediately put down the steel scissors, the metal clicking sharply against the table. "Don't count them."
"But you have lots."
"Ana."
"You are still beautiful."
Jennifer looked down at the small, earnest face, a lump of cold ash settling in her throat. "I know."
Ana smiled, entirely satisfied with the world, and returned to her book. Jennifer forced her hands to remain steady as she finished the haircut, but the moment she was done, she retreated upstairs, locking the bathroom door and standing before the unforgiving mirror with a pair of sharp, silver tweezers clutched in her trembling hand.
She began her war with the things she could simply buy. There were heavy creams, potent serums, chemical masks, rare oils, acidic peels, and clinical treatments, many of them so exorbitantly expensive that most rational women would have considered the price an obscenity. Jennifer knew the mechanics of the industry intimately enough to distinguish genuine, cellular science from clever, predatory packaging, and yet obsession possesses a dark magic capable of rendering even a fiercely intelligent person susceptible to promises they would once have laughed out of a room. She frantically tried newer, harsher formulations. She continually rotated her night creams, searching for a miracle in a jar. She subjected herself to invasive procedures she had previously cautioned her own clients against using in anything but strict moderation. She discreetly consulted elite specialists in hidden Parisian clinics and travelled out of the city for injections and lifts she did not want her own employees gossiping about.
For a brief, intoxicating while, each new attempt manufactured a fragile hope. Her skin would emerge looking artificially brighter, her face pulled slightly firmer, the shadows beneath her eyes chemically erased. Then, inevitably, the effect would begin to fade, the illusion would crumble, and the mirror would quietly, maliciously return the exact reality she had bled money to escape. She became fundamentally impatient. She was no longer satisfied with the patronising compliment of looking remarkably good for her age. She desperately wanted to look younger than her age, then vastly younger, and eventually she found her mind occupied by the pathological fantasy of a face upon which time had left no footprint at all.
At the salon, she quietly stopped allowing herself to be photographed with her clients. She engineered ways to avoid certain harsh angles. She politely refused invitations to society events where she knew she would be documented standing beside women twenty or thirty years her junior. When someone offered a compliment, she accepted it with her usual manufactured grace, but the moment they turned away, a paranoid voice whispered that the praise had been born out of pity or sheer, patronising politeness. Her terror was no longer that she would slowly become unattractive. The terror that kept her awake was the certainty that one day she would walk into a crowded room and realise, with devastating clarity, that absolutely nobody was looking at her anymore.
She began researching, a pursuit that quickly mutated from idle curiosity into a desperate, consuming hunger.
At first, it was strictly orthodox research. She pored over dense medical journals, obscure dermatological studies, experimental clinical trials, and academic articles dissecting cellular ageing and maximum human longevity. She forced herself to understand collagen degradation, the accumulation of senescent cells, the fraying of telomeres, theoretical stem-cell therapies, and the myriad, wildly funded laboratories around the globe futilely attempting to arrest the biological clock. She learned vastly more than she had ever intended to comprehend, and found, lurking stubbornly beneath the extravagant, gilded promises of the beauty industry, a bleak and unyielding truth that refused to be softened by clever advertising: time was not a cosmetic problem; it was a terminal disease. Some surgeries could violently alter an appearance, chemical medicines that could delay specific decay, and emerging research that might someday extend a healthy lifespan, but there existed absolutely nothing on earth that could return a fifty-eight-year-old woman to the cellular body she had possessed at twenty-one.
The more she read, the more suffocating her desperation became, because this clinical knowledge had brutally stripped away her comforting illusions without offering a single, viable alternative. One night, long after the house had fallen silent, with Ana deep in sleep and Alice retired to her room, Jennifer sat alone in the heavy oak study with only the singular beam of the desk lamp cutting through the gloom. Rain tapped a relentless, erratic rhythm against the tall windows. She clicked through one peer-reviewed medical paper after another, descending slowly through legitimate forums into encrypted, private discussions, and finally into obscure, poorly coded pages whose addresses seemed to appear and disappear like ghosts in the machine. She followed cryptic links that bled into other links, eventually finding herself wandering through corners of the dark internet she would once have dismissed with absolute, aristocratic contempt.
There were stories festering in these depths of impossible, miraculous cures, hidden, unmapped clinics, people who claimed to have existed far beyond ordinary human limits, and frantic, disjointed accounts from women who swore they had regained entire decades of lost youth after meeting a certain individual in France. Most of it was transparent, unhinged nonsense. Jennifer’s rational mind knew that perfectly well. Yet, buried among the digital rot and desperate fantasies, one specific name kept surfacing like a drowned body.
Hazel.
The name appeared exceedingly rarely, and it never lingered in the same digital space twice. There were no photographs that Jennifer could possibly trust, no verified address, and no telephone number to call. Some disjointed accounts claimed this Hazel lived somewhere near Bordeaux. Others labelled her a miraculous healer, a dangerous witch, an underground doctor, a brilliant fraud, or simply an ancient, terrifying woman who understood something that modern medicine aggressively denied. Most of the frantic testimonials had been scrubbed from the internet entirely by the time Jennifer attempted to return to them. Only a few scattered remnants survived, written in broken, terrified French and English by women who seemed far too paralysed by fear to explain exactly what had transpired in the dark. One woman wrote, in a trembling font, that Hazel possessed the power to give back “the face one had before the years.” Another stated only that she had travelled to Bordeaux looking like a dying crone and had returned looking like a bride. A third had left only a single, chilling sentence before the entire page vanished into the void:
Nothing is given for nothing.
Jennifer stared at those words, glowing harshly on the monitor, for a very long time. The silence of the house pressed against her spine.
Then, her fingers moving almost against her own will, she searched for Hazel again.
This time, she found a fragmented address.
It was located in Bordeaux, situated aggressively far from the fashionable, pulsing heart of the city, hidden beyond neglected streets that grew increasingly desolate and quiet as the sun retreated. Jennifer almost laughed aloud when she finally pinpointed the location on the digital map. It seemed utterly absurd. She had spent gruelling weeks navigating dense scientific literature and cutting-edge medical research, and now the ultimate answer—if there was an answer to be found at all—appeared to be waiting inside a rotting, forgotten house at the dead end of an unremarkable road. She firmly told herself she was not going because she actually believed the deranged stories. She was going merely to satisfy her own exhausting obsession, to finally discover the mundane fraud hiding behind the myth, and to put the madness to rest.
She booked a discreet room, packed a small, practical suitcase, and informed Alice that an urgent matter required her to travel south for business.
Ana looked up from her breakfast, her brow furrowing. "Only two nights?"
"Only two nights," Jennifer promised, forcing a lightness into her tone.
"Will you come back before Sunday?"
"Of course I will."
Ana nodded, satisfied with the contract, and returned to her book. Jennifer kissed the crown of her head, lingering for a second to inhale the scent of her child.
On the grey, overcast morning of her departure, Alice insisted on helping carry the small suitcase out to the waiting car.
"Business?" she asked, her tone entirely casual.
"Something like that."
Alice smiled, a knowing look in her eyes. "You never tell me anything."
"There is nothing of interest to tell." Jennifer firmly closed the boot of the car, the mechanical thud finalising the departure.
Alice looked at her for a long, quiet moment, the smile fading into something resembling genuine concern. "Be careful."
Jennifer laughed sharply. "Of what?"
Alice shrugged, wrapping her arms around herself against the morning chill. "I don't know. Bordeaux."
Jennifer stepped forward and kissed her cheek. "I assure you, I will survive Bordeaux."
She drove south beneath a pale, bruised sky that seemed to press heavily against the roof of the car.
By the time she finally navigated to the fragmented address, a thick, bruised evening had already begun to fall over the countryside. The road steadily narrowed, the tarmac giving way to broken gravel, until the surrounding houses seemed to physically retreat from one another, leaving long, unnerving stretches of absolute darkness between them. The directions led her to an ancient property surrounded by a high, crumbling stone wall. The heavy iron gate hung crookedly from a single, rusted hinge, groaning softly in the wind. Beyond it stood a house that appeared to have been violently abandoned decades ago. The pale plaster had sloughed away in great, diseased patches, exposing the dark, wet stone beneath; several of the tall windows were severely cracked, others blindly boarded over with rotting wood, and thick, aggressive weeds had completely swallowed what might once have been a formal garden. The slate roof sagged dangerously toward one side, and the bare, twisted branches of an enormous, dead tree leaned heavily over the structure like black, grasping fingers.
Jennifer remained locked inside the running car, her hands gripping the leather steering wheel.
This was ridiculous. It was a mistake born of sheer, exhausted madness. She reached for the gearshift, fully intending to turn around and flee back to the sanity of Paris.
Then, she looked into the rearview mirror. She saw the lines. She saw the white hair. She remembered the actor turning his back to her.
She turned off the engine and opened the door.
The broken gate made a long, agonising shriek of metal upon metal when she forced it open. The ruined path beneath her expensive shoes was slick with fresh rain, and the heavy, suffocating smell of damp earth and rotting vegetation rose thickly from the weeds. Somewhere nearby, water dripped steadily from a shattered gutter, sounding like a ticking clock in the unnatural silence. Jennifer climbed the crumbling stone steps and knocked firmly upon the heavy wood.
Nothing happened. The house absorbed the sound entirely.
She knocked again, harder this time.
The door opened with a slow, creeping silence.
The woman standing behind it was impossibly, terrifyingly old. Jennifer could not have even begun to guess her age; Hazel's face seemed to exist entirely beyond the ordinary, cruel arithmetic of human time. Her skin was paper-thin and so deeply, intricately furrowed it resembled a map of dried riverbeds. Her hair was a sparse, ghostly grey, and her frail shoulders were severely bent beneath the weight of a heavy, dark woollen dress that smelled of dust and closed rooms. Her eyes, however, were horribly clear. They were dark and sharp, and they rested upon Jennifer without a single flicker of surprise.
"You came," she said. Her voice was a dry rasp, like dead leaves dragging across stone.
Jennifer stared at her, an icy prickle of dread crawling up her spine. "You know who I am?"
Hazel did not bother to answer. She merely stepped aside, dissolving into the gloom of the hallway.
Jennifer hesitated for one final, rational second, then stepped over the threshold.
The smell of the house was the first thing that assaulted her senses. It was a heavy, suffocating atmosphere of damp stone, rotting wood, long-extinguished fires, and something sharply medicinal and metallic that she could not quite identify. The air was unnaturally cold, biting through her coat despite the mild season, and somewhere deeper inside the lightless bowels of the house, water continued to drip at maddening, regular intervals. Hazel closed the heavy door behind her, sealing them in the dark.
Jennifer looked around, her eyes struggling to adjust to the oppressive dimness. "Are you Hazel?"
The ancient woman offered a single, slow nod.
"I found your name online. People say things about you."
Hazel looked at her, those clear, dark eyes pinning Jennifer to the floorboards. "You want to be young."
It was not a question. It was a dissection.
Jennifer felt a sudden, profound drop in her stomach, an unexpected chill that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room. "Yes."
Hazel turned without another word and began to walk down a narrow, suffocating passage.
Having come this far into the madness, Jennifer followed.
They descended a steep, winding staircase that seemed to plunge directly into the earth. The basement walls were rough, bare stone, slick and weeping with moisture in places, and the stone steps were treacherously uneven beneath Jennifer's shoes. At the very bottom of the descent stood a chair unlike anything she had ever expected to find in a domestic house. It was heavy, rigidly upright, and constructed of a dark, cold material that might once have been industrial metal. Thick, leather straps hung limply from its heavy arms and lay coiled across its back.
Jennifer stopped dead at the base of the stairs, her breath catching in her throat. "What is that?"
Hazel slowly turned her head and looked at the chair. "You sit."
Jennifer did not move a muscle. Every instinct screamed at her to run. "What happens?"
Hazel raised a skeletal finger and pointed toward a small, rusted table nearby. Upon it lay a single, neatly folded sheet of thick parchment paper.
Jennifer approached it cautiously, her heart hammering against her ribs, and picked it up.
The handwriting upon the page was ancient, spidery, and uneven, as though written by a hand that frequently forgot how to hold a pen. There was no signature. No greeting. Only the terms of a nightmare.
She read:
Let youth return. Let beauty bloom. Let the years pass and leave no mark.
For every six months, the gift shall be renewed. For every six months, a living girl beneath twenty-one shall be given.
Give the offering, and remain untouched by time.
Withhold it, and the gift shall turn against you. The years shall return. The flesh shall wither. And the beauty once bestowed shall become the curse upon your face.
Jennifer read the grotesque words again, her mind violently rejecting the information. She looked up at Hazel, her voice trembling with a mixture of terror and sudden, blinding anger.
"What does this mean?"
Hazel remained perfectly, infuriatingly silent.
"Is this some kind of sick joke?"
No answer. Only the dripping of the water in the dark.
Jennifer forced a laugh, a harsh, brittle sound devoid of any humour. "This is what people actually believe? You extort desperate women with fairy tales of human sacrifice?"
Hazel's clear eyes did not leave her face. They held no malice, no amusement, only an ancient, terrifying patience.
Jennifer looked back down at the heavy paper. The words were undeniably grotesque. They were ridiculous. They were physically impossible. And yet, holding the paper in her trembling hands, standing in the freezing damp of the basement, she could not stop looking at them. A sickening, quiet part of her brain was already calculating the logistics.
"How does it work?" she whispered.
Hazel gave a minute shrug of her bent shoulders.
"What happens to the girl?"
Hazel did not answer. She simply waited.
Jennifer's manicured fingers tightened convulsively around the edges of the paper, crumpling the ancient script. "You want a human sacrifice? Every six months?"
Hazel nodded, a single, definitive motion.
"And if I refuse?"
Hazel slowly raised her hand and pointed directly to the heavy, strapped chair.
Jennifer understood the silent architecture of the moment. She could leave. Absolutely nothing physical prevented her from turning around and walking up the stairs. The door was behind her, unlocked. Her expensive car was waiting outside. The city of Bordeaux was still out there, entirely indifferent and reassuringly ordinary. She could drive back to Paris, back to Ana's warm hugs, back to Alice's quiet competence, back to the bustling salon, and back to her miserable mirrors, expensive creams, and futile treatments. She could simply grow old, decaying slowly and humiliatingly like everyone else in the world.
Hazel waited, offering neither encouragement nor threat.
Jennifer looked at the ominous chair again. It looked like an instrument of execution. Then she looked down at the crumpled paper in her hand.
"How young?" she asked, her voice sounding hollow, like it belonged to a stranger.
Hazel pointed a crooked finger to the exact words on the page.
"Less than twenty-one," Jennifer read aloud. "Alive?"
Hazel nodded again.
Jennifer swallowed hard, her throat painfully dry. "And I have to bring her here to you? Myself?"
Hazel nodded once more. There was absolutely no threat in her ancient face. No coercion. No judgment. That, somehow, made the ensuing decision infinitely worse. It left the weight of the moral rot entirely upon Jennifer’s own shoulders.
Jennifer squeezed her eyes shut. In the darkness behind her eyelids, she saw the handsome actor at the party turning his back on her. She saw the glowing, effortless face of the young woman he had chosen. She saw her own sagging, lined reflection glaring back at her from the bathroom glass. And then, briefly, she saw Ana sleeping peacefully with her soft unicorn resting beside her.
She opened her eyes, burying her conscience in the shallow grave of her vanity.
"I agree."
Hazel immediately stepped towards the heavy chair, gesturing for her to sit.
Jennifer approached it like a woman walking to the gallows, and lowered herself onto the cold seat. Almost instantly, Hazel moved with a terrifying, fluid speed that belied her ancient frame. The thick leather straps closed around Jennifer's delicate wrists, across her waist, and tightly around her ankles with a series of heavy, metallic clicks. They were not drawn tight enough to cause physical pain, but they were relentlessly firm, making it instantly, chillingly clear that she would not be leaving this basement until Hazel permitted it.
Jennifer stared blindly upwards into the gloom. A strange, mechanical apparatus loomed above her, suspended from the stone ceiling. Its surface was dark and highly polished, resembling obsidian, with a large, circular glass aperture set directly at its centre.
"What is that?" panic finally bleeding into her voice.
Hazel did not answer. She never answered.
The machine engaged. The circular light came on.
It was impossibly, violently bright. For one agonising instant, Jennifer saw absolutely nothing but a blinding, searing white. The stone room vanished. The straps vanished. Hazel vanished. There was only the terrible, consuming brightness burning into her retinas, followed immediately by a physical sensation she would never be able to accurately describe: it was neither burning heat nor cold, neither excruciating pain nor rapturous pleasure, but something immense passing directly through the cellular structure of her body so completely that she felt, for one bewildering, terrifying moment, as though her flesh had become entirely weightless, dissolving into the air itself.
Then, there was only darkness.
Jennifer tried to drag air into her lungs, gasping like a drowning woman breaking the surface. The darkness surrounding her no longer smelled of damp stone and rot; it seemed soft, expansive, and incredibly fresh.
She heard the gentle lapping of water. Then, the sharp, melodic chirping of birds. Then, a peaceful, stretching silence.
When she finally gathered the strength to open her eyes, she found herself lying flat on her back upon a bed of cool, damp grass.
For several disorienting seconds, she could not comprehend where she was or how she had arrived there. The sky above her was a brilliant, unblemished blue, free of the oppressive clouds that had choked Bordeaux. There were tall, leafy trees swaying nearby, their branches moving gently in a warm breeze. Somewhere just beyond the tree line, she could hear the bright, uncomplicated laughter of children. She registered the distant, muffled hum of city traffic and the faint, cheerful ringing of a passing bicycle bell. The air she breathed smelled richly of damp earth and warm sunlight, carrying a clean, floral sweetness that reminded her viscerally of spring, though it had been November when she entered the house.
Jennifer pushed herself up, sitting up slowly. She was in a public park. A broad, ornamental pool of water lay just beyond a low stone retaining wall, its surface flashing brightly beneath the afternoon sun.
She rose to her feet, swaying slightly. Her body felt profoundly strange. It felt lighter. The chronic, dull ache that usually nested in her lower back was entirely gone. Her knees supported her weight without their usual, brittle protest.
Drawn by an instinct she dared not voice, she walked slowly toward the edge of the water.
At first, looking down, she saw only the trembling, broken reflection of the blue sky. Then, the water stilled, and she looked deeper.
A young woman looked back at her.
Jennifer froze; her breath trapped painfully in her chest.
The face staring up from the water was impossibly smooth and radiantly clear. The delicate skin beneath the eyes, which only hours ago had been shadowed and lined, was taut and flawless. The jawline was sharp, delicate, and entirely unmarked by gravity. Her hair fell thickly around her shoulders, a dark, glossy, heavy curtain without a single, traitorous strand of white hidden within it.
She slowly raised a trembling hand and touched her cheek. The girl in the water perfectly mirrored the movement.
Jennifer's breath hitched, a desperate sound escaping her lips. She turned her face sharply from side to side. The reflection tracked her flawlessly.
There was no mistake. It was not a trick of the light or a hallucination born of trauma. The vibrant, glowing woman staring back at her from the depths of the pool could not have been a day over twenty-one.
She remained anchored beside the water for a very long time, utterly unable to form a word. Then, a sound broke from her throat—she laughed. It was a bright, silver, genuinely young woman’s laugh. She slapped both hands over her mouth, her eyes wide with shock and a terrifying, euphoric joy. For the first time in over three decades, Jennifer did not see the ghost of the past when she looked into a mirror. She simply saw herself.
The sudden, piercing thought of Paris rushed back to her. Ana. Alice. The bustling salon. Her meticulously curated house, her garden, the expensive, conservative clothes hanging in her wardrobe, the framed photographs on the walls—all the ordinary, heavy things that had belonged to a woman of fifty-eight now seemed absurdly, comically distant from the vibrant person standing beside the water. She looked once more at her flawless reflection and smiled a predatory, triumphant smile. Whatever impossible magic Hazel had wielded in that rotting basement, whatever strange, macabre bargain she had just bound her soul to, it had undeniably worked. There was no surgical scar, no lingering trace of illness, no unnatural mark upon her skin to suggest that anything horrific had occurred. She looked young. Not merely 'younger than she had been,' but young in the absolute, purest sense of the word, as though thirty-seven years of existence had simply been surgically excised from her timeline and thrown away.
The long journey back to Paris felt entirely dreamlike. She drove for hours with the driver's side window rolled halfway down, letting the cold, rushing air tangle wildly through her thick, dark hair. At the first highway service station, she stopped simply to stand before the automatic glass doors and catch sight of her reflection again. She beamed at the beautiful stranger in the glass, and the young woman smiled brilliantly back. She bought a cheap black coffee and stood outside beneath the pale afternoon sky, listening to the distant, rhythmic roar of the highway traffic, intimately feeling the strange, intoxicating lightness of her own newly minted body. Her joints moved with a fluid grace she had entirely forgotten. Her hands, gripping the paper cup, looked impossibly delicate and unblemished. Even the simple, mechanical act of walking across the tarmac seemed to contain a forgotten, electric pleasure.
By the time she finally navigated her car onto her own quiet street, deep evening had fallen over Paris.
She unlocked the heavy front door and stepped into the warm hallway. Ana opened the door to the sitting room.
For several agonising seconds, the child simply stood in her socks, staring at her. The little girl was holding the enormous stuffed unicorn tightly against her chest, its soft, lavender mane resting securely beside her own dark hair. Ana looked at Jennifer with absolute, blank non-recognition, then instinctively glanced behind the young woman, as if expecting her actual mother to walk through the door a moment later.
"Maa?" she asked, her small voice laced with profound caution.
Jennifer smiled brilliantly, dropping her keys onto the hall table. "It’s me."
Ana immediately took a step backwards, clutching the toy tighter. "No."
The word was so small, so definitive, that Jennifer almost laughed, entirely convinced that the child was simply playing a game or had misunderstood the lighting. "It is me, darling. I'm home."
Ana shook her head violently. Her wide eyes moved frantically over Jennifer's smooth, unlined face, scanning her with the careful, defensive suspicion children sometimes reserve for strangers who inexplicably know their names. "You are not my Maa."
Something heavy and cold plummeted inside Jennifer’s chest, extinguishing the euphoria of the drive. The reality of the transformation suddenly showed its teeth.
She slowly sank to her knees upon the hardwood floor, bringing herself down to the child's eye level. "Look at me, Ana. Please look at me."
The child did not move an inch, her small body rigid with terror.
Jennifer’s mind raced. She desperately remembered the unicorn. She remembered the small, frayed blue ribbon tied carefully around its silver horn because Ana had once tearfully complained that the creature looked sad without one. She remembered the specific picture book they had read the night before she left for Bordeaux, the worn copy about the little girl who built a ladder to catch the moon. She remembered the precise way Ana always demanded the same ending and stubbornly never allowed Jennifer to skip the final page, even when it was late.
Kneeling on the floor, Jennifer began speaking, telling her the story. She did not simply recite the words printed in the book, but wove in the intimate, secret rituals that belonged exclusively to the two of them—the exact, silly questions Ana always interrupted with, the exaggerated, ridiculous answers Jennifer always provided, the specific page where Jennifer always pretended to forget the hero's name just so that Ana could triumphantly correct her.
Slowly, agonisingly slowly, the terror in the child's face began to fracture and change. The suspicion gave way to a profound, bewildered recognition.
"Maa?" she whispered, her voice trembling.
Jennifer nodded, tears suddenly spilling over her flawless, youthful cheeks.
Ana dropped the enormous unicorn to the floor. She ran forward and threw herself into Jennifer's arms.
Jennifer caught her, holding the small, warm body so tightly it bordered on pain, and buried her face in the child's hair, squeezing her eyes shut. She had imagined many terrible consequences during the long drive home from Bordeaux, but she had never anticipated the sheer, paralysing terror of being entirely forgotten by the one person whose recognition mattered more to her than all the mirrors in Paris combined.
Alice appeared in the hallway a moment later, wiping her hands on a dish towel, and stopped dead in her tracks.
The young nanny stared at Jennifer, kneeling on the floor, then down at Ana clinging desperately to the stranger's neck. All colour drained rapidly from Alice's face.
"Who are you?" Alice demanded, her voice sharp with sudden panic, stepping protectively forward.
Jennifer let out a wet, genuine laugh through the tears still shining in her eyes. "Apparently, Alice, I have a very complicated, entirely impossible explanation to give you."
It took nearly an hour of sitting at the kitchen table before Alice even began to believe her. Even then, the belief did not stem from Jennifer’s wild, impossible explanation, but from the undeniable accumulation of small, domestic impossibilities. Jennifer knew exactly where she kept Alice's graded university papers hidden; she knew which specific drawer contained Ana's mothballed winter clothes; she knew which chipped mug Alice religiously used for her evening tea; and she knew precisely which loose floorboard in the upstairs corridor let out a sharp squeak when stepped upon. She knew every intimate, private habit of the household because she was the architect who had built it.
At last, Alice sat back heavily in her chair, staring across the table in absolute, terrified bewilderment. "You really found a way to become young? This isn't surgery. This is... impossible."
Jennifer smiled, the expression looking strange and unsettling on such a youthful face. "I found a way to become myself again."
She did not tell them about the rotting house in Bordeaux. And she certainly did not tell them about Hazel, or the price written on the parchment paper.
For several days, Paris seemed to have transmuted into an entirely different city. When Jennifer walked into the salon on Monday morning, draped in a sharp black coat, she carried herself with the vibrating, nervous electricity of a woman stepping into a dream she had relentlessly chased her entire life. Her own employees did not recognise her. One of the junior stylists, looking blankly at the flawless young woman standing in the reception area, actually stepped forward and asked whether she had an appointment to see the owner.
Jennifer let out a bright, chiming laugh. "I am afraid I am the owner."
A stunned, suffocating silence descended upon the room. Scissors paused in mid-air. Hair dryers were clicked off.
Then came the frantic deluge of questions. The story Jennifer eventually engineered for them was simple, elegant, and entirely impenetrable: an exclusive private treatment, a highly experimental cellular procedure, a remarkable reversal of ageing that she herself was still navigating with her doctors. She provided absolutely no names and offered no clinical details. She quickly learned that the more stubbornly mysterious she remained, the more extraordinary and coveted the narrative became.
Within a week, the salon was bursting at the seams. Women who had known Jennifer for years pushed through the glass doors and stopped dead in sheer astonishment. Some stared with naked, hungry envy. Others reached out and touched her bare arm, as though they desperately needed tactile proof that she was not an illusion. A few whispered demanding questions about offshore surgeries and hidden scars. Jennifer would only offer a serene, untouched smile and murmur that she had discovered something truly wonderful.
The heritage brands noticed immediately. Photographers began circling with intrusive questions. Elite beauty magazines pleaded for exclusive interviews, offering lavish spreads. Wealthy clients who had once waited weeks for an appointment now willingly waited months, desperate to breathe the same air. Jennifer became, almost overnight, the most devastatingly convincing advertisement her own vain profession could have ever asked for. She had spent thirty long years selling the fragile possibility of beauty, and now she appeared to have physically stepped out of the promise herself.
Her technical knowledge had not diminished; if anything, her work became infinitely sharper. She understood these ageing women with a cruel, crystalline clarity now. She knew exactly why they lingered at the mirrors before leaving the salon, why they nervously touched the corners of their eyes when they mistakenly thought nobody was watching, and why a rational woman might gladly bankrupt herself for a chemical cream whose only active ingredient was desperate hope. Jennifer had once arrogantly believed beauty was a tangible asset a person possessed. Now, she understood that for most of the world, beauty was merely a ghost they were terrified of losing.
And she could effortlessly sell them the hope of catching it.
Money flowed in like a rising tide. Coveted invitations arrived faster than she could answer them. The first party was held at a gilded hotel overlooking the Seine; the second was an intimate gathering at the private estate of a ruthless fashion editor. Then came a relentless blur of exclusive dinners, gallery openings, hidden clubs, and sprawling terraces lit by flickering candles and overflowing with vintage champagne beneath the summer skies. Jennifer began receiving lavish attention from the very people she had spent decades trying to impress, discovering with vicious amusement that sheer, radiant youth kicked open heavy doors which money alone could not budge.
Men looked at her differently. It amused her at first, a novel distraction. Then, it intoxicated her.
She danced again, her body fluid and tireless. She stayed out until the sky bruised purple. She wore daring, architectural dresses she had aggressively stopped buying years ago, having convinced herself they belonged only to women in their twenties. She bought them now without a second of hesitation. There were intoxicating evenings when she would return to the townhouse long after midnight, kick off her punishing heels in the shadowed hallway, and stand before the antique mirror, laughing until her ribs ached at the sheer, beautiful absurdity of it all.
Once, at a crowded party, a handsome young actor leaned in close, smelling of gin and expensive tobacco, and boldly asked her age.
Jennifer took a slow, deliberate sip of champagne, her dark eyes flashing over the rim of the crystal. "How old do you think I am?"
He studied her flawless face for a long, calculating moment. "Twenty-four?"
She laughed, the sound cutting cleanly through the heavy music. "Then keep your answer."
She began seeing him. When she grew bored, she discarded him and took another lover. Then another. Nothing lasted very long, and Jennifer never asked it to. She had spent too many bitter years feeling that the vibrant current of life was rushing past her. Now, she intended to move faster than time itself.
Through all this frantic consumption, Ana remained the quiet, gravitational centre of her life. Jennifer still ate breakfast with her every morning, listening attentively to her rambling school stories. She still trimmed her dark hair in the kitchen, and still read to her every night she was home. Alice continued taking the child to school, meticulously managing her lessons and keeping the heavy house in perfect, silent order. The comforting, domestic rhythms of the household remained entirely undisturbed, even though the matriarch at its centre had mutated beyond recognition.
Sometimes, Ana would sit beside her on the sofa and reach out to touch her dark, heavy hair.
"You don't have any white ones anymore."
"No, I don't."
"Why?"
Jennifer would smile, a soft, maternal expression that masked a chilling void. "Because Maa found a very good secret."
Ana considered this with absolute seriousness. "Can I have it when I'm old?"
Jennifer leaned down and kissed her warm forehead. "When you are very, very old."
The months passed with a deceptive, frictionless speed. Spring broke softly over Paris, and then the heavy, golden heat of summer arrived. The meticulously tended garden filled again with violent bursts of colour. The city grew warm and wonderfully noisy, its crowded cafés spilling out onto the pavements, its fragrant evenings stretching long and lazy beneath the pale sky. Jennifer felt as though she had been miraculously handed back a life she had mistakenly, bitterly mourned as over.
There were quiet mornings when she woke long before Ana and stood completely alone before the bathroom mirror, studying herself in the grey light. Twenty-one. The thought still violently astonished her. She sometimes tried to mentally reconstruct the haggard, lined face she had worn before the drive to Bordeaux. It became surprisingly difficult. The memory of her older self was slipping away like water through cupped hands. That phenomenon frightened her only once. Then, she simply laughed at her own anxiety. Why should she force herself to remember an exhausted, dying woman when she had finally become the flawless creature she had always deserved to be?
Hazel's name began to fade. It did not disappear completely—Jennifer still occasionally remembered the rotting house in Bordeaux, the suffocating, damp smell of its stone walls, the terrifying chair in the basement, and the ancient woman's silent, judgmental face hovering over the handwritten bargain. But trauma and memory, like everything else, become soft and malleable when nothing urgently demands their attention.
The first six months passed almost beautifully. Jennifer scarcely noticed the subtle shifting of the seasons. Paris cooled, moving from the stifling warmth of summer into the softer, bruised colours of autumn, and dead leaves began to gather like dry bones along the pavements outside the salon. Her new life had become so intensely full, so fiercely bright, that there seemed to be no empty, shadowed space left in it for the past to hide.
It was on an entirely ordinary, unremarkable morning in October that the illusion shattered.
She was standing before the bathroom mirror with one hand raised, idly deciding whether to wash her thick hair before leaving for the salon, when she saw it. It was a single, rigid strand near her temple, thin and glittering like silver wire in the sharp morning light. For a long, paralysed moment, she simply stared at it, her lungs refusing to expand. Then, with a trembling surge of panic, she pinched it between her nails and ripped it free.
She laughed. The sound was thin, nervous, and scraped against the tiles.
"Ridiculous."
She held the dead strand between her fingers and examined it under the harsh vanity bulb. It was undeniably real. She leaned aggressively close to the mirror and mapped her face. The skin was still tight and smooth, the dark eyes were bright, the jaw remained utterly untouched by gravity. Nothing else had decayed. Perhaps it was simply an abnormal strand. Perhaps the light had merely caught a blond highlight and made it appear white.
She threw the hair into the wastebasket, forcing her heart rate to slow, and walked downstairs.
Ana was already eating toast at the kitchen table, her heavy schoolbag resting beside her chair, and the great stuffed unicorn lying across the hardwood floor as though it had collapsed in exhaustion. Alice was calmly pouring cold milk into a glass.
"You're late," Alice noted, not looking up.
"I know."
"You promised you would have breakfast with her."
"I am having breakfast with her."
Ana looked up, her mouth ringed with jam, and smiled. "Maa, you are always late."
Jennifer kissed the crown of her daughter's head and sat down heavily. She violently forced herself to forget the hair. By the time she had finished her bitter coffee, she almost believed she had.
That night, the house in Bordeaux came for her in the dark.
She dreamed she was standing once again beside the ornamental park pool, but there was no manicured grass around it and no blue sky above. The water stretched endlessly in every direction, a vast, freezing ocean, black as spilt ink and perfectly, unnervingly still. She looked down into the void and saw her own reflection, glowing and beautiful. Then, something shifted in the suffocating depths beneath the water. At first, it was only a pale, bloated shadow, but slowly the shape drifted upward until she could clearly see the dead, white face of a young girl staring up at her from beneath the surface.
Jennifer scrambled backwards, her silent screams trapped in her throat.
The drowned girl opened her eyes. She was so very young. And then, her waterlogged lips stretched into a terrible smile.
Another pale face floated up beside hers. And another. And another. Soon, the entire black surface of the water was crowded with dozens of silent, dead young women, their dead eyes fixed upward on Jennifer. She tried to run, to tear herself away from the shoreline, but her feet were rooted to the damp earth. Somewhere close behind her, breathing cold air against her neck, Hazel spoke in a voice that sounded like grinding stones.
Every six months.
Jennifer woke violently, bolting upright in the pitch-black bedroom, a strangled gasp tearing from her lungs.
For several panicked seconds, she did not know where she was. The bedroom was tomb-quiet. Icy rain tapped relentlessly against the windowpane like fingertips. Beside her, the digital clock bled red into the dark: 3:17 AM.
Then, the crushing reality returned.
Hazel.
The basement.
The handwritten contract.
The bargain.
Jennifer threw off the heavy duvet and stumbled into the bathroom. She violently slapped the light switch and pressed her face close to the mirror. Her face was still young, but suddenly she could no longer see the youth; she could only see the impending rot waiting just beneath the porcelain surface. She frantically parted her hair with shaking, desperate fingers.
She found two more stark white strands buried near her temple.
She did not sleep again.
The next morning, she drove to the salon an hour earlier than usual, her hands gripping the leather wheel with white-knuckled terror. She kept the radio completely off. Paris passed beyond the rain-streaked windshield in a grey, meaningless blur of buses and umbrellas, but Jennifer saw none of it. She was obsessively counting the months in her head. Six months. It had been exactly six months to the week since she walked into that basement.
By noon, sitting in her pristine office, she had frantically tried to convince herself that the nightmare meant nothing. By three o'clock, she knew with absolute, terrifying certainty that she was lying to herself.
She locked her private office door and sat in the silence. For almost an hour, she did not touch the computer or her phone. The memory of Hazel's handwritten letter had resurrected itself in her mind with photographic, agonising clarity. She could see the texture of the old paper, the dark, rusty ink, the archaic phrasing. Most of all, the final, inescapable condition pounded against the inside of her skull like a hammer.
A living girl beneath twenty-one.
Jennifer leaned back in her expensive leather chair and squeezed her eyes shut. "No," she whispered.
The word sounded pathetic and utterly useless in the empty room.
She opened her eyes and looked through the glass partition that overlooked the bustling salon floor.
Rose was there.
Nineteen years old, staggeringly beautiful, vibrating with life, and completely at ease. She had come in out of the rain for her usual, bi-weekly appointment. She was sitting comfortably beneath the warm halogen lights while a junior assistant gently brushed out her damp hair. When she caught sight of Jennifer through the glass, she lifted a delicate hand and beamed.
"Jennifer! I was beginning to think you had completely forgotten about me."
Jennifer forced the corners of her mouth upward into a flawless, maternal smile. "I could never forget you, Rose."
Rose laughed, a bright, chiming sound. "Good. I have a major photoshoot next week, so I need you to work your absolute magic. Make me impossible."
"You already are."
Jennifer watched the girl for a moment longer than was professionally appropriate. The ticking clock in her mind grew deafening.
Rose had been patronising the salon for nearly a year. She trusted Jennifer implicitly. She knew her moods, her favourite expensive products, and the charming, self-deprecating stories she told when she was exhausted. She would frequently linger long after her appointments simply to drink coffee in the breakroom and gossip about her university classes, her burgeoning modelling career, the men who pursued her, and the bright, limitless ambitions that mapped out her long future. Jennifer intimately knew that Rose's mother lived two hours outside of Paris, that she had two close friends at college who were currently travelling, and that she harboured dreams of dominating French cinema.
That late afternoon, when the rain grew heavier, and the final clients had buttoned their coats and drifted away into the twilight, Jennifer emerged from her office. She calmly told her four remaining employees that she had administrative work to finish and that they were free to lock their stations and go home early.
Rose was waiting comfortably in the dim, tranquil treatment room.
"You are doing my facial yourself?" she asked, looking up with genuine, delighted surprise as Jennifer entered and closed the heavy door.
"I thought I would spoil you today."
"I knew there was a reason you were my favourite."
Jennifer moved through the room with methodical, terrifying precision. The familiar, soothing fragrance of botanical cleansers and heavy creams filled the enclosed space, masking the faint, sterile scent of the medical supplies kept locked in the lower cabinets. Rose lay back upon the heated table with a soft sigh, her dark hair pulled securely away from her flawless face by a white cloth band.
"Close your eyes," Jennifer murmured.
Rose obeyed instantly, surrendering entirely to the older woman's care.
For several agonising minutes, there was only the quiet, rhythmic sound of Jennifer moving around the dim room. She applied the cool cleanser with expert, massaging strokes, speaking to Rose in the same low, hypnotic cadence she had used a hundred times before. Rose melted beneath her hands, her breathing growing deep, slow, and trusting.
Jennifer stepped back and stood silently beside the treatment table, looking down at the girl.
For one terrible, suspended moment, a wave of revulsion crashed over her. She imagined simply walking away. She imagined going home to Ana, accepting the white hair, and letting the relentless years take back everything they had temporarily lent her. She vividly imagined her skin sagging, her bones aching, and looking into the mirror to see the dying old woman return.
Then, she remembered the euphoric rush of waking up young on the grass. She remembered the admiring, hungry stares of men, the jealous whispers of her peers, and the absolute, God-like power of being flawless. She remembered Ana running into her arms, and the sheer terror of being an unrecognisable crone to her own daughter.
Jennifer reached into the lower cabinet. The glass syringe was small, cold, and easily hidden in the palm of her hand.
She stepped back to the table and stood over Rose for several long, suffocating seconds.
Rose stirred slightly, a frown ghosting across her brow. "Jennifer?"
"Just relax, darling," Jennifer whispered, her voice devoid of any human warmth.
The girl's eyes fluttered open for a fraction of a second, clouded and confused. Then, as the needle pierced the delicate skin of her neck, they rolled back and closed permanently.
Jennifer stood perfectly still, watching the young chest rise and fall as the chemical dragged the girl down into the dark.
When she was certain Rose was unconscious, she stripped off her protective gloves and began to frantically prepare the room. Her hands were shaking violently. She violently hated her own weakness. She had always prided herself on being a practical, ruthless woman who could execute difficult decisions without collapsing. Yet, when she finally dragged Rose up from the treatment table, the dead weight of the young woman's limp body shocked her. She was heavy, a dense burden of muscle and bone, and for several frantic moments, Jennifer had to lean against the wall, her lungs burning, trying to catch her breath.
The rear exit of the salon opened directly onto a narrow, lightless service alley. Jennifer had deliberately parked her car flush against the loading doors. She dragged Rose out into the freezing rain, concealing the girl's slumped form beneath a heavy trench coat, freezing in terror every time a distant noise echoed off the brick. No one stopped her. No one asked questions. The indifferent city of Paris continued entirely uninterrupted. A delivery truck groaned past at the far end of the alley. Someone laughed brightly from a lit upstairs window. Somewhere close by, a muffled radio was playing a pop song Jennifer had heard a hundred times.
She hauled Rose up and let her drop heavily into the open trunk of the car.
Before slamming the lid shut, Jennifer paused, staring down at the girl's face in the pale glow of the alley light. Rose simply appeared to be in a deep, peaceful sleep. The absolute betrayal of it tasted like copper in Jennifer's mouth. She reached out, her trembling fingers hovering inches from the girl's soft cheek.
Then, her jaw tightened, and she slammed the trunk shut, locking the nightmare in the dark.
The drive to Bordeaux stretched into an eternity. She kept both hands clamped on the steering wheel, driving in absolute, suffocating silence. The sky darkened into a bruised purple, and by the time she finally navigated the outskirts of the southern city, a torrential rain had begun to hammer against the roof of the car. She remembered the labyrinth of backroads leading to Hazel's estate with a terrifying, instinctual clarity. She recalled the rusted, broken gate, the skeletal trees, and the oppressive, unnatural silence that swallowed the abandoned property.
When she finally killed the headlights outside the rotting house, there was absolutely no light in any of the shattered windows.
Jennifer stepped out into the freezing downpour and popped the trunk. Rose had not moved an inch.
She stood in the overgrown yard, staring at the black facade of the house.
"Hazel!" she screamed over the sound of the rain.
Nothing answered but the wind.
She called again, her voice cracking with rising panic. "Hazel!"
Still nothing.
She looked toward the heavy oak door. It was standing open by a few inches, a black void waiting to be fed. A cold, creeping sickness crawled up her spine.
Panting with exertion, she dragged Rose’s limp body out of the trunk and hauled her up the stone steps, laying her gently beneath the broken, rotting shelter of the porch. Jennifer backed away instantly. She wanted to knock, to demand an acknowledgement of the transaction, but some primal, screaming instinct demanded she flee immediately.
She retreated to the car, her clothes soaked through to the skin. As she jammed the key into the ignition, her eyes darted to the rearview mirror.
The front door was now completely open. Hazel stood in the threshold. The ancient woman was smiling—a grotesque, skin-stretching grimace that belonged in hell.
Jennifer froze, the key turning halfway.
